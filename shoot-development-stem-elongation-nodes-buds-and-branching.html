1. The developmental problem: a repeated but flexible body

A seedling may begin with a very small shoot apex, yet the mature plant may contain hundreds of leaves and many independent branches. The increase in size is only one part of this transformation. Each newly produced leaf occupies a particular position on a stem, each stem segment has an internal organization, and some of the junctions between leaves and stems contain a new centre capable of producing an entire lateral shoot. A satisfactory explanation of shoot development must therefore account for the repetition of a structural unit and for the selective use of the developmental possibilities created by that repetition. The mature shape of a plant is not a simple enlargement of its embryo.

Consider two young plants that have produced the same number of leaves. One has a compact axis, and the other has a long slender axis. They have performed approximately the same number of leaf initiation events, but they need not have achieved the same amount of longitudinal growth. Now consider two plants of equal height with similar leaf counts. The first has a single conspicuous main stem, whereas the second has several vigorous side branches. Again, similar initial organ production has resulted in quite different bodies. Developmental analysis must separate the production of potential sites for growth from the actual growth that occurs at those sites.

A shoot comprises an axis and the organs associated with that axis. The stem is the axial portion, whereas a leaf is a lateral organ. A node is a region of the stem at which one or more leaves are attached; an internode is the portion of the axis between successive nodes. An axil is the angle on the upper, or adaxial, side of a leaf base where the leaf meets the supporting stem. The axil can contain an axillary meristem, which may become enclosed within a bud. The meristem is the organized population of cells with the capacity to produce new shoot tissues. The bud is a larger structural entity that includes the meristem and may include small leaf primordia or protective structures.

A convenient descriptive unit of shoot construction is the phytomer. In a simple representation it consists of a node with its associated leaf and axillary growth site, together with an adjoining internode. Authors may choose the internode above or below a node as part of the unit, provided the convention is consistent. A phytomer is not an indivisible embryonic segment comparable in every respect to an animal somite. Its components arise through spatially connected but partially separable developmental processes. One can therefore recognize a normal node even when the associated axillary meristem fails to develop.

The shoot apical meristem, or SAM, continuously supplies cells to the growing shoot during vegetative development. The previously established SAM organization explains how a population of undifferentiated cells can persist while descendants are recruited into organs. The present problem begins at the point where descendants are recruited. Some will participate in constructing the main axis; others will contribute to leaves; and a small population in suitable positions can retain or reacquire the organization needed for a new meristem. These developmental outcomes allow an initially narrow apex to create a complex branching body.

Primary shoot growth means the construction and extension of the primary body. It should be distinguished from secondary growth, which increases the girth of an already established axis through the activity of lateral meristems in plants that possess them. A newly formed internode may increase in both length and diameter while still undergoing primary development. An increase in diameter alone is therefore not sufficient to identify secondary growth. One must establish the source and developmental context of the newly formed tissue.

2. From the shoot apex to the mature stem

The apex is not simply a cap of rapidly dividing cells. It is an organized structure whose regions contribute differently to the developing shoot. The central domain retains a population capable of renewing the apex. The flanks produce lateral organ primordia. Beneath the central region, cells contribute to internal tissues of the axis. The rib region is especially relevant to the establishment of the central stem body. These domains provide a useful functional description of the source of new tissues, although the exact boundaries and contributions vary with developmental stage and species.

A small developing shoot can be studied in longitudinal section. At its extreme tip, the outlines of leaf primordia appear close to the apex. Older primordia occur farther from the tip because new material has been produced above them and intervening stem tissue has grown. This positional succession supplies a useful developmental series: one section may contain several different ages of organs. The regions are not necessarily equivalent to fixed distances from the tip. If a mutation changes growth rates, a primordium located one millimetre from the apex in the mutant may differ in age from a primordium at the same distance in the wild type.

The cell walls of plant cells strongly constrain cell rearrangement. A daughter cell generally remains next to its former sister after cytokinesis, unlike cells in many animal tissues that can migrate over considerable distances. Patterning of the shoot must consequently be accomplished mainly by controlling the position and orientation of cell divisions and the relative rates and directions of expansion. This constraint explains why division planes can be important even when the total number of divisions is unchanged. Divisions that add cells along an axial file have a different geometric consequence from divisions that add files across the diameter.

The terms anticlinal and periclinal describe division orientation relative to the local tissue surface. An anticlinal division forms a new wall approximately perpendicular to the surface and can increase the number of cells within a surface layer. A periclinal division forms a wall approximately parallel to the surface and can generate additional layers. Neither term automatically specifies the axial length change of the entire stem because the local geometry may be curved. A longitudinal section and a transverse section provide complementary views of the same developmental process.

During early organ production, the apex creates the primary arrangements of nodes and internal tissues. Subsequent expansion separates many of the initially crowded nodes. New stem tissue is therefore not produced exclusively at a geometrical point at the very tip. Regions displaced from the extreme apex can remain proliferative or expansive. The developmental distance over which this occurs depends on the kind of shoot. A young eudicot internode can contain diffusely distributed dividing cells, whereas certain grass internodes maintain a concentrated proliferative region near the base.

The axial pattern is also coordinated with the differentiation of tissues. If the outer surface expanded substantially more slowly than the interior, mechanical stress would build between the tissues; if the interior expanded without establishing continuous conducting strands, transport to young organs would be compromised. Actual development couples growth with wall remodeling, vascular differentiation and the maintenance of tissue continuity. The coordinated behavior of a multicellular organ is not equivalent to summing the behavior of isolated cells growing independently in culture.

3. Nodes, internodes and the three primary tissue systems

The node is a recognizable point of organization because lateral organs are attached there. A leaf trace is a vascular connection between the vascular system of the stem and that of the leaf. The nodal region may therefore have a complex internal geometry in which conducting strands change direction or connect to organs. When an axillary bud becomes a growing lateral shoot, its supply and export routes also need to become functionally continuous with those of the supporting axis. A section taken through a node can look quite different from a section through the middle of an internode.

The internode is not merely an empty spacer. It contains the dermal, ground and vascular tissue systems that collectively support an elongated axis. The surface must protect growing tissues and permit appropriate exchange with the environment. The interior must transport water, mineral nutrients and assimilates while maintaining mechanical integrity. As the internode extends, these functions need to remain connected across increasing distances. The final length of an internode is consequently limited not only by developmental programs for elongation but also by the supply of materials and the ability of the tissues to accommodate deformation.

The protoderm is a primary meristematic tissue that contributes to the epidermis. Young epidermal cells remain connected in an external sheet. Their walls and cuticle provide a boundary between the living interior and the surroundings. In a growing internode, this sheet must accommodate increasing surface area through cell division and expansion. Some epidermal cells differentiate into specialized structures, including stomata or hairs in appropriate species and regions. The exact complement of epidermal cell types is not identical across stems; developmental identity should not be inferred solely from the presence of one surface structure.

Ground meristem gives rise to much of the ground tissue of the primary axis. In a typical young eudicot stem the ground tissue can be described in terms of a peripheral cortex and a central pith. Parenchyma cells often retain living protoplasts and can participate in storage or metabolism. Collenchyma provides flexible support in many growing organs through unevenly thickened primary walls. Cells that develop strongly thickened secondary walls, such as some sclerenchyma cells, provide more rigid reinforcement. Not all young stems contain all these cell types in the same arrangement. Their differentiation is related to the mechanical demands and developmental age of the organ.

Procambium establishes the primary vascular strands. Primary xylem and phloem differentiate in spatial relationship to one another and to the developing organs. Xylem provides routes for water and dissolved mineral transport and contributes to support. Phloem carries assimilates between sources and sinks. In a young stem that is still increasing in length, some early xylem conducting cells possess wall reinforcement compatible with elongation, whereas later elements may develop more extensive patterns of secondary wall deposition. The developmental distinction between protoxylem and metaxylem is thus connected to when conducting cells differentiate relative to surrounding growth.

A typical herbaceous eudicot stem has vascular bundles arranged in a ring, with xylem toward the centre and phloem toward the outside of each collateral bundle. In many monocot stems, such as grasses, bundles are distributed through the ground tissue rather than arranged in a single conspicuous ring. This is a comparison of common anatomical patterns, not a universal diagnostic rule for every angiosperm. The essential developmental principle is that procambial differentiation establishes connected vascular paths while the ground and dermal systems acquire their own characteristic organization.

Stem anatomy should be distinguished from root anatomy. A transverse section of a primary root frequently shows a central vascular cylinder in which xylem and phloem occupy alternating radial positions, whereas stem vascular strands commonly contain xylem and phloem side by side within a bundle. The next chapters investigate root meristem organization and root tissue differentiation in detail. For present purposes the distinction prevents a student from assuming that every axis has the same internal pattern simply because both roots and shoots elongate.

4. How cells lengthen an internode

A plant cell increases its volume when water enters and the surrounding wall yields sufficiently to accommodate expansion. Turgor pressure is the hydrostatic pressure of the cell contents against the wall. It creates a mechanical driving force, but the existence of turgor does not establish that the cell is growing. A mature cell can remain turgid for long periods while its dimensions change very little because its wall does not yield irreversibly at the existing pressure. Growth requires a permissive wall as well as an adequate supply of water.

The primary wall consists of a hydrated network in which cellulose microfibrils provide strong reinforcement and matrix polymers contribute to its mechanical behavior. In an elongating cell the direction of reinforcement can influence the direction in which the wall yields most readily. In a simplified example, circumferential reinforcement limits radial expansion and permits preferential longitudinal extension. This is a useful geometric model rather than an assertion that all growing cells have a perfectly transverse and uniform cellulose arrangement. Newly deposited wall material, changing microfibril orientations and constraints from neighboring cells complicate actual tissue mechanics.

The vacuole plays an important role in enlargement by allowing cells to increase volume without synthesizing a proportional volume of dense cytoplasm. Solute accumulation lowers the osmotic component of water potential, permitting water uptake when an appropriate gradient exists. Membrane transport, metabolism and wall modification all require resources. An increase in fresh mass may therefore primarily reflect water uptake rather than a comparable increase in dry organic matter. In a growth experiment, fresh mass, dry mass, cell length and whole-organ length answer different questions.

A simple wall-yield model expresses relative volumetric growth rate as the product of wall extensibility and the excess of turgor over a yield threshold. When pressure is below the threshold the model predicts no irreversible expansion; above it, increasing pressure or extensibility can increase growth. This idealization assumes that hydraulic supply and wall properties are adequately described by constant parameters over the measurement interval. It does not directly predict axial elongation unless the relationship between changes in volume and changes in length is known. A cylindrical cell that expands mainly in length behaves differently from a cell expanding in three dimensions.

Cell production and expansion are also temporally coupled. A proliferating cell must attain sufficient size and biosynthetic capacity to complete the cell cycle. Its daughters inherit walls and cytoplasmic components and can subsequently expand. Later, many descendants cease division while continuing to enlarge. The number of cells in a mature axial file thus reflects the history of cell production, and their mean length reflects a combination of earlier division, expansion and differentiation. A final measurement cannot uniquely reconstruct these histories without observations at earlier stages.

Gibberellins are plant growth regulators that often promote internode elongation. Their developmental effect can involve changes in cell proliferation, cell expansion or both. A plant that is deficient in active gibberellin may be short because its internodes elongate less, even though it produces recognizable nodes. In a standard response pathway, bioactive gibberellin promotes removal of DELLA growth-repressing proteins through regulated proteolysis. A mutation that prevents hormone synthesis and one that prevents normal removal of a growth repressor can therefore produce similar short phenotypes yet differ in their response to externally supplied hormone. Detailed hormone metabolism belongs to plant physiology; here the important distinction is between production of organ sites and extension of the axis.

Mechanical constraints can modify the expression of growth potential. If an inner tissue is capable of faster expansion than a tightly constrained outer layer, the final tissue deformation depends on both. Removing a constraint can cause immediate elastic changes as well as later biological growth; these should not be confused. A short-term displacement after cutting a tissue is not, by itself, evidence that new cell wall material has been synthesized. Time-resolved measurements distinguish reversible deformation from persistent growth.

5. Why length and developmental rate must be measured separately

One can represent the length of a simple stem segment as the sum of the lengths of cells along an axial file. If the file contains N cells with average axial length l, its length is approximately N times l. This relation is useful for separating possible contributions of proliferation and expansion, but the approximation depends on an appropriate sampling geometry. A curved cell file, overlapping cells or a measurement taken obliquely can cause errors. It is also necessary to compare the same developmental region rather than unrelated parts of two plants.

Suppose an internode has a mature representative file of 100 cells with mean axial length 150 micrometres. The estimated file length is 15 millimetres. Another internode has 80 cells with mean length 180 micrometres, giving 14.4 millimetres. The second internode contains fewer cells even though its cells are longer. The similar organ lengths conceal two distinct cellular outcomes. A student should therefore resist interpreting a nearly normal final organ length as evidence that all of its developmental processes are normal.

The relative rate of elongation can be more informative than the absolute rate when initial lengths differ. If a segment increases from 5 to 10 millimetres in a day, its absolute increase is 5 millimetres. A segment increasing from 20 to 25 millimetres has the same absolute increment but a smaller proportional change. Under an exponential-growth approximation the mean relative elongation rate is the logarithm of the final-to-initial length ratio divided by elapsed time. This calculation does not imply that the organ actually follows exponential growth throughout its life; it summarizes proportional change over a defined interval.

A growing stem is also a record of organ production through time. The interval between successive leaf initiation events is called a plastochron. If a plant initiates organs faster, it may produce more nodes per unit time without necessarily increasing the length of each internode. A plant with slower organ initiation could nevertheless have a longer stem if its internodes elongate sufficiently. The rates of leaf placement and internodal extension are distinct variables, even though they may respond to shared developmental and environmental signals.

In a hypothetical comparison, plant A has twelve mature internodes averaging two centimetres and plant B has eight mature internodes averaging three centimetres. Each has a total internodal length of twenty-four centimetres. Plant A has more repeated axial positions at which lateral organs and potential branches can occur. The plants may therefore differ substantially in future architecture despite equal current stem length. A branching study that reports only height and branch number cannot determine whether the difference reflects branch activation probability, internode growth or the number of available axils.

Developmental timing is particularly important when interpreting a dwarf mutant. A plant examined at the same chronological age as the wild type may be at an earlier developmental stage. Its fewer leaves may reflect slower organ production, not failure to specify an organ at a particular node. Conversely, a dwarf with normal node count but shorter internodes offers more direct evidence of reduced axial elongation. Ideally, measurements include both time since germination and a staging criterion such as the number of initiated leaves or the age of a particular internode.

6. A lateral shoot begins in a leaf axil

Branch formation begins before an obvious side shoot appears. A young leaf forms at the apex and establishes a boundary with the stem. Near its adaxial base, a group of cells occupies a position in which a new axillary meristem can develop. This process is distinct from the formation of the leaf itself. A leaf can develop even if a normal axillary meristem fails to form, and a meristem can exist without producing a long lateral shoot. The relationship between leaf production and branch formation is therefore close but not obligatory at every subsequent step.

The new axillary meristem must organize a renewing cell population and the spatial relationships characteristic of a shoot apex. Once formed, it can initiate its own leaves and a new stem. Its descendants can eventually produce additional axillary meristems in the new axis. Branching thus creates a hierarchy: a primary shoot gives rise to a secondary shoot, which may generate tertiary shoots. These terms refer to the relative origin of axes, not to their absolute sizes. A secondary axis can become longer than the original axis if its growth conditions and developmental state favor it.

The distinction between specification of an axillary meristem and activation of a bud is central to developmental genetics. A defect in meristem specification reduces the number of sites from which a normal branch can arise. A defect in bud activation acts at a later stage: the potential branch site exists, but the bud does not enter sustained outgrowth. Mature plants with both kinds of defect can look similar from a distance. Inspection of the leaf axils, early developmental markers and responses to release from inhibition are required to separate the causes.

Evidence from Arabidopsis supports the retention of meristematic competence in cells within the developing leaf axil. Expression of SHOOT MERISTEMLESS, a KNOX-family transcription factor associated with shoot meristem identity, helps identify this developmental population. In this model, cells with appropriate competence remain at the boundary as neighboring leaf tissues pursue differentiation. The persistence of a molecular marker is not alone proof that every marked cell becomes a functional stem cell; lineage and perturbation experiments are needed to establish the relation between marker expression and future meristem formation.

Local signaling at the leaf axil differs from the signaling conditions that position a new leaf primordium. In Arabidopsis, a local reduction in auxin signaling is associated with the establishment of the axillary meristematic population, followed by a cytokinin response associated with niche formation. This context dependence matters. It is incorrect to reason that because auxin accumulation helps initiate a leaf primordium, increasing auxin everywhere on the flank of a shoot apex must also promote axillary meristem specification. The same hormone can have different developmental consequences depending on position, competence and timing.

Genes such as LATERAL SUPPRESSOR and REGULATOR OF AXILLARY MERISTEMS contribute to proper axillary meristem formation in model plants. Their mutant phenotypes help establish that normal leaf axils require specific developmental regulation rather than automatically becoming lateral shoots. The exact extent of a phenotype depends on genotype, developmental phase and the node examined. An examination question may describe failure of meristems in only a subset of axils; the correct reasoning is to recognize a defect in formation at those sites, not to assume that a gene has no role merely because some axils remain normal.

7. Establishing a new stem-cell niche

In the developing axil, a group of competent cells must undergo a change in organization before it can function as an autonomous shoot apex. Competence describes the ability to respond to a developmental signal, not the guarantee that an organ will form. An axil may contain a population that retains meristem-associated gene expression for some time before a recognizable dome emerges. At this stage it is misleading to describe the axil as containing a miniature branch with all the organs already specified. The establishment of the new axis and the production of its organs occur progressively.

The boundary between a leaf primordium and the primary axis is developmentally important because the two adjacent tissues are pursuing different patterns of growth. A leaf primordium expands outward, while the stem must maintain axial continuity. Cells in the boundary can differ from their neighbors in rates of division, expansion and expression of regulatory genes. The relatively restrained expansion of a boundary can maintain separation between neighboring structures. A meristem located within the boundary must ultimately initiate a new local pattern of growth without abolishing the distinction between the pre-existing leaf and stem.

To understand the evidence for this process, imagine observing the same developing axil at successive ages with a fluorescent reporter for SHOOT MERISTEMLESS expression. At an early stage a small population shows reporter activity. As the leaf grows and its tissues become distinguishable, activity persists in a restricted population near the boundary. Later a subset of the axil develops a recognizable meristem. Such a sequence supports the idea that meristematic competence has been maintained during organ differentiation. An alternative explanation would be that competent cells were lost and meristem identity was independently induced again. Distinguishing these alternatives requires tracking individual cells rather than simply comparing pictures from separate specimens.

Live imaging is valuable because it establishes the continuity of a cellular population. However, the fluorescence reporter has limitations: the time required to produce and degrade its protein can differ from the timing of endogenous transcription. A cell can remain fluorescent after its transcription rate changes. Imaging and marker expression therefore need to be interpreted together with independent indicators of cell behavior. If a regulatory gene is removed and the competent population disappears before niche formation, the combination of timing and genetic perturbation is much stronger evidence than a single photograph of a mature mutant.

A local auxin minimum can be created by the redistribution of auxin away from a population of cells. This does not require that the entire shoot be auxin deficient. A small domain at the leaf axil and a neighboring leaf primordium can experience distinct hormone environments because transporters are distributed asymmetrically and local cells differ in their capacity to respond. It is the spatial contrast, rather than a globally averaged concentration, that helps explain why one region can retain meristematic properties while another region begins the development of a determinate lateral organ.

Cytokinin signaling can then participate in establishing the functional niche. This does not mean that an isolated cell exposed to cytokinin necessarily becomes a complete axillary meristem. The appropriate cell population, positional environment and regulatory identity must be present. One can test this distinction with a developmental intervention that increases cytokinin response in an axil that has lost its meristematic competence. A failure to restore a normal meristem could indicate that the earlier developmental defect cannot be bypassed by the later signal alone. A successful rescue would instead suggest that some responsive cells remain or can regain the appropriate identity. Neither conclusion can be made without knowing the spatial and temporal specificity of the intervention.

Regulatory genes that maintain a population of competent cells and genes that allow the later population to organize a niche need not act at precisely the same time. For example, a perturbation that reduces STM expression from the beginning could produce a different defect from one introduced after the axillary dome has appeared. The first may prevent formation, while the second may impair maintenance of an already established meristem. The general principle is important for interpreting developmental genes: observing a missing mature structure does not identify the earliest causal stage. The stage of gene action must be inferred from observations made while the structure is developing.

Once a functional axillary meristem is established, its organizing functions are conceptually related to those of the primary SAM. It must maintain a sufficiently stable stem-cell population while generating cells for lateral organs and internal tissues. However, the size, position and timing of the two meristems may differ substantially. The lateral meristem develops in the context of an existing parent shoot and its environmental and physiological conditions, whereas the primary SAM was established during embryogenesis. They are related developmental systems, not anatomically identical structures in every respect.

8. The axillary bud and the decision to grow

A bud is not equivalent to an actively extending branch. It often contains an organized meristem and several small organ primordia, but these structures may remain compact for a long interval. During this time the meristem can be alive, and limited development or metabolism may continue. Growth inhibition should therefore not be equated with death or complete physiological inactivity. A viable bud has the potential to begin or resume a more extensive developmental program if conditions change.

It is helpful to distinguish bud formation, bud activation and sustained branch elongation as successive operational stages. Formation establishes the organized lateral growth site. Activation refers to the early transition toward increased developmental activity, which can be detected by growth measurements, changes in metabolism or appropriate molecular markers. Sustained outgrowth entails continued production and expansion of branch tissues. These stages overlap in time, and no single universally applicable molecular threshold separates them. Nevertheless, they identify different classes of developmental problems.

For example, a bud may swell after the removal of the main apex and then cease extending. Swelling is evidence that something has changed in the bud, but it does not establish that a self-maintaining branch has formed. The growth could be a transient response to water uptake or mobilization of reserves. A more informative experiment records bud length at several intervals, the appearance of new leaves and the persistence of the growth response. Establishment of a lateral shoot requires not only activation but also continuing access to nutrients and a functioning relationship with the main axis.

The term dormancy covers several biologically distinct conditions. In a growing herbaceous plant, inhibition of a viable bud by other actively growing parts of the same plant is often described as paradormancy. In some perennial plants, a bud can become internally dormant and require particular seasonal conditions before it becomes capable of renewed development. Growth may also be prevented by unfavorable external conditions despite the absence of a strong intrinsic block. The latter situations are commonly distinguished as endodormancy and ecodormancy, respectively. These categories are useful conceptual distinctions, but they should not be treated as identical processes simply because none produces visible branch elongation at the time of observation.

An axillary bud normally receives signals and resources from several tissues. Its connection to the parent axis determines access to water and assimilates, while nearby leaves and growing apices can influence the allocation of those resources. The ability of the bud to develop its own export routes and later its own photosynthetic surfaces changes these relationships over time. The developing branch is not an independent organism isolated from the supporting plant; it is a new developmental axis embedded within an integrated physiological system.

A useful examination distinction follows from the fact that not every available bud becomes a visible branch. Suppose a plant forms twenty axillary buds but only four produce sustained branches. Another plant forms ten buds and four branches. The raw branch count is the same, but the proportion of available buds that grew is twenty percent in the first plant and forty percent in the second. The denominator is essential. If the question concerns bud activation, the relevant denominator should be viable buds rather than simply all nodes, especially when comparing mutants that differ in axillary meristem formation.

9. Apical dominance and the logic of classical experiments

The characteristic architecture of many young plants includes a vigorous main axis and a smaller number of lateral branches. In such plants, removal of the growing apex often permits one or more axillary buds below the cut to grow. This phenomenon is the basis of the classical concept of apical dominance. The apex is dominant in the sense that its presence contributes to suppression of growth at certain lateral sites. It need not completely inhibit every bud, and the strength of suppression differs among species, genotypes and environmental conditions.

Classical experiments separated the effects of removing a shoot apex from those of replacing a chemical signal associated with the apex. In the standard design, an intact plant is compared with a decapitated plant and with a decapitated plant to which auxin is supplied at the cut surface. If lateral buds grow after removal of the tip but are inhibited when auxin is supplied to the stump, auxin can restore part of the inhibitory influence under those conditions. Historically this type of experimental reasoning, associated particularly with work by Thimann and Skoog, established a connection between auxin and apical dominance. It did not establish that the natural inhibitory action of the apex consists only of auxin diffusing directly into every dormant bud.

A rigorous design includes a decapitated plant with the same application vehicle but no added auxin. A paste or solvent could affect the wound environment, and the cut itself could change water relations and initiate wound responses. The plants should be matched for developmental stage, bud position and growth conditions. Because buds at different nodes can behave differently, comparing the uppermost bud of one plant with a basal bud of another is not a clean test. Measurements need to be made at equivalent positions in independently replicated plants.

The auxin replacement experiment provides evidence of sufficiency in a limited experimental sense: supplied auxin can suppress outgrowth in a decapitated context. It does not establish that all other effects of decapitation have been restored or that auxin is the only necessary component of the intact apex's influence. For example, a growing apex consumes assimilates, whereas a paste containing auxin does not reproduce the same metabolic demand. This difference becomes especially important when examining the earliest events that occur after removal of the tip.

Apical dominance should be distinguished from apical control in the broader study of plant architecture. The latter expression is often used for the influence of a dominant axis on the relative growth or orientation of branches that are already developing, particularly in woody plants. The distinction is useful because branch initiation, early activation and the later hierarchy of existing branches are not interchangeable events. An intervention that allows suppressed buds to activate may not produce a final plant in which every lateral axis becomes equally long.

Removal of the primary tip can also change which remaining shoot becomes dominant. If one lateral branch grows rapidly and develops its own active apex, it can influence other growth sites. The result may be a new leader rather than a permanently leaderless plant with equivalent branches. This observation reveals the dynamic nature of branching regulation: the primary axis is not uniquely privileged because of an immutable embryonic label. Relative positions, ongoing growth and physiological connections can allow a lateral axis to assume a dominant role.

A strong conceptual test of apical dominance involves contrasting a branching-deficient plant whose axillary meristems are absent with one whose buds are merely suppressed. Removing the tip can release an existing competent bud but cannot instantly replace a missing meristem. If a branch later appears at an initially empty site, the experiment must consider delayed meristem initiation or adventitious regeneration, not simply activation of an invisible dormant bud. Early anatomical observations make the later phenotype interpretable.

10. Auxin and the indirect regulation of bud outgrowth

Auxin is produced in growing shoot tissues including the apex and young leaves. Directional transport through the stem creates a basipetal auxin transport stream, meaning that the net movement in this context is toward the base of the shoot. This does not imply that auxin is transported identically in every tissue of the plant. In particular, the direction of transport through a mature stem and the local transport patterns within a developing leaf axil are distinct spatial situations.

In many classical branching models, the main stem's auxin transport stream influences an axillary bud indirectly. The auxin supplied by the primary apex need not enter the bud at a concentration sufficient to act as a direct local inhibitor. An indirect explanation is necessary because the developmental relationship between the bud and the parent axis depends on the transport and signaling properties of the connecting tissue. In a simplified model, changes in stem auxin status alter the production or availability of other signals that do act within or near the bud.

Cytokinins often promote bud outgrowth. Changes in auxin status of the main axis can modify cytokinin production, transport or availability in the nodal environment. Cytokinins may originate from roots or from shoot tissues, and their effects depend on the plant's developmental and nutritional context. It is therefore insufficient to memorize that roots make cytokinin and shoots make auxin as if the hormones had exclusive sources. The meaningful developmental inference is that the influence of a growing apex can be transmitted through changes in the signaling environment of lateral growth sites.

A second explanation concerns the ability of the lateral bud to establish an auxin export pathway into the parent stem. An actively growing shoot produces auxin and normally exports it toward its base. If an existing strong transport stream makes it difficult for a lateral bud to establish an effective export route, its sustained outgrowth may be inhibited. The canalization model links directional transport to a feedback process in which auxin flow and the organization of transport capacity reinforce one another. This provides a possible mechanistic basis for competition between different growing apices without requiring the apical auxin itself to act as a direct inhibitor inside the bud.

The second-messenger and canalization explanations emphasize different aspects of an integrated process. The first stresses regulation of local growth-promoting or growth-inhibiting signals by the main axis. The second stresses the establishment and competition of transport pathways. A result in which manipulation of stem auxin changes lateral outgrowth may be compatible with both. To distinguish the contributions, one would need measurements of signaling responses, transport capacity and developmental timing rather than relying only on branch counts.

Directional movement is especially relevant in interpreting inhibitor experiments. If an inhibitor of polar auxin transport is applied to the main stem, its effects will depend on where it is placed relative to the apex and the bud. Interfering with the main axis above a bud is not equivalent to interfering with the developing bud's own export route. Transport inhibitors can also affect other auxin-dependent processes, including vascular differentiation and organ positioning. A whole-plant treatment that produces fewer branches is therefore not decisive evidence for a single simple direction of auxin action.

The same distinction helps explain why auxin can be associated with both growth promotion and branching suppression. Within an elongating tissue, auxin can participate in the regulation of wall properties and cellular expansion. Within the integrated shoot, a strong apical auxin stream can contribute indirectly to maintaining lateral buds in an inhibited state. These effects operate at different levels of organization. A molecule has no universal label as either a promoter or inhibitor of the whole plant's growth; its developmental effect must be specified with respect to the process, tissue, concentration and time being considered.

11. Strigolactones and the integration of branch-inhibitory signals

Strigolactones are a class of carotenoid-derived signaling compounds with important roles in plant development. One of their major shoot-related effects in many species is suppression of excessive branching. Genetic studies in several model plants revealed unusually branched mutants in which production of, or response to, these signals is impaired. In Arabidopsis, mutations in members of the MORE AXILLARY GROWTH pathway can produce such phenotypes. In rice, related developmental regulation influences the number of lateral shoots called tillers.

A hormone pathway can be disrupted at several different steps. A biosynthetic mutant may fail to produce enough active signal while retaining the capacity to respond to it. A perception mutant may produce the compound but fail to recognize or transmit its presence. Both classes may have similar high-branching phenotypes in untreated plants. A suitable treatment with a biologically active signal or analogue can sometimes distinguish them: a biosynthetic deficiency may be rescued, whereas a fully insensitive genotype should not show the corresponding specific rescue. Differences in delivery, compound stability and partial alleles must be considered when actual experimental responses are interpreted.

At an introductory mechanistic level, the strigolactone receptor D14 participates in signaling that involves the MAX2/D3 family of F-box proteins. Regulated degradation of downstream repressors in the SMXL/D53 family is a key part of the signaling logic. Arabidopsis and rice differ in the names and precise relationships of the relevant proteins, so terminology should be tied to the species. The important developmental consequence is that an intact signaling pathway can help constrain bud outgrowth, while impaired signaling can release some of this restraint. Detailed biosynthetic reactions and receptor chemistry are reserved for plant physiology.

The pathway does not operate independently of auxin and cytokinin. Changes in stem auxin status can influence the production of branch-regulating signals, and strigolactone responses can affect properties of auxin transport and the state of the bud. A model in which each hormone has an isolated, additive effect is useful only as a first approximation. Real developmental regulation may involve interactions: a cytokinin response might have a different effect when strigolactone signaling is strongly active than when it is impaired.

The Arabidopsis transcription factor BRANCHED1, or BRC1, is a member of the TCP family and has been studied as a local integrator of signals affecting branching. Higher BRC1 activity is commonly associated with inhibition of axillary bud outgrowth in the contexts in which it functions. Reduced BRC1 function can allow greater branching. Its relationship to the related maize gene teosinte branched1 illustrates how regulatory genes can have conserved roles in shoot architecture while their exact expression patterns and phenotypic effects differ among species.

An association between BRC1 expression and bud growth does not establish that BRC1 is the sole switch controlling every axillary bud. Buds integrate several constraints, including assimilate availability, developmental state and transport relationships. A bud with reduced BRC1 function may still fail to develop if it lacks sufficient resources or a competent meristem. Similarly, high BRC1 expression in a suppressed bud might be part of the causal mechanism, a response to another signal or both. A gene's expression pattern should be interpreted together with loss-of-function, rescue and other functional evidence.

A useful genetic thought experiment combines a mutation reducing axillary meristem formation with a mutation reducing branch suppression. If the first mutation eliminates the competent lateral growth sites completely, the second cannot generate ordinary branches from those missing sites merely by releasing a downstream inhibitory pathway. Where meristems remain, the second mutation may increase the probability of outgrowth. This conditional prediction is more biologically meaningful than asserting that one mutant phenotype will always dominate another regardless of developmental context.
