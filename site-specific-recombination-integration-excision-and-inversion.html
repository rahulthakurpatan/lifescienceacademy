1. DNA rearrangement as a biological solution

DNA is usually introduced as a stable store of information. A cell must copy that information and correct damage without changing the intended sequence. Yet there are situations in which a precise alteration in the arrangement of DNA is advantageous. A bacterial virus can gain a stable place in the host chromosome. A circular chromosome that has become a dimer can be separated into two individual circles. A bacterium can change which surface protein is expressed by reversing a short regulatory segment. In each case, the useful event is not the production of another copy of the whole genome. It is a controlled change in the connections between existing DNA segments. Site-specific recombination is one of the biological solutions to this problem.

Consider two pieces of double-stranded DNA. Each contains a short sequence that is recognized by a particular enzyme. The enzyme brings those sequences together and makes a reciprocal exchange of DNA connections at specified positions. The new connections can put a DNA segment inside a different molecule, release a segment that was previously part of a larger molecule, or change the orientation of a segment within the same molecule. These are called integration, excision and inversion, respectively. The molecular machinery does not need to read the biological meaning of the surrounding genes. It operates on recognizable DNA addresses, and the arrangement of those addresses determines what happens to the larger pieces of DNA.

The term recombination refers to a change in the combination or physical linkage of DNA sequences. It need not imply that two homologous chromosomes exchange entire chromosome arms during meiosis. The term site-specific describes an important property of the recognition process: the enzyme acts on a particular short DNA element, or on a pair of particular compatible elements, rather than locating a partner by extensive sequence similarity. The DNA around the recognition elements can differ greatly in sequence and function. This explains how a phage genome, containing genes quite different from those of its bacterial host, can still join the host chromosome reproducibly at a particular attachment site.

In this chapter, the focus is conservative site-specific recombination. In the elementary reaction, existing DNA phosphodiester bonds are exchanged without an obligatory round of DNA replication or addition and removal of nucleotides. The products contain the DNA material present in the substrates, reorganized into different connections. Conservative does not mean that the genome must remain the same size. For example, a segment that becomes a separate circle is no longer part of its original chromosome, although its nucleotides still exist in the circle immediately after the reaction. If that circle cannot replicate independently, it can disappear from the cell population later. This distinction between the immediate chemical product and its subsequent biological fate is essential.

A student can approach any site-specific recombination problem by separating three levels of reasoning. The first is chemical: which covalent bonds change, and how are new bonds formed? The second is structural: which recognition sites participate, how are they oriented, and are they on one or two DNA molecules? The third is biological: what does the resulting DNA organization do to replication, gene expression, inheritance or survival? Confusing these levels leads to incorrect conclusions. A reaction may be chemically reversible but biologically biased toward one product because the released circle is diluted. A protein may be catalytically competent but unable to recombine sites because a necessary accessory protein is absent. A DNA segment may be inverted without any change in its nucleotide composition, yet the genes inside it may no longer be expressed.

2. DNA polarity, recognition sequences and the meaning of an arrow

The starting point is the DNA double helix. Two complementary strands run antiparallel. Each strand has a chemically defined five-prime end and three-prime end. The backbone contains phosphodiester connections between sugars. Complementary bases provide a predictable pairing relationship between strands, whereas the arrangement of bases along a strand supplies sequence information. A protein can recognize a sequence by contacting the exposed chemical groups of base pairs and the shape of the DNA backbone. Consequently, a recombinase can distinguish a suitable site from much of the surrounding chromosome without needing to separate the entire duplex into free single strands.

A recombination site is a DNA segment at which strand exchange can occur. Many sites have two binding regions arranged approximately as inverted repeats, separated by a central region where the DNA strands are cleaved and exchanged. The repeated regions provide docking surfaces for protein subunits. The central sequence contributes to catalytic alignment, compatibility of the reacting partners and orientation. This is a useful general organization, but attachment sites in regulated natural systems may be more complex. For example, the lambda phage attachment region contains additional protein-binding elements beyond its short exchange region. One must not assign the exact dimensions of a simple loxP site to every recombinase family.

An arrow drawn above a site represents the orientation of that sequence relative to the chosen direction along the DNA. It does not represent a replication fork or RNA polymerase movement. It also does not identify which single DNA strand is being cut. Both strands belong to the recognition site. If an oriented site is turned end for end, its arrow changes direction, although the chemical polarity of the individual strands remains the ordinary five-prime to three-prime polarity. The reference strand read from left to right after an inversion corresponds to the reverse complement of the old sequence. Merely reversing the written order of letters without taking complements does not describe a double-stranded DNA inversion correctly.

The canonical loxP sequence illustrates the source of directional information. It has a length of 34 base pairs, with two 13-base-pair inverted repeats flanking an asymmetric eight-base-pair spacer. The outer elements allow Cre protein to bind in the appropriate arrangement. Although those two recognition arms have a symmetry relationship, the central spacer makes the complete site asymmetric. Therefore, the whole site can be assigned a direction. Two loxP sites facing the same way along a chromosome are described as direct repeats; two facing toward or away from one another are inverted repeats. These labels refer to the orientation of entire sites, not merely to the arrangement of the two short binding arms within one loxP site.

To make this distinction concrete, imagine a left flank A, a central segment M and a right flank B. If the two complete sites are aligned in the same direction, the arrangement is A, forward site, M, forward site, B. If the second site is reversed, the arrangement becomes A, forward site, M, reverse site, B. The amount and identity of DNA in the two starting molecules can be the same except for the orientation of the second site. Nevertheless, a single conservative recombination event will generate fundamentally different molecular products. This is why a diagram showing only two boxes labelled loxP, without arrows or equivalent sequence information, is insufficient to predict a unique outcome.

Two sites can also occur on different molecules. In that case, their orientation has to be interpreted together with the topology and the chosen representation of each molecule. An isolated circular DNA has no absolute left or right end. One can rotate the circular map without changing the molecule. A linear chromosome, in contrast, has distinct flanking sequences at its ends. A correct analysis begins by marking identifiable flanks and the orientation of each site, then following the connections through the reaction. Reliance on the position of an arrow on a drawing alone, without tracing its flanks, can give misleading results.

3. Why site-specific recombination differs from homologous recombination

Homologous recombination typically involves DNA molecules or regions that share substantial sequence similarity. In bacterial cells, RecA promotes homology search and strand exchange; in eukaryotic cells, Rad51 plays a related role. Such reactions help repair broken DNA, restore missing information using a homologous template and produce genetic variation through exchange between homologous chromosomes. A critical challenge in that process is locating an appropriate homologous partner across a large amount of potentially unrelated DNA. The resulting intermediates can include heteroduplex DNA and Holliday junctions.

Site-specific recombination solves a different recognition problem. The enzymes bind short designated sites and establish a synaptic complex between them. A phage attachment site and its bacterial counterpart need not be surrounded by long homologous regions. The central exchange regions must generally support proper strand pairing and catalytic compatibility, but the DNA beyond these regions can be unrelated. For that reason, absence of extensive homology is not equivalent to absence of sequence constraints. The enzyme recognizes particular sites and the reacting DNA segments must be arranged in a suitable geometry.

The distinction is especially important because a Holliday junction can occur in both homologous recombination and many site-specific recombination reactions. A Holliday junction is a four-way DNA connection, not the name of one exclusive biochemical pathway. In homologous recombination it may arise through strand invasion and subsequent rearrangement of homologous DNA molecules. In the tyrosine recombinase family it is produced after the exchange of the first pair of DNA strands between aligned target sites. The presence of such an intermediate therefore does not establish which type of recombination occurred. Information about the substrate, enzyme and sequence requirements is necessary.

Neither process should be confused with nonhomologous end joining. End joining is a mechanism for reconnecting broken DNA ends, particularly double-strand breaks. Its substrate is a discontinuity that needs repair, and nucleotides can be removed or added before the ends are joined. A conservative site-specific recombinase creates and controls its own reactive DNA ends within an assembled complex. The ordinary reaction is not an attempt to rescue a random broken chromosome. Instead, the controlled cleavage and joining are the planned molecular operations that rearrange the DNA. There may be abnormal situations in which recombinase-associated cleavage creates damage, but such failures are not the normal pathway.

Transposition is another process that can change genomic arrangement. A transposase recognizes the ends of a mobile element and moves or copies that element to a target. Depending on the transposon, a new target sequence duplication or another characteristic product may be generated through DNA repair or replication. Some transposition systems have preferred target sequences, so the word specific on its own is not a complete classification criterion. The classical conservative site-specific reaction considered here is defined by reciprocal exchange at a pair of cognate recombination sites. Certain mobile elements also encode genuine site-specific recombinases for other stages of their life cycles. A student must distinguish the chemistry and purpose of each individual step rather than assigning one label to every activity of the element.

This comparison explains why site-specific recombination is useful for deliberate genome rearrangement. If two recognizable sites are placed around a particular DNA interval, the enzyme can change that interval without conducting a search for long stretches of homologous sequence. The product remains dependent on local chromosomal accessibility and compatible sites. Thus the system is neither a universal sequence editor nor a mechanism that can automatically act at every gene. It is a molecular switch whose input is a specified pair of DNA addresses and whose output follows from the geometry of those addresses.

4. Predicting the products from DNA connectivity

The simplest way to understand the three elementary rearrangements is to trace the connections made at the two sites. Imagine that each site consists of two halves with an exchange region between them. Write the first site as L1–R1 and the second site as L2–R2, where the letters refer to sequence-defined sides of each oriented site. The unrecombined sites connect L1 with R1 and L2 with R2. After a reciprocal exchange, the connections are L1 with R2 and L2 with R1, with the strands paired appropriately. No arbitrary choice of a new linkage is involved. The enzyme acts in a geometry that brings compatible partners together and exchanges their covalent connections. In schematic problems, this change in connectivity is often enough to deduce the overall product.

First consider direct repeats on a linear chromosome. The left flank A runs into the first site, the intervening segment M runs from that site to the second, and the right flank B extends from the second site. Because the sites face in the same direction, the two outer flanks can become connected through one recombinant site. The two ends of M become connected through the other recombinant site. Consequently, the product containing A and B remains linear, while M is released in a circular molecule. Both original sites contribute to the products; they have not simply vanished. With two identical sites such as wild-type loxP, each recombinant molecule carries one recognizable loxP site. With nonidentical compatible sites, the two recombinant sites may instead have distinct hybrid compositions.

It is helpful to draw the same event using a loop. The enzyme binds both sites and the intervening DNA forms a loop because it connects them along the same molecule. Once the two sites are synapsed, the change in backbone connections closes the loop as a separate DNA circle. It simultaneously restores continuity between the external flanks. This explanation makes sense of the term excision: the central segment is released from its previous context. Deletion, in genetic descriptions, refers to the loss of that segment from the chromosome being followed. The reaction itself has not converted the segment into free nucleotides. When the excised molecule lacks an origin of replication, it usually cannot be maintained as an independently inherited genetic element. The chromosome then carries a lasting deletion even though the molecular chemistry was conservative.

If the substrate carrying direct repeats is circular rather than linear, the connectivity rule still applies, but the topology of the products changes. The two arcs of the original circle become two smaller circles, each containing one recombinant site. It is inappropriate to call one of the circles the chromosome product merely because it is drawn larger. In a plasmid problem, the circle carrying the functional replication origin may be the only molecule maintained during later growth. If both circles contain suitable origins and selectable features, both can persist under appropriate conditions. If they do not have origins that function in the cell being examined, neither may survive for many cell divisions. Questions about the sizes of products and questions about which product is inherited thus require different information.

Now reverse the orientation of the second site on a linear DNA molecule. The corresponding sides of the two recognition elements are now encountered in opposite orders along the map. Upon reciprocal exchange, the connections at the two ends of M are replaced with connections that reverse the order of the internal DNA relative to the external flanks. The product remains one continuous linear molecule. The two sites still lie on opposite orientations, but the central interval has become its reverse complement relative to the reference direction. The result is inversion, not deletion. The length of the DNA is retained in the ideal conservative reaction.

The same principle applies when the substrate with inverted sites is circular. There is one circular product, with the intervening arc reversed relative to the rest of the circle. Because a circular map has no inherent starting point, it is necessary to specify an external reference gene or sequence to describe which arc has inverted. Drawings can be rotated or reflected as representations without altering the biological DNA. A fixed external landmark eliminates that ambiguity. In exam diagrams, the presence of genes labelled A and B outside the interval is often intended to supply precisely this reference.

One practical implication of inversion is reversibility. After an inversion between two ordinary identical sites, the substrate still has two sites capable of recombining in opposite directions. A second event can restore the original orientation. Thus persistent activity of the recombinase can produce a population containing both orientations. The final population need not be evenly split. Differences in how easily the DNA sites synapse, in the fitness of the cells carrying the two arrangements, and in regulatory proteins that favor one reaction can all change the observed distribution. A statement that inversion is reversible does not establish that both directions have equal rates under all conditions.

Integration can be understood as the connectivity reverse of excision. Imagine a circular donor molecule carrying one recombination site and a linear recipient molecule carrying a compatible site. Synapsis brings the donor site to the recipient site. Reciprocal strand exchange opens the donor circle at the recombination region and inserts its continuous DNA into the linear recipient. The product contains two recombinant sites, one at each boundary of the inserted DNA. Both flanks of the recipient are still present. The circle is no longer a separate physical molecule. It has become part of the linear DNA. The same connectivity operation, applied to two separate circular molecules, produces a larger circle containing the DNA of both substrates, often called a cointegrate.

This example also reveals why integration and excision cannot be predicted from site orientation alone. The direct-repeat rule is a shortcut for two sites on the same molecule. If each of two distinct molecules carries one site, an intermolecular event can connect the two molecules. If they are both linear, reciprocal exchange can produce two linear molecules carrying exchanged flanks rather than one larger continuous molecule. For example, two separate DNA molecules A–site–B and C–site–D can become A–recombinant site–D and C–recombinant site–B when aligned compatibly. The total number of linear molecules remains two. At the chromosome scale, such an exchange can amount to a translocation. The outcome is therefore determined jointly by site compatibility, site orientation and substrate connectivity.

The basic diagram must also be distinguished from the topological details of a real DNA reaction. Two sites may meet in a synaptic geometry that traps DNA crossings. The resulting circles may be intertwined, and an inversion can change the pattern of knots or supercoiling. The simple connectivity map predicts the sequence organization, not necessarily whether two product circles are physically unlinked. A topoisomerase may be needed to remove some topological links. In a gel experiment, circular molecules of equal sequence length can migrate differently depending on whether they are nicked, relaxed, supercoiled or linked to another circle. A sequence-based prediction and an electrophoretic prediction are not identical.

5. DNA conservation, length calculations and the fate of excised DNA

Quantitative reasoning about a conservative rearrangement starts with a simple conservation principle. Provided the reaction is complete and no subsequent degradation or synthesis occurs, the sum of the lengths of all product DNA molecules equals the sum of the lengths of the input molecules. Lengths are usually expressed in base pairs for double-stranded DNA. A site of length 34 base pairs already includes both complementary strands; its length should not be doubled to 68 base pairs merely because the duplex contains 68 nucleotides. Careful definition of the units is especially important in questions that count sites as well as the DNA between them.

Suppose a linear DNA substrate contains an upstream segment of 1200 base pairs, a downstream segment of 800 base pairs, an intervening region of 600 base pairs and two 34-base-pair loxP sites. The total length is 2668 base pairs. If the sites are directly repeated, the excision reaction leaves a linear product of 2034 base pairs and a circular product of 634 base pairs. The lengths sum to 2668 base pairs. The central interval of 600 base pairs did not include the two recognition sites in the definition used here. One site remains with that interval and one remains with the joined outer flanks. A common wrong answer is to predict a 2000-base-pair linear product and a 668-base-pair circle, because that answer assigns both recognition sites to the circle. It violates the expected site connectivity even though the total length is conserved.

If the two sites in the same 2668-base-pair substrate are inverted relative to one another, the product is still 2668 base pairs long. The upstream and downstream segments remain in the same relationship to the outside ends, whereas the internal segment reverses orientation. A conventional assay measuring only the length of the intact molecule cannot distinguish the two orientations. Instead, an orientation-sensitive readout is needed, such as a pair of junction-specific PCR assays or a diagnostic restriction map. This illustrates an important experimental principle: a valid structural change need not be accompanied by a change in total DNA mass.

Consider a separate example of integration. A circular donor of 2400 base pairs already includes its recombination site, and a recipient molecule of 7000 base pairs already includes its own site. Under the assumption of one conservative insertion and no additional DNA synthesis or deletion, the integrated molecule has a length of 9400 base pairs. The two boundary sites are made from the original reacting sites. One should not add the length of two new sites to 9400 base pairs because they are not extra DNA newly synthesized by the recombinase. If the recipient is circular, the product is a circle; if the recipient is linear, it is a linear molecule with its original terminal flanks. The length prediction is the same in both cases, but the physical topology differs.

A second kind of calculation concerns the frequency of recombination in a cell population. Suppose that a validated assay detects the excised chromosomal junction in 72 of 100 independent cells after a transient period of recombinase expression. The measured fraction of recombined cells is 72 divided by 100, or 0.72. The percentage is 72 percent. This does not establish that 72 percent of all target alleles recombined unless the cells are known to carry exactly one target copy or the assay separately counts alleles. In diploid cells, one or both alleles might have recombined, and the relationship between cell frequency and allele frequency must be specified. A cell-level reporter might be positive as soon as one allele changes, whereas an assay that requires both alleles to be deleted would report a different fraction.

For an elementary idealized case, suppose each of two equivalent alleles has an independent probability p of recombination. The probability that neither recombines is (1 − p) squared. The probability that both recombine is p squared. The probability that exactly one recombines is 2p(1 − p). These three probabilities sum to one, as can be verified by expansion. If p equals 0.8, the predicted frequencies are 0.04 for neither, 0.64 for both and 0.32 for exactly one. At least one recombined allele is present in 0.96 of cells. The difference between 96 percent of cells having a detectable event and 64 percent of cells having both alleles changed can be substantial when interpreting a conditional gene inactivation experiment.

The independence assumption is not guaranteed in biology. Cells receiving more recombinase may be more likely to recombine both alleles, while cells that express little recombinase may change neither. Local chromatin differences can also make one allele easier to recombine than the other. Thus the simple binomial model is useful only when its assumptions are given or reasonable for the question. If an experiment finds more double-recombined and completely unrecombined cells than the independent model predicts, variation among cells is one possible explanation. The observation alone does not reveal which source of variation is responsible.

Recombination efficiency is likewise different from maintenance of a product. A released circle may be present immediately after recombination but lost over subsequent generations. If a simplified model assumes that an initially nonreplicating circular molecule persists physically but is inherited by only one of the two daughters at each division, the number of cells containing that molecule remains one while the total population doubles with every generation. After n divisions, its fraction among the descendants is 1 divided by 2 raised to n. After four divisions the fraction is one-sixteenth, or 6.25 percent. Real molecules can also be degraded, distributed unequally, or replicated if an origin is functional. The calculation therefore represents a deliberately restricted segregation model rather than a universal law of excised DNA stability.

These examples show that mathematical questions about site-specific recombination need not involve a complicated enzyme-kinetic model. Frequently, the most powerful approach is to conserve DNA sequence length, distinguish molecules from alleles and cells, state the assumed topology, and separate the direct molecular event from later replication and selection. Each numerical result then has a clear biological interpretation.

6. The chemical problem: cleavage without losing the capacity to rejoin DNA

A DNA strand contains a series of covalent phosphodiester bonds. To exchange the ends of two strands, an enzyme must interrupt a phosphodiester connection at each reacting site and subsequently create a new connection between DNA segments. An ordinary hydrolytic reaction could cleave the backbone using water, but this would leave ends whose subsequent joining might require activation through another enzymatic reaction. Site-specific recombinases instead use a particularly economical chemical strategy. A hydroxyl group belonging to an amino acid side chain attacks the phosphate at the cleavage position. The DNA backbone bond is replaced by a temporary covalent linkage between the protein and the DNA. The other DNA end carries a free hydroxyl group that can participate in the reverse kind of exchange. The reactive DNA ends remain associated with the catalytic assembly rather than being released as uncontrolled broken chromosomes.

The chemical process is called transesterification because one ester linkage at the phosphorus atom is exchanged for another. In the first step, the hydroxyl group of the enzyme is the attacking nucleophile. In the later joining step, a hydroxyl group at the end of a DNA strand becomes the nucleophile and displaces the enzyme from the phosphate. The DNA strands need to be positioned so that this second attack joins an end to the appropriate partner rather than merely restoring the original phosphodiester bond. The specificity of the reaction is therefore not supplied by chemical reactivity alone. It is supplied by the combination of sequence recognition, spatial alignment and controlled changes in the protein assembly.

This is different from saying that the reaction requires no energy in any sense. Cleavage, conformational changes and assembly all involve free-energy relationships. Binding an enzyme to its substrate and changing the curvature or supercoiling of DNA can favor certain molecular states over others. Cells expend energy on producing the recombinase and maintaining their DNA topology. Nevertheless, the central conservative cleavage-and-rejoining chemistry does not ordinarily require the enzyme to hydrolyze ATP in order to activate each DNA end, because the phosphodiester bond is exchanged for a covalent protein–DNA bond rather than simply destroyed by hydrolysis. The later exchange restores the DNA phosphodiester linkage. This is why describing a site-specific recombinase as an ATP-dependent DNA ligase would be incorrect.

Catalytic recombinases are commonly divided into two large families according to the amino acid side chain that becomes linked to DNA. In tyrosine recombinases the hydroxyl group of a tyrosine residue acts at the phosphate. In serine recombinases the hydroxyl group of a serine residue performs the corresponding chemical role. The two enzyme classes differ in more than the identity of this attacking group. They differ in the polarity of the covalent linkage, in how the four DNA strands are exchanged, and in the intermediates produced during an ordinary complete recombination event. These differences provide a useful framework for understanding the behavior of individual named systems rather than memorizing a disconnected list of enzymes.

7. The tyrosine recombinase mechanism

Cre, FLP and lambda integrase belong to the tyrosine recombinase family. Their reactions can be understood using a minimal model in which two protein subunits associate with each of two DNA sites. Together these components form a synaptic complex containing four recombinase subunits and two DNA duplexes. The actual organization of a regulated system can include additional binding sites, DNA-bending proteins and other architectural features. The simple four-subunit description refers to the catalytic core responsible for the exchanges, not necessarily to the total number of proteins bound anywhere in the complex.

The first requirement is site occupancy. A recombinase must bind its target and adopt a configuration that supports association with another site. Binding does not guarantee catalysis. A protein may recognize the sequence successfully but fail to establish the required interactions with neighboring subunits. Conversely, a protein that possesses an intact catalytic tyrosine might fail to recognize a particular DNA variant. These two possibilities can be distinguished experimentally because defective binding reduces the population of protein–DNA complexes, while a catalytic defect may allow complexes to accumulate without products. Observing a DNA-bound protein is therefore not enough to conclude that recombination has occurred.

During productive synapsis, the two DNA sites are brought into an arrangement that allows coordinated cleavage. Four DNA strands ultimately have to be exchanged, but tyrosine recombinases typically do not cut all four at once. Two of the four catalytic subunits first cleave one strand at each DNA site. A tyrosine hydroxyl attacks the relevant phosphodiester phosphate, producing a covalent bond between the enzyme and the three-prime DNA phosphate. The remaining end bears a free five-prime hydroxyl group. Notice the polarity carefully: protein attached to the three-prime end, free five-prime hydroxyl. This is the opposite of the characteristic linkage made by the serine recombinase family. The free hydroxyl group does not have to be phosphorylated before this joining reaction.

The free five-prime hydroxyl at one cleaved site is brought into a position from which it can attack the phosphotyrosine linkage at the other site. An equivalent reciprocal attack occurs at the second cleaved strand. These attacks displace the covalently linked tyrosines and form DNA phosphodiester bonds in a new arrangement. At this stage, one pair of strands has been exchanged, but the other pair still retains the original connections. As a result, the four DNA arms are physically connected at a branched intermediate known as a Holliday junction.

The junction is not yet the final product. Its existence creates a central problem: how can the complex direct the remaining two strands to exchange rather than simply reversing the first exchange? The recombinase assembly changes its configuration so that the catalytic arrangement of the second pair of subunits becomes favorable. DNA bending and the geometry of the junction help coordinate this transition. For the standard mechanism, the second pair of strands is cleaved, producing new three-prime phosphotyrosine intermediates and free five-prime hydroxyl groups. The complementary exchange and joining then removes the branched connection, leaving the two DNA duplexes with their new flanking relationships.

The Holliday junction intermediate demonstrates the stepwise nature of the mechanism. If both strands of each duplex had been severed simultaneously before partner exchange, a model built around sequential single-strand exchanges would be inappropriate. In the tyrosine pathway, each initial exchange involves one strand from each duplex. The paired strand exchange establishes a four-arm junction while other backbone connections are still intact. The second exchange completes the rearrangement. The junction need not migrate through long stretches of homologous sequence as it might in a classical homologous recombination model. Its rearrangements are constrained by the short exchange region and the bound protein architecture.

A useful prediction follows from this sequence of steps. A mutation that specifically prevents the transition to the second catalytic configuration can allow a branched intermediate to form but prevent efficient accumulation of final products. In an idealized assay, the intermediate would accumulate relative to the wild type. A mutation that eliminates the essential catalytic hydroxyl group should instead prevent the relevant cleavage step from occurring. If all subunits bear such a catalytic mutation, synapsis may still be detectable, but neither the normal Holliday junction nor the final recombinant product should be generated. These are mechanistic predictions, not a claim that every amino acid substitution leaves all other properties of the enzyme unchanged. The mutant protein must also be examined for stability, binding and assembly.

Replacing the catalytic tyrosine with phenylalanine is a particularly clear conceptual comparison. Both amino acids contain aromatic rings, but phenylalanine lacks the phenolic hydroxyl group required for the characteristic nucleophilic attack. If the mutant retains the overall protein structure and the binding interface, catalytic loss is a plausible direct explanation for failed strand cleavage. However, a failed reaction alone cannot prove that the loss is due only to removal of the nucleophile. The substitution could affect the local conformation as well. Binding measurements, protein abundance and appropriate controls are needed to support the more specific conclusion.

It is also important to distinguish the temporary covalent recombinase–DNA linkage from the completed recombinant DNA. A covalent protein–DNA complex is evidence that a cleavage reaction has taken place, but it is not by itself evidence that partner joining has occurred. If a joining step fails, the complex may persist. Depending on cellular processing, persistent protein-linked DNA breaks can be detrimental. In the successful pathway the protein is displaced during DNA joining and can be released from the products. Thus normal recombination does not leave a tyrosine residue permanently inserted into the DNA backbone.

8. The serine recombinase mechanism

Serine recombinases use a related transesterification chemistry but a distinct strand exchange pathway. Examples include certain bacterial resolvases and invertases, as well as large serine integrases from bacteriophages such as phiC31. A minimal description again involves two recognition sites and a catalytic assembly of four enzyme subunits. As in the tyrosine family, individual natural systems may impose additional requirements for site architecture, DNA topology or accessory regulatory components. The name serine recombinase indicates the catalytic amino acid, not that every enzyme in the family carries out the same biological rearrangement or recognizes the same site.

In the productive complex, the catalytic serine hydroxyl attacks the backbone phosphate. In this case, the enzyme becomes linked to the five-prime DNA phosphate, leaving a three-prime hydroxyl at the other end. Both strands at each recombination site are cleaved in a coordinated process, giving two double-strand breaks within the controlled protein assembly. Each cleaved DNA end remains held in an organized relationship to the recombinase subunits. It is crucial to understand that cleavage of both strands does not imply that freely diffusing chromosome ends are produced and repaired by ordinary end joining. The catalytic complex itself retains the partners and governs their exchange.

The widely accepted subunit-rotation model explains how the cleaved ends are rearranged. One pair of DNA-associated protein subunits rotates relative to the other pair by approximately 180 degrees, aligning the broken ends with their new partners. In a conceptual diagram, the two halves of the complex turn relative to one another while the covalently attached DNA ends move with the enzyme. When the new DNA ends are suitably aligned, the free three-prime hydroxyl groups attack the phosphoserine linkages and restore phosphodiester bonds. The result is a pair of recombinant duplexes. The model accounts for the reciprocal exchange without requiring a Holliday junction as a normal intermediate.

The exact regulatory and mechanical details can differ between serine recombinases, and the rotation model should not be interpreted as a rigid rule that every enzyme always makes precisely one half-turn and then immediately dissociates. Additional rotation, reversal or failed rejoining can occur in certain systems and experimental circumstances. For the examination-level comparison, the defining concept is that the cleaved DNA halves can be exchanged by relative movement of the enzyme subunits, unlike the sequential single-strand exchanges of the tyrosine mechanism. The two families arrive at a similar class of rearranged products through different intermediate structures.

Sequence compatibility still matters. A serine recombinase may recognize both of two DNA sites even when their exchange regions are unsuitable for efficient completion. After cleavage and partner exchange, the DNA ends must align in a way that supports joining and restoration of base pairing. Recognition and successful ligation are therefore distinct steps. A mismatch in an essential central overlap can reduce correct product formation, although its precise effect depends on the enzyme and substrate. A student should avoid the simplistic conclusion that a serine recombinase can exchange any two pieces of DNA merely because it creates double-strand breaks.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which two purified proteins each rearrange a pair of cognate DNA sites. Protein A generates a four-arm DNA junction that can be isolated during the reaction. Protein B generates a covalent protein complex at the five-prime ends of cleaved DNA and no detectable Holliday junction. Taken together, these observations strongly suggest a tyrosine pathway for A and a serine pathway for B, provided the junction and covalent linkage have been properly identified. The absence of a detected intermediate by itself is weaker evidence than a positive chemical identification. An intermediate might be too short-lived or insufficiently abundant to observe. For that reason, a complete mechanistic conclusion should rest on complementary evidence, such as catalytic residue identity, linkage polarity and strand cleavage pattern.

The contrasting linkages are worth recalling through the DNA hydroxyl required for joining. Tyrosine recombinases retain the phosphate at the three-prime end, and the incoming five-prime hydroxyl makes the new bond. Serine recombinases retain the phosphate at the five-prime end, and the incoming three-prime hydroxyl makes the new bond. The general logic is identical in the two cases: one reactive hydroxyl is supplied by the protein for cleavage and another by DNA for joining. The polarity determines which end is covalently protein-linked and which end is nucleophilic in the joining step. Once this relationship is understood, the direction labels become consequences of the mechanism rather than unrelated facts to memorize.

9. Directionality, accessibility and reaction specificity

The site geometry predicts which rearrangement is available, but the enzyme and its cellular environment determine how efficiently that rearrangement occurs. Natural recombinases have evolved within particular biological systems, where allowing all geometrically possible reactions at the same rate could be damaging. A chromosome dimer resolution system benefits from efficiently resolving linked chromosome copies without continually promoting the reverse event. A temperate bacteriophage benefits from integration during establishment of a prophage and excision when the phage enters a productive phase. The chemical capacity to reverse an exchange is useful, but biological regulation determines when the exchange should proceed in each direction.

In many minimal systems, binding two sites on the same DNA molecule is favored relative to bringing together sites on separate, freely diffusing DNA molecules. This can bias the apparent outcome toward excision when the sites are directly repeated on the same molecule. However, such a preference is not a universal rate law. The distance between sites, their accessibility, protein concentration, DNA bending, supercoiling and the presence of competing sites can all influence formation of a productive synapse. Extremely short intervening segments may resist formation of an appropriate loop because of DNA stiffness; longer segments have more conformational possibilities but may bring their sites together less often. Different enzymes can have different geometric requirements.

Chromatin adds another level of regulation in eukaryotic cells. A short target sequence buried within a nucleosome or an inaccessible chromosomal region may not be as readily available to a recombinase as an identical target in accessible chromatin. An enzyme produced in the cytoplasm may also be ineffective if it cannot reach nuclear DNA. Thus a site that works well in an isolated DNA assay may behave differently inside a cell. The substrate sequence is a necessary determinant, but it is not the only determinant of activity.

When a reaction fails, the most productive question is therefore which step failed. Did the protein reach the DNA? Did it bind? Did two sites synapse? Did cleavage occur? Was the intermediate rearranged? Did rejoining occur? Did the product persist long enough to be detected? A negative result from a final reporter assay cannot discriminate all these possibilities. A series of complementary measurements is needed to localize the defect. This way of thinking is the basis for the experimental reasoning that follows in later sections.

10. The historical evidence for a specific chromosomal address

Bacteriophage lambda provides an especially instructive starting point for the history of site-specific recombination. A temperate bacteriophage can establish a state in which viral genetic information is maintained with a bacterial host over many cell divisions. In the case of lambda, this often involves incorporation of the phage DNA into the chromosome of Escherichia coli. Early genetic work revealed that the phage did not merely add a large amount of foreign DNA at arbitrary locations. Its incorporation showed a characteristic relationship to bacterial genetic markers. The location of those markers relative to the prophage provided evidence that integration occurred at a defined chromosomal region.

Allan Campbell proposed a model in 1962 that explained how integration of a circular phage DNA molecule into a bacterial chromosome could generate the observed arrangement of markers. The importance of this model was not simply its description of a circle entering a chromosome. It offered a testable interpretation of what happens to the order of genetic sequences when the attachment sites on two DNA molecules exchange connections. If the circular phage and bacterial chromosome recombine at a particular region, the prophage should be flanked by two new junctions containing sequences from the respective parental sites. Excision should reconnect the bacterial flanks and restore the organization of the circular phage DNA. The structure of the products could thus be used to test a hypothesis about the molecular event.

Subsequent biochemical and genetic work identified the relevant attachment sites and the phage-encoded integrase. This made it possible to distinguish proteins that carry out the catalytic reaction from proteins that assist the assembly or direction of the reaction. Rather than relying only on the inheritance of a phage-associated marker, researchers could examine the DNA substrates and products themselves. Studies of the lambda system have contributed substantially to the general understanding of tyrosine recombinases, including their covalent protein–DNA intermediates and their dependence on the ordered exchange of DNA strands.

An important lesson from the history is that a genetic map and a chemical mechanism answer different questions. A consistent site of prophage insertion is evidence for positional specificity, but it does not identify the attacking amino acid in the recombination enzyme. The prediction that hybrid attachment sites flank the integrated sequence can be tested by analyzing the DNA junctions, whereas the polarity of the covalent enzyme–DNA linkage requires biochemical evidence. The complete model becomes convincing when genetics, structural information and chemistry converge on the same interpretation.

11. Lambda phage DNA integration and the architecture of attachment sites

Lambda is a double-stranded DNA bacteriophage whose genome is linear within the viral particle. The linear genome has complementary cohesive ends. After entry into a suitable bacterial cell, these ends can base-pair, and the remaining backbone discontinuities can be sealed to form a circular DNA molecule. This initial circularization is not the same reaction as chromosomal integration. The cohesive ends are involved in closing the viral genome; attachment sites are involved in the later recombination with the bacterial chromosome. A circular viral genome can exist without being integrated, so detection of a circular phage molecule alone is not evidence that a prophage has been formed.

During establishment of lysogeny, the relevant recombination substrates are an attachment site on the phage DNA, attP, and an attachment site on the bacterial chromosome, attB. The letters identify their parental origins: P for phage and B for bacterium. It is useful to distinguish the DNA that is brought into the chromosome from the small recognition region where the exchange takes place. A large phage genome can be integrated even though the actual covalent rearrangement is localized to the attachment regions. No long stretch of homology between the surrounding viral and bacterial genes is required for this event.

The phage attachment site is architecturally more elaborate than a minimal recombination site such as loxP. It contains a central region participating directly in strand exchange and additional binding regions that help organize the reaction. The bacterial site is relatively compact. The central overlap of the lambda attachment sites is seven base pairs in the standard mechanistic description. This short overlap should not be confused with the full attachment site. In particular, the phage attachment region includes additional sequences recognized by integrase and by accessory DNA-binding proteins. A diagram that labels the complete attP site as only seven base pairs would omit the regulatory architecture that helps make integration directional.

The simplest symbolic model writes attP as P–O–P′ and attB as B–O–B′, where O represents the shared exchange region, and the symbols to either side represent parental sequence regions. This notation is deliberately schematic. It is intended to make the origin of the product junctions easy to trace, not to assign exact physical lengths or binding-site boundaries to each symbol. When the circular viral DNA exchanges connections with the bacterial attachment site, the resulting integrated prophage is bounded by hybrid attachment sites. These are named attL and attR because of their conventional positions on the left and right sides of the integrated viral region. With the conventional map, attL contains B–O–P′ and attR contains P–O–B′. The parent-specific flanks have exchanged partners.

This symbolic result also predicts an important distinction between the integrated state and the original substrates. The DNA organization at the two new boundaries is not simply a second copy of the original attP site or a second copy of attB. The recombination event conserves the sequence material but redistributes it into hybrid sites. Therefore, a reaction that recognizes attP and attB need not act equally effectively on attL and attR. In lambda, differences in the architecture of these two sets of substrates contribute to regulation of the forward and reverse reactions. The products themselves have changed the regulatory context for the next possible recombination event.

The phage enzyme integrase, usually abbreviated Int, is a tyrosine recombinase. Its catalytic domain carries out the backbone exchange by the general tyrosine mechanism. However, lambda Int also participates in higher-order interactions with DNA regions outside the immediate exchange site. These interactions help organize the attP DNA into an assembly that can encounter attB productively. The enzyme's role is therefore not limited to an isolated catalytic reaction at a phosphate. It is part of a sequence-recognition and assembly system adapted to the biology of the phage.

A bacterial protein called integration host factor, or IHF, is an important accessory component of the standard lambda integration reaction. IHF binds DNA and promotes sharp bending. Bending can bring otherwise separated protein-binding regions into proximity, making it possible for integrase molecules to establish a productive architecture across the phage attachment region. In a simplified picture, the DNA wraps and bends around the bound proteins to create an integration-competent nucleoprotein assembly, often called an intasome. The assembly can then engage the bacterial attachment site. This arrangement is an example of how DNA shape can contribute to specificity independently of the direct chemical contacts at the cleavage positions.

Why should a virus depend on a host DNA-bending protein? In the natural biological context, the phage genome must establish its genetic program inside a host that already contains proteins capable of shaping and organizing DNA. The phage can exploit such proteins to assemble a regulated reaction without encoding every structural component itself. Host participation also creates a relationship between the physiological context of the bacterium and efficiency of the phage reaction. This does not mean that IHF is the catalytic recombinase. Removing IHF may interfere with productive assembly even though the active-site tyrosine of Int remains chemically intact.

The standard integration pathway can now be followed in causal order. First, the appropriate phage and bacterial attachment sites must be present. Integrase and IHF organize the phage attachment region and facilitate interaction with attB. Once the two exchange regions are correctly aligned, the catalytic complex makes the first pair of single-strand cleavages, exchanges the strands and forms a Holliday junction. Rearrangement of the catalytic complex permits the second strand exchange and joining. The phage DNA becomes part of the bacterial chromosome and the new attL and attR boundaries remain. The integrated phage is called a prophage. Its propagation during bacterial chromosome replication is conceptually distinct from the chemistry that established the integrated state.

The genomic size of the bacterium increases by the inserted phage DNA length in the ideal conservative model. This does not imply that a recombinase synthesized the extra DNA. That DNA was already present as a separate viral molecule before integration. The bacterial chromosome has gained DNA at the expense of the separate circular substrate. Likewise, formation of the two attachment junctions does not imply that new copies of long regions were produced by DNA polymerase. They are the products of reciprocal exchange of pre-existing DNA segments.

In a lysogenic bacterium, the prophage can be maintained as part of the bacterial chromosome and passed to daughter cells. The term lysogen describes the host bacterium carrying the prophage. This stable inheritance is possible because the phage DNA becomes physically associated with a replicating bacterial chromosome. The integration event provides the physical arrangement; the regulation of phage gene expression determines whether the prophage remains in a comparatively silent state or enters a productive viral program. These processes are interconnected but distinct. A mutation affecting gene-expression control might alter the frequency of induction without directly changing the catalytic chemistry of integrase, whereas a catalytic mutation in integrase could prevent normal attachment-site recombination without necessarily abolishing synthesis of every phage protein.

12. Excision of the prophage and regulation of reaction direction

For a temperate bacteriophage that has established an integrated state, the ability to reverse integration can be biologically advantageous. When the phage enters its productive developmental pathway, restoration of a separate viral DNA molecule allows it to undertake the replication and packaging steps appropriate to its life cycle. The standard precise excision reaction uses the two hybrid sites, attL and attR, that were produced during integration. Their exchange reconstructs the original bacterial attachment site and the phage attachment site. The bacterial chromosome loses the prophage interval, and the phage DNA is recovered as a separate circle.

The reverse connectivity is easy to follow symbolically. The two hybrid arrangements are B–O–P′ and P–O–B′. Reconnecting the parental partners yields B–O–B′ and P–O–P′. This reproduces the attachment-site organization characteristic of attB and attP, respectively. A precise excision therefore removes the phage interval without requiring repair of an arbitrary chromosomal double-strand break. The defined junctions specify the boundaries of the reaction. Rare aberrant recombination events or other forms of genome rearrangement should not be confused with this standard precise pathway.

The excision reaction uses Int and IHF but also depends, in the standard natural lambda system, on a phage protein called Xis, or excisionase. Xis is a recombination directionality factor in functional terms. It binds DNA and promotes an architecture appropriate for excision. It can simultaneously oppose formation of the architecture required for integration. The key concept is that excisionase does not replace integrase as the enzyme responsible for reciprocal backbone exchange. Instead, it changes the assembly and substrate preferences of the recombination machinery. Other factors can modulate the system, including the bacterial DNA-binding protein Fis, but the core examination-level distinction is Int and IHF in integration versus Int, IHF and Xis in the standard excision pathway.

This makes lambda a powerful counterexample to a naïve interpretation of chemical reversibility. If a reaction can in principle exchange bonds in either direction, why does the phage not continually integrate and excise with equal probability? The answer is that the cell does not present identical biochemical conditions for the two processes. The starting attachment sites differ, their binding-site architecture differs, and Xis changes the available nucleoprotein configurations. The direction of the preferred reaction can therefore be switched by regulating the accessory factors. A reversible reaction at the level of phosphodiester exchange can become strongly directional in a regulated biological system.

An illustrative mutant helps make this distinction precise. Imagine a phage whose Int remains active and whose attachment sequences are normal, but whose Xis is absent. Under conditions that otherwise support integration, the absence of Xis does not by itself remove the catalytic mechanism needed to establish a prophage. Efficient natural excision, however, is expected to be defective because the excision-specific assembly cannot form normally. By contrast, if the catalytic tyrosine of Int is inactivated without any compensating recombinase activity, both the usual integration and excision reactions should be strongly impaired. The two proteins have different causal positions in the pathway, so their mutations have different expected consequences.

Similarly, a defect in the binding of IHF at a key DNA region can impair reaction assembly even if both Int and Xis are present. In a purified system this may be localized by comparing DNA binding, complex formation and final product generation. In a living bacterium, deletion of the host factor can have effects beyond the lambda attachment sites because the protein participates in other aspects of bacterial DNA organization. Thus a phenotype observed in the host mutant must be interpreted carefully. It is possible that some of the reduction in recombination is indirect. Rescue by supplying the appropriate factor and testing the reaction in a simplified biochemical system strengthen the causal interpretation.

A useful distinction is between precise excision and an abnormal loss of viral DNA. If a lysogen loses the viral marker, one might initially suppose that the prophage excised normally. But loss of a marker can also result from deletion, mutation or other rearrangements. To establish precise excision, it is necessary to detect restoration of the expected attB junction and, where feasible, the corresponding circular phage junction containing attP. Detection of both products supports reciprocal exchange. A chromosomal junction with missing bacterial bases or unexpected inserted bases would instead indicate a different or aberrant event. Thus even a plausible phenotypic outcome must be confirmed at the DNA level.

13. Distinguishing the lambda reaction from other aspects of phage biology

The phage life cycle offers many processes that involve DNA. The incoming linear genome can circularize through complementary cohesive ends; the circular DNA can replicate; an attachment-site reaction can integrate it into the chromosome; the prophage can be excised; newly produced genomes can ultimately be packaged. These are distinct operations even though they occur in the same biological system. They must be identified by their substrates and products rather than by the general observation that phage DNA has changed form.

The cohesive-end site used in genome packaging and circularization is generally referred to as cos. The attachment sites are called att. A question that asks which site mediates chromosomal integration is asking about the att regions, not the cohesive ends. The cohesive ends supply complementary single-stranded sequences that can pair and help close the genome. The attachment-site exchange instead brings two defined duplex regions together in a recombinase-dependent reaction. Students sometimes incorrectly associate all phage DNA joining with DNA ligase because they recall that ligase seals the circularized phage genome. The normal lambda attachment-site exchange does not require an independent ligase to replace the essential catalytic function of Int.

Another source of confusion arises from the phage regulatory switch. Lambda gene-expression control can determine whether a lysogenic state is established or maintained and whether productive development occurs. Changes in that regulatory program affect expression of proteins involved in integration or excision, but the regulatory switch itself is not the DNA backbone exchange reaction. A transcription factor can determine whether Int or Xis is produced; the recombination machinery then acts on the DNA. Detailed transcriptional control belongs to the later gene-expression chapters. Here the important link is that regulation of protein availability changes the preferred direction of recombination without changing the chemical identity of the enzyme.

The phage system also illustrates why genetic inheritance does not require every DNA molecule to possess an independently active replication origin at all times. An integrated prophage can be inherited because it is copied as part of the host chromosome. After excision, the separate phage DNA must rely on a suitable replication program if it is to increase in copy number. The integrated and free states therefore have different relationships to the host replicon. This concept connects site-specific recombination with the earlier chapters on extrachromosomal replicons and DNA replication without confusing insertion with replication itself.

14. Cre–loxP: a simple and widely used site-specific system

The Cre–loxP recombination system originated in bacteriophage P1. Cre is the recombinase, whereas loxP is the DNA recognition site. Their natural relationship illustrates a principle also seen in lambda: bacteriophages can encode enzymes that manipulate defined DNA junctions as part of their genome biology. P1 differs from lambda in important aspects of its natural maintenance strategy. The Cre system is well suited to resolving certain multimeric forms of P1 DNA, and P1 can be maintained as a plasmid-like prophage. It would therefore be wrong to assume that Cre exists solely to carry out the same regulated chromosomal integration reaction as lambda Int.

The relatively compact loxP recognition element is especially useful for experimental applications. Two 13-base-pair inverted repeats surround an asymmetric eight-base-pair spacer. In a minimal representation, the two recognition arms provide binding sites for Cre molecules. The central spacer defines orientation and contains the positions involved in strand exchange. When Cre molecules associated with two loxP sites establish a productive synaptic assembly, the general tyrosine mechanism applies. Two strands are exchanged first to establish the branched intermediate, and exchange of the remaining pair completes the reaction. Cre does not require the same dedicated Int, IHF and Xis assembly used by lambda. It can act in suitable purified systems without separate phage directionality factors.

This comparatively self-contained enzyme–site relationship explains why Cre can be used to rearrange DNA in organisms quite different from its natural bacterial host. The enzyme does not need to know whether a DNA sequence encodes a mouse enzyme, a plant regulatory protein or a bacterial marker. It must encounter compatible lox sites in a productive configuration. Introducing suitable sites into a genome defines the interval that can be rearranged. Expressing the enzyme determines when and where that rearrangement becomes possible. Thus the experimental system separates the specification of a target from the activation of the reaction.

However, the term site-specific should not be taken as a guarantee that every reaction is completely free of unintended effects. If multiple compatible sites are present, the enzyme can in principle bring together more than one possible pair. A pair on different chromosomes may undergo an intermolecular exchange. The relative frequency of such events depends on cellular context and site geometry. At sufficiently unusual levels of expression, recognition of imperfect sites or other adverse effects can also become relevant. The central conclusion for a student is that the desired pair must be identified, and that an experiment must verify the structure of its intended product rather than assume it solely from the presence of Cre.

The logic of a Cre-mediated conditional deletion begins with a target DNA interval flanked by directly oriented loxP sites. Such an interval is commonly described as floxed. The term does not mean that the interval is already deleted. It means that the target has been provided with recognition sites that make a future deletion possible. Before Cre acts, the intervening DNA is still present and may be functional. For instance, an important coding exon can be placed between two loxP sites, with care that the introduced sequences do not themselves disrupt normal gene function. After Cre-mediated recombination, that exon and one loxP site become part of a separate circle, and the chromosome retains a single loxP site at the rearranged junction.

Whether the altered gene loses its function depends on the role of the removed exon or regulatory sequence. Removal of a critical exon can disrupt the open reading frame, eliminate an essential domain or prevent production of a functional transcript. But not all exon deletions necessarily create a null allele. Some produce a shorter protein with partial or altered activity; alternative splicing or another promoter can sometimes bypass the expected effect. Therefore, a successful recombination event at the DNA level is not identical to proof of complete functional inactivation. RNA and protein evidence, together with the relevant phenotype, can establish the biological outcome.

Conditional recombination is particularly valuable when a gene is needed for early development but its function is to be examined in adult tissues. A conventional inherited deletion of both copies might prevent survival to the stage of interest. If the target gene is floxed instead and Cre is expressed only in a selected tissue or at a selected developmental stage, the gene can be altered after the earlier requirement has been satisfied. The system does not make the gene itself sensitive to time. It makes the DNA rearrangement conditional on production or activation of the recombinase. This distinction is fundamental to interpreting the resulting phenotype.

A straightforward genetic cross illustrates the principle. One parental line carries the floxed allele. Another carries a Cre transgene expressed in a defined cell type. Descendants carrying both genetic components can undergo recombination in cells where Cre is active. Cells without Cre activity can retain the original floxed configuration. The resulting animal may therefore contain different genomic configurations in different tissues. This mosaic arrangement is intentional in many conditional experiments. It allows one to ask whether a phenotype originates in a selected cell population without requiring every cell of the organism to carry the same rearranged target.

The controls follow directly from this reasoning. A floxed animal without Cre activity controls for effects of the introduced recognition sites and the genetic background. A Cre-expressing animal without the floxed target helps identify effects associated with Cre expression itself. The appropriate matched comparison depends on the genetic design. To verify that the predicted cell population was altered, DNA from the relevant tissue can be examined for the expected recombinant junction. Because a tissue sample often contains multiple cell types, an unrecombined signal may still be present even if recombination was efficient in the intended population. This is not automatically evidence that the entire experiment failed.

Temporal control can be introduced conceptually by coupling Cre activity to a controllable cellular signal, such as ligand-dependent activation of a fusion protein. The common CreER approach uses a modified hormone-receptor domain to restrict recombinase activity until activation by an appropriate ligand, often tamoxifen in mammalian experimental systems. The essential distinction is between the presence of recombinase-related protein and its ability to act on nuclear DNA at the chosen time. Such systems can exhibit incomplete induction or activity outside the intended window, so temporal control is not mathematically perfect. Interpretation requires controls for the inducing condition and for recombination in its absence. Detailed experimental implementation belongs to methods-oriented chapters; here the emphasis is on the biological logic of the conditional rearrangement.

Another important Cre application is irreversible or effectively persistent lineage marking. A reporter gene can be separated from a promoter by a transcriptional stop element flanked by directly oriented loxP sites. In a cell lacking Cre activity, the stop element prevents the intended reporter expression. In a cell where Cre removes the stop interval, the reporter becomes accessible to the promoter. If the recombinant chromosome is inherited by daughter cells, their reporter expression can persist even after the original Cre activity has ended. The reporter then provides a record of a past recombination event, not simply a measure of current recombinase expression.

A lineage-tracing result has to be interpreted with this distinction in mind. A labelled cell at a later developmental stage may be a descendant of a cell that expressed Cre earlier. It need not currently express the promoter that originally drove Cre production. Conversely, an unlabelled cell cannot always be assumed never to have belonged to the population of interest. The reporter may have recombined inefficiently, or reporter expression may be below detection. Lineage marking therefore gives evidence about ancestry conditional on the performance and specificity of the genetic reporter. The biochemical persistence of the rearranged DNA is what makes the historical record possible.

15. FLP–FRT and the natural problem of plasmid maintenance

A second widely used tyrosine recombinase comes from the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. FLP, or flippase, is associated with the natural two-micrometre plasmid. Its recognition element is called FRT. The minimum commonly used FRT architecture is similar in broad organization to loxP, consisting of a central asymmetric spacer flanked by two inverted binding elements. Natural FRT regions may contain additional recognition information beyond the minimal experimental site. The sequence specificity of FLP differs from that of Cre. Consequently, an ordinary loxP site is not interchangeable with an ordinary FRT site merely because both recognition elements can be represented as a pair of arms surrounding a spacer.

The natural yeast plasmid illustrates that recombination can contribute to maintenance of an extrachromosomal DNA population rather than simply removing DNA. Circular plasmids must replicate and segregate to maintain an appropriate abundance over cell generations. FLP-mediated recombination is part of a system that can support plasmid copy-number recovery when copy number becomes low. This biological outcome depends on the context of DNA replication and the plasmid's regulatory system; it does not mean that FLP itself is a DNA polymerase. The recombinase changes DNA connectivity and orientation, while replication machinery supplies new DNA. The relationship between these two activities is analogous to the distinction between lambda integration and the later propagation of the integrated phage.

The FLP catalytic cycle belongs to the same broad chemical family as Cre. The conserved tyrosine supplies the nucleophile for DNA cleavage, generating a three-prime phosphotyrosine linkage and a free five-prime hydroxyl. Sequential reciprocal exchanges proceed through a Holliday junction intermediate. The specific arrangement of the catalytic elements and protein interactions is not identical in every enzyme of the family. For the purpose of recognizing an examination mechanism, however, both enzymes can be classified together because of the characteristic covalent linkage and sequential strand-exchange pathway. The enzyme–site pairing remains system-specific.

FLP–FRT can be used in experimental systems to remove a selectable marker that has served its purpose during an earlier genetic manipulation. Imagine that a modified allele carries both a pair of FRT sites around a marker and a pair of loxP sites around a critical exon. FLP activity can remove the marker if its FRT sites are directly oriented, while leaving the ordinary loxP-flanked exon available for a subsequent Cre-mediated deletion. This example shows why independent recombination systems are useful in designing a sequence of genetic changes. The two reactions are specified by different recognition addresses.

The relative placement of sites in such a construct is as important as their identity. Suppose the desired exon lies inside the interval between the two FRT sites. FLP-mediated excision would then remove not only the marker but also the exon, even if the exon were not itself associated with an FRT sequence. The recombinase acts at the designated boundaries and does not distinguish valuable from dispensable DNA inside the loop. A complete sequence map must therefore be interpreted before inferring the effects of either enzyme. In a well-designed schematic, the FRT-flanked marker can be removed without destroying either loxP site or the exon it encloses.

Inversions and integrations can also be mediated by FLP, depending on the site arrangement. The name flippase does not imply that the enzyme always performs inversion. Similarly, the name integrase does not mean that every possible reaction of an integrase inserts DNA. Names often reflect the original biological role or discovery of an enzyme rather than the entire set of geometrically possible DNA products. When a question describes an enzyme with appropriate compatible sites, the products should be predicted from the topology and regulatory requirements given, not from the enzyme's name alone.

16. Comparative biology: chromosome resolution, inversion switches and integrases

Circular DNA molecules can become multimers through recombination or replication-related processes. For a bacterial chromosome, the formation of a dimer can interfere with segregation because two genome equivalents are physically joined in one circle rather than being present as separate circles. The Xer recombination system of Escherichia coli helps resolve chromosome dimers at a specific dif site in the chromosome. XerC and XerD are tyrosine recombinases that act together in the regulated chromosome-resolution reaction. The system is coupled to cellular chromosome segregation and division, including the action of FtsK, which helps coordinate DNA handling near the division site.

This example emphasizes a general biological problem: chromosome copying alone is not enough to guarantee successful inheritance. Newly replicated chromosomes must also be physically separable and properly distributed. Homologous recombination can generate chromosome dimers under certain circumstances. A different form of recombination, the site-specific resolution reaction, can restore the useful monomer arrangement. These two processes are not antagonistic in a simple sense. One contributes to genetic exchange and repair, while the other helps resolve an arrangement that can arise as a consequence of exchange. Both contribute to genomic stability when appropriately regulated.

A resolvase system provides another illustration of the connection between DNA topology and biological directionality. Some bacterial mobile elements create cointegrate structures during their movement. A serine resolvase can act at designated res sites to separate a cointegrate into its component DNA circles. The recombination step is mechanistically distinct from the transposition step that generated the structure. The requirement for particular site arrangements and DNA topology helps prevent indiscriminate exchange. Thus it is possible for a mobile element to use one enzyme for movement and another enzyme of a site-specific recombinase family for resolution of the resulting intermediate.

In bacterial gene regulation, an inversion can function as a genetic switch. The Hin system is a standard example of a serine recombinase associated with phase variation in Salmonella. Recombination changes the orientation of a DNA segment that influences expression of alternative flagellar antigen states. In such a system, inversion is useful precisely because the reaction can produce two alternative orientations without removing the essential DNA interval. A changed orientation can alter which promoter is directed toward a transcription unit and, through the organization of the regulatory network, change the proteins expressed by the cell. The biological phenotype depends on the regulatory architecture surrounding the invertible segment.

Phase variation differs from ordinary transcriptional regulation in an important respect. In a conventional transcriptional response, a regulatory protein can change gene expression while the underlying DNA arrangement remains fixed. In an inversion-based switch, the physical orientation of a regulatory DNA segment changes. That arrangement can be inherited through DNA replication until another recombination event reverses it. The resulting phenotype is consequently associated with a heritable but reversible DNA state. Not all phase variation uses DNA inversion, so this mechanism should be recognized as a specific example rather than a definition of every phase-variable phenotype.

Large serine integrases illustrate a different solution to the need for directionality. Representative systems such as phiC31 use distinct phage and bacterial attachment sites to generate hybrid products. In the absence of the appropriate recombination directionality factor, the reverse exchange of those hybrid sites is often inefficient. Such a system can favor stable integration because the products are not equivalent to the original substrates. If a suitable directionality factor is present, excision may be promoted. The important comparison with lambda is that both systems can regulate attachment-site recombination, but their catalytic mechanisms differ: lambda Int is a tyrosine recombinase, whereas phiC31 integrase belongs to the serine family.

These examples reveal a recurring evolutionary pattern. A limited number of basic catalytic strategies can be adapted to different biological tasks by changing DNA recognition sequences, protein interactions and regulatory architectures. Chromosome resolution, phase variation, plasmid maintenance and viral integration are not chemically identical in every detail, but they share the requirement for a controlled rearrangement at designated DNA sites. Their biological diversity comes partly from the contexts in which those sites and enzymes are deployed. The most transferable understanding is therefore the distinction between a conserved chemical reaction and its system-specific biological regulation.

17. From genetic evidence to a biochemical mechanism

Understanding a recombination pathway requires asking what each type of evidence actually demonstrates. The early lambda integration model predicts a specific exchange of flanks. In 1977, Landy and Ross examined the sequences of lambda attachment sites, providing biochemical confirmation of the relationship between parental and integrated junctions. The important inference was that the sequence relationship predicted by a model of reciprocal attachment-site recombination was present in actual DNA. This evidence distinguished the specific rearrangement model from a vague proposal in which the phage became associated with the chromosome without a defined exchange of covalent connections.

The sequence of a product junction can establish that a particular pair of flanks has become linked, but it cannot independently determine the entire order of reaction intermediates. Two different molecular mechanisms might generate the same final arrangement. To distinguish them, one must examine the steps during the reaction. In a purified recombination system, DNA binding, covalent cleavage intermediates, branched DNA species and final products can be investigated as different molecular states. The strength of such a system is that the experimenter can compare defined combinations of the DNA substrates and proteins rather than infer their roles only from the behavior of a complex cell.

A control in which the enzyme is omitted tests whether the detectable rearrangement depends on the added catalytic protein under the assay conditions. A control lacking one recognition site tests whether the expected pair of sites is necessary. A catalytically inactive enzyme that retains DNA binding can provide an additional distinction between substrate occupancy and productive catalysis. These controls are complementary, not interchangeable. The absence of recombination when a whole protein is removed shows that it is necessary under the conditions tested, but it does not establish which domain of the protein is essential. The absence of recombination when a catalytic residue is altered becomes more informative if the mutant is shown to bind DNA and assemble normally.

DNA footprinting gives a different kind of mechanistic evidence. In this approach, a DNA-binding protein can protect particular regions of a DNA substrate from cleavage by an external agent. The positions protected in the presence of the protein are compared with those exposed in its absence. Classical investigations of Cre interaction with loxP identified a protected region encompassing the compact recombination site, including its characteristic binding arms and central sequence. The protected region provides evidence for the physical association of Cre with the site. The method does not imply that every base within the protected region is individually contacted by an amino acid; shielding, DNA deformation and the shape of the complex can affect accessibility.

This distinction is particularly relevant to a site containing an asymmetric exchange region. A change in a binding arm can impair protein recognition. A change within the central region may leave much of the binding intact yet impair the proper pairing of exchanged strands. If the DNA is still protected in a binding assay but recombination is reduced, it would be premature to conclude that the site is completely unrecognizable. Instead, the mutation may interfere with formation of the catalytic configuration or a later reaction step. By comparing binding and product measurements, the experimenter can narrow the range of possible interpretations.

Structural studies provide another way to evaluate the reaction pathway. Crystal structures of recombinase complexes can show how protein subunits associate with DNA and how a DNA intermediate is positioned in an enzyme assembly. Structures of tyrosine recombinase complexes are consistent with a reaction in which different subunits are arranged for successive pairs of strand exchanges. A static molecular structure is a particularly strong source of information about geometry, but it does not by itself reveal the lifetime of a state or prove how frequently a state occurs in living cells. Time-resolved biochemical evidence is needed to relate the observed structural arrangements to the order of molecular events.

Single-molecule observations of lambda recombination have provided another approach to the sequence of reaction events. In such studies, changes in the effective length or motion of a DNA tether can reveal formation of a loop and subsequent changes in DNA connectivity. An important interpretive difficulty is that formation of a stable loop and formation of covalent recombinant DNA may both shorten the effective tether. Observing a shortening alone therefore does not establish that the reaction has reached completion. Comparing the effects of removing the bound proteins and analyzing the final products helps distinguish a noncovalent synaptic complex from a covalently rearranged DNA substrate. The conceptual lesson is broadly applicable: similar physical signals can arise from chemically distinct states.

The evidence for site-specific recombination is especially strong because these methods address different dimensions of a common model. Genetics identifies which sequences are joined and how the rearrangement is inherited. Biochemistry distinguishes binding, cleavage and joining. Structural observations show how the catalytic complex can align its DNA partners. Experiments with altered proteins and substrates test the necessity of individual components. None of these methods should be treated as a universal substitute for the others. A student who understands this division of evidence is better equipped to interpret an unfamiliar Part C problem than one who simply memorizes the sequence of enzyme names.

18. Reading an experimental dataset: locating the defective step

Consider a hypothetical educational experiment using a pair of identical, directly repeated target sites. The DNA substrate is capable of forming a circular excision product and a shorter linear molecule. Three proteins are studied: the normal recombinase and two mutant forms. Each protein is tested for stable site binding, formation of a branched DNA intermediate and accumulation of final recombinant product. Suppose the normal protein produces detectable binding, intermediate and product. Mutant A binds normally but generates neither intermediate nor product. Mutant B binds normally and yields an abundant branched intermediate but little final product. These observations suggest that the two mutations interfere with different stages.

Mutant A could be defective in the first cleavage step, but the data do not prove this directly. The protein may bind an individual site without bringing two sites into a productive synaptic configuration. It may also cleave DNA but fail to accomplish the first strand exchange and joining, so that the branched intermediate never accumulates. A direct test for covalent enzyme–DNA complexes, together with a test for synapsis, would help distinguish these explanations. The logically correct conclusion is that the observed defect occurs at or before detectable production of the first exchanged junction. A precise mechanistic claim requires additional evidence.

Mutant B has passed at least some of the steps required to produce a branched intermediate. In the standard tyrosine recombinase pathway, a defect in the second strand exchange or in the conformational rearrangement needed to activate the remaining subunits could explain accumulation of this intermediate. However, a branched structure identified solely by slow electrophoretic migration is not necessarily a Holliday junction. Protein-bound DNA or topologically linked products might also migrate abnormally. Structural identification or appropriate diagnostic cleavage patterns are needed before assigning the species to a specific intermediate. This caveat prevents a common overinterpretation of gel bands.

The same reasoning can be extended to the lambda accessory factors. Imagine that attP and attB react efficiently with Int and IHF, whereas attL and attR yield little product unless Xis is added. When Xis is added to the attL/attR reaction, the original parental sites are restored. This pattern is consistent with a factor that promotes excision-specific assembly. To conclude that Xis directly catalyzes cleavage would be unjustified. An experiment in which Xis is present but Int is absent can test whether Xis alone is sufficient for the observed strand exchange. The known reaction mechanism predicts that Int provides the catalytic activity.

Enzyme abundance is another possible confounding variable. A mutant expressed at a much lower concentration than the normal protein might give less recombinant product simply because fewer molecules are available. A comparison at equal total protein concentration is not always sufficient either, because a substantial fraction of one preparation could be unfolded or inactive. Verifying relevant binding or catalytic competence is therefore useful when the question asks whether a particular mutation changes the efficiency of a specific reaction step. In cellular experiments, measuring expression, localization and target accessibility helps to distinguish a biochemical defect from a failure to deliver the enzyme to its substrate.

A time course is often more informative than a single endpoint. Suppose the fraction of recombinant DNA in a hypothetical irreversible deletion assay is 0.20 at one observation, 0.50 at a later observation and 0.75 at the final observation. The increasing fraction indicates accumulation of product, but it does not automatically establish a constant reaction rate. The amount of remaining substrate changes, recombinase activity may change over time, and differences in growth or survival could alter the composition of the sampled population. An endpoint percentage should not be equated with a rate constant without a justified kinetic model.

A minimal first-order model can nevertheless be useful when its assumptions are explicitly supplied. If each unrecombined substrate molecule has a constant probability per unit time of undergoing a single irreversible event, and substrate synthesis, degradation, reverse recombination and selection can be neglected, the rate of loss of substrate is proportional to its abundance. Let S be the number of unrecombined substrate molecules at time t, and let k be the first-order rate constant, measured in inverse time. The equation dS/dt = −kS has the solution S(t) = S(0)e^(−kt). The fraction converted to product is then 1 − e^(−kt). A reaction that converts 75 percent of substrate in 10 minutes leaves a fraction of 0.25, giving k = ln(4)/10, approximately 0.139 per minute. Its predicted half-time is ln(2)/k, which is 5 minutes.

This model describes a hypothetical effective conversion process, not the elementary kinetics of every recombinase. In a real reaction, binding of two sites, assembly of several protein subunits, multiple catalytic steps and release of products need not obey a simple first-order law across all conditions. An inversion between two identical sites may also undergo reverse recombination, violating the irreversible assumption. A successful model is useful when it relates measured quantities to a clearly stated set of conditions; its limitations are part of the interpretation, not defects to be ignored.

19. Molecular verification and the difference between genotype and phenotype

A rearrangement changes the identity of the DNA flanks at its junctions. This property provides a general strategy for detecting the product by PCR. For a direct-repeat excision, primers positioned outside the original target interval may amplify a shorter DNA fragment after the interval has been removed. If both primers face inward and remain on the retained molecule, the difference between the parental and recombinant amplicon lengths can be predicted from the deleted interval and recognition-site contribution. A second assay that spans the newly formed junction can provide stronger evidence for the expected product identity.

However, PCR product intensity is not automatically proportional to the number of starting molecules. A shorter product can amplify more efficiently than a longer one under the same conditions, and the reaction can cease to be proportional to template abundance as amplification progresses. In a mixed sample, a prominent short band could therefore coexist with a substantial amount of unrecombined DNA. A quantitative claim needs an assay validated for comparable efficiency or another calibrated method. The distinction between detecting a genotype and accurately measuring its abundance is central to experimental interpretation.

Inversion illustrates the limitations of length-based analysis particularly clearly. A pair of primers entirely outside an invertible segment can produce amplicons of the same length before and after recombination. To distinguish orientations, one can reason about a primer lying within the interval and a second primer anchored in an external flank. The relative orientation of their extension directions changes when the internal segment reverses. A compatible pair generates a product only when the primer-binding regions face one another on the appropriate strands. Reciprocal orientation-sensitive assays can distinguish the two DNA states. Sequencing the amplified junction provides confirmation that the new arrangement matches the predicted inversion.

Restriction mapping can also reveal a rearrangement if recognition sites within the segment occupy asymmetric positions relative to its boundaries. Consider a linear DNA molecule with a fixed restriction site in the left flank and a single diagnostic site inside an invertible interval. If the internal site is 200 base pairs from the left boundary before inversion and the interval is 1000 base pairs long, the corresponding site lies 800 base pairs from that boundary after inversion, ignoring any separately defined boundary-site lengths. The restriction fragment between the fixed left site and the internal site therefore changes in length by 600 base pairs. The total substrate length does not change. The altered restriction map, rather than the total length, reveals the new orientation. This example assumes that the diagnostic site remains recognizable after reverse complementation, as occurs for a conventional palindromic restriction site.

A phenotype can serve as a useful readout when recombination changes the organization of a promoter, reporter or coding sequence. Yet a phenotype can be influenced by factors unrelated to recombination. A fluorescent reporter may fail to become detectable because its promoter is inactive, the protein is unstable or the cells have been damaged. Conversely, a change in reporter expression can arise from transcriptional regulation without the expected DNA rearrangement. When possible, a strong interpretation combines DNA junction evidence with the functional readout. The DNA assay answers whether the genetic structure changed; the reporter answers whether a biological consequence was observed.

The same principle applies to genetic applications in agriculture and experimental biology. Recombinase systems can remove a DNA marker from a modified plant allele or control gene activity in selected animal tissues. The important scientific reasoning is that the introduced recognition sites define the molecular boundaries of the rearrangement. The enzyme does not independently validate the usefulness, safety or completeness of the resulting allele. The intended structure and functional phenotype have to be assessed. These applications illustrate the power of a precisely specified DNA rearrangement while also revealing why sequence verification and appropriate controls remain necessary.

20. Integrating the chapter with the rest of fundamental processes

The study of DNA in this subunit began with the need to preserve and copy a genome. Replication produces additional copies of an information-bearing molecule. Proofreading and mismatch repair reduce copying errors. Other repair pathways correct chemical damage, restore breaks or permit copying to continue in the presence of lesions. Homologous recombination contributes to template-directed restoration and exchange between related DNA molecules. Site-specific recombination completes this sequence by showing how cells and mobile genetic elements can reorganize DNA deliberately at recognized sites rather than only restore a pre-existing arrangement.

These processes all depend on the chemical and structural properties of DNA, but their biological goals differ. A replication fork is designed to extend newly synthesized strands using a template. A repair complex recognizes and processes a lesion or abnormal structure. A homologous recombination complex establishes a relationship between homologous DNA regions. A site-specific recombinase uses designated sequence addresses to establish a controlled exchange. The common involvement of DNA phosphodiester chemistry, base pairing or branched intermediates does not make these pathways interchangeable. The substrate and the purpose of the reaction are the most reliable starting points for classification.

Site-specific recombination also provides a natural bridge to gene expression. A promoter can be reversed relative to a coding region, a stop sequence can be removed, or a gene can become associated with new regulatory surroundings. Each of these DNA rearrangements can change which RNA molecules are produced even though the recombinase is not itself an RNA polymerase. The next conceptual problem is therefore how the cell reads a DNA template and transforms its sequence information into a functional RNA. Understanding the physical arrangement of the DNA explains why promoters, transcription units and regulatory sequences must be interpreted in the correct orientation and context. That relationship connects the study of DNA integrity with the next stage of molecular information flow.

  2. The enzyme lacks all binding activity
  3. DNA replication has doubled every substrate
  4. Second-pair exchange or its required transition is defective
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D — Second-pair exchange or its required transition is defective. Formation of the junction demonstrates that first-pair exchange occurred. Product failure points to a later step, subject to controls for intermediate stability.

47. A protein forms a 5′ covalent linkage and free 3′ hydroxyl ends during coordinated double-strand cleavage. What model fits best?

  1. Serine recombinase with subunit rotation
  2. Tyrosine recombinase with obligatory Holliday intermediate
  3. DNA polymerase proofreading
  4. RNA ligation
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A — Serine recombinase with subunit rotation. The linkage polarity and cleavage pattern support serine recombination. Tyrosine cleavage has the opposite covalent polarity.

48. Int and IHF support integration in a defined system; attL/attR excision is weak until Xis is added. Xis alone produces no exchange. What is supported?

  1. Xis is the sole catalytic enzyme
  2. IHF is DNA polymerase
  3. Xis promotes excision-specific assembly
  4. attP and attB are dispensable for integration
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C — Xis promotes excision-specific assembly. Xis changes reaction directionality in the presence of catalytic Int. Its failure alone contradicts the sole-catalyst claim.

49. A mutation of the lambda integrase catalytic tyrosine preserves expression and normal binding. Which standard reactions should be impaired?

  1. Excision only
  2. Integration and excision
  3. Integration only
  4. Neither, since Xis catalyzes excision
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B — Integration and excision. Both attachment-site exchanges rely on Int catalysis. Xis cannot replace its nucleophile.

50. A tissue sample yields a strong short recombinant PCR band and a faint long parental band. Which claim is justified?

  1. All cells recombined
  2. Exactly 90% of alleles recombined
  3. No DNA rearrangement occurred
  4. Both products may be present, and calibrated quantitation is needed
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D — Both products may be present, and calibrated quantitation is needed. PCR efficiency and tissue composition can differ. Band intensity alone is not an accurate allele-frequency measure.

51. A fluorescent lineage-labelled cell no longer expresses Cre. What interpretation fits the design?

  1. A previous recombination event in it or an ancestor can account for the label
  2. The label proves current Cre expression
  3. Cre must still be covalently bound
  4. The chromosome cannot have replicated
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A — A previous recombination event in it or an ancestor can account for the label. Heritable reporter activation can persist beyond the expression window. The label records a genomic history, not necessarily ongoing enzyme production.

52. A marker and critical exon both lie inside directly repeated FRT sites. FLP alone is applied. What happens in the ideal complete event?

  1. Only marker removed because exon is valuable
  2. Only exon removed
  3. Both marker and exon excised together
  4. No change unless Cre is added
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C — Both marker and exon excised together. The FRT sites specify the boundaries, so the entire intervening interval is released regardless of biological function.

53. A linear substrate A→lox–M–lox←B is tested with primers located entirely in A and B. Which assay can distinguish inversion most directly?

  1. Measure total DNA length only
  2. Use an external and orientation-specific internal primer plus junction sequencing
  3. Count total nucleotide mass
  4. Measure recombinase abundance alone
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B — Use an external and orientation-specific internal primer plus junction sequencing. Inversion does not change total length. A directional junction assay reveals the altered relative orientation.

54. Two separate linear substrates A–site–B and C–site–D exchange their aligned compatible sites. What is the expected pair?

  1. A–site–B and C–site–D unchanged
  2. A–site–C and B–site–D regardless of alignment
  3. A circular product only
  4. A–site–D and C–site–B
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D — A–site–D and C–site–B. Reciprocal exchange reconnects the left side of each aligned site with the other right side. Molecule connectivity, not a generic deletion rule, determines the products.

55. A DNA tether shortens after addition of recombination proteins, then restores its original length when proteins dissociate; no new junction is found. What is most supported?

  1. Reversible noncovalent looping rather than demonstrated completed recombination
  2. Proven conservative excision
  3. DNA polymerase extended the tether
  4. Complete inversion with verified sequence
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A — Reversible noncovalent looping rather than demonstrated completed recombination. A protein-bound loop changes effective tether length without changing DNA covalent connectivity. The lack of a new junction and reversal support that interpretation.

Concept map

Quick retrieval practice

Why can direct-site excision leave a circle?

Joining the outer flanks closes the remaining molecule while joining the intervening DNA ends closes the excised segment, subject to substrate topology.

What distinguishes the first covalent DNA intermediates?

Tyrosine recombinases form 3′ phosphotyrosyl bonds; serine recombinases form 5′ phosphoseryl bonds.

Why is Xis not simply a nuclease?

It changes the assembly and directionality of λ recombination rather than supplying the strand-cleavage chemistry.

Why can a positive reporter be insufficient evidence?

Reporter expression can reflect effects other than the intended rearrangement; junction tests and suitable controls are needed.

What determines whether a recombination event reverses efficiently?

Product-site compatibility, topology, accessory factors and the catalytic system jointly determine reverse reaction efficiency.
