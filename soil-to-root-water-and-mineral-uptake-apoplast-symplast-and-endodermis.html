1. The root as a selective interface

A plant acquires carbon dioxide through its aerial surfaces, but much of the raw material required to build living tissue comes from below ground. A root must obtain water for cell expansion, potassium for osmotic regulation, nitrate for nitrogen metabolism, phosphate for nucleic acids and energy transfer, and many other substances. The challenge is that the soil does not provide these resources in a mixture that matches the plant's needs. A root growing in saline soil may encounter abundant sodium but little available phosphate. It must absorb water and selected ions without allowing the composition of its vascular fluid to become a simple copy of the soil solution.

Imagine a young root growing into a moist soil pore. Water first contacts the outside of the root. From there it must traverse the outer layers of living tissue before entering the conducting xylem. This journey across the radius of the root is called radial transport. Movement along the length of the root and stem is axial transport. Although these processes are physically coupled, they have different anatomical and regulatory constraints. The radial pathway contains multiple cell membranes, walls and tissue barriers. Mature xylem conduits, by contrast, provide a comparatively open longitudinal pathway for bulk flow.

Water is electrically neutral and its net movement follows a difference in water potential. Dissolved ions experience both chemical and electrical gradients; many require a selective membrane protein to cross a membrane. This distinction explains why root water uptake and mineral uptake can vary independently. An ion transporter can change the amount of potassium entering a cell without directly changing the membrane permeability to water. Conversely, closing water channels can reduce hydraulic conductivity even when the capacity for nutrient uptake remains intact.

The root solves its selectivity problem with a division of labour. Its peripheral cells face the soil and provide extensive surfaces for initial acquisition. Interior tissues regulate exchange between the outer root and its vascular system. The plasma membrane serves as a selective molecular boundary, while the endodermis limits an uncontrolled extracellular shortcut. Neither alone is sufficient: selective membrane carriers would have little control over vascular composition if external solution could freely bypass every membrane. The following sections trace this arrangement from the soil environment to the xylem lumen.

At the scale of an entire plant, the root and shoot exchange more than a single stream of water. The leaves supply sugars to living root tissues through the phloem; root respiration uses these substrates to maintain ATP-dependent processes. The xylem carries water and mineral solutes toward the shoot. These streams are connected through the living cells around the conducting elements. If photosynthetic carbon delivery to roots falls substantially, the ability to maintain ion gradients can decline even while the physical driving force for water movement remains. A purely mechanical description of the root is therefore insufficient to explain mineral nutrition.

Consider a comparison between a plant during active daytime transpiration and the same plant at night. The daytime plant may have a substantial gradient of water potential from soil to xylem because water is being lost from leaves. During the night, the gradient associated with evaporation may diminish. Ion uptake and metabolic processes may nevertheless continue, and their osmotic effects can maintain some water entry. The relative contributions of physical and physiological processes change with the plant's condition; there is no need to postulate two fundamentally different kinds of water molecule or an active motor that switches on at night.

Root uptake also serves the maintenance of internal composition, a concept sometimes called homeostasis. A plant needs particular ions within useful concentration ranges. An essential mineral can become harmful if it accumulates excessively; a nonessential ion can compete with essential nutrients or disturb osmotic and electrical balance. Selective uptake and controlled transport help a plant maintain composition despite variability in external supply. This is why it is important to distinguish whether an experimental observation concerns an element entering the root, accumulating in the root, or arriving at a distant organ.

The outer surface of an absorbing root is simultaneously part of the soil environment and part of a living organism. Its extracellular walls participate in the movement of soil water, while its living membranes regulate the movement of individual chemical species. At the inner boundary, an entirely different architectural problem is solved: the root must separate the environment outside the vascular cylinder from the apoplastic space inside it. A root can thus provide extensive access at its periphery while imposing restrictions at its interior. This arrangement permits more control than would a solid impermeable cylinder or an open tube directly connecting soil to xylem.

One important prediction follows from this division of functions. A plant with a normal endodermal seal but defective selective uptake may be unable to acquire a particular mineral effectively. A plant with normal uptake proteins but a defective endodermal seal may acquire the mineral into its cells but struggle to maintain appropriate concentrations within the vascular system. A third plant with both properties intact may nevertheless exhibit reduced root acquisition because the soil cannot deliver the resource. Similar whole-plant symptoms therefore need not identify the same physiological defect. The explanatory sequence used in this chapter is designed to localize such failures rather than memorize one cause for every symptom.

2. Water and mineral availability in the soil

Soil is not simply a reservoir of dilute mineral solution. It contains particles of different sizes, pore spaces, gases, organic substances and organisms. Some pores contain water while others contain air. The portion of water present around and between soil particles is influenced by the strength of its interaction with the solid matrix. Water adhering strongly to small particles can be less available for root uptake than an equal volume of water in a less retentive pore system.

Water potential expresses the free-energy status of water relative to a reference state. In a typical unsaturated soil at a fixed elevation, the matric and solute components are particularly important. Matric potential becomes negative because adsorption and capillary forces reduce the free-energy status of water. Dissolved salts also lower water potential. Thus two soil samples with identical water contents can differ greatly in their ability to supply water. One may have a more negative matric potential, while the other may contain a higher concentration of osmotically active ions.

Texture influences both retention and delivery. Sandy soil has many relatively large pores that drain readily; a clay-rich soil has a greater proportion of small pores and extensive particle surfaces that retain water. Neither texture is universally superior. When soils dry, the pathways connecting liquid-filled pores become less continuous, and hydraulic conductivity falls. Even if a root maintains a low water potential, it cannot sustain high uptake if the surrounding soil transmits water too slowly. This is a supply limitation outside the root rather than a defect in its aquaporins.

Minerals in the soil may be dissolved, reversibly adsorbed to particles, bound within organic matter or incorporated into poorly soluble compounds. Total element content is therefore not equivalent to the concentration accessible to membrane transporters. Phosphate can be retained by iron and aluminium compounds in acidic soils or by calcium compounds at high pH. An increase in total phosphorus need not cause a proportional rise in dissolved phosphate. The physical chemistry of the soil determines what concentration the root surface actually encounters.

The immediate region of soil influenced by a root is called the rhizosphere. Root respiration changes local gas composition; secretion of protons, organic compounds and other substances may alter pH or nutrient availability. Microorganisms also consume, release and transform nutrients there. The rhizosphere can therefore differ markedly from soil sampled several centimetres away. An experiment comparing uptake in two soils must consider differences in pH, water status and chemical form, not merely compare the mass of fertilizer originally added.

A useful distinction when thinking about water availability is between water held by the soil and water that can be delivered during a specified period. Suppose two pots each contain enough water to replace one day's transpiration. In the first pot the water occupies connected pores around a distributed root system. In the second pot the same water is held in small, isolated patches of soil distant from the active roots. The second plant may wilt sooner even though the pots initially contain equal masses of water. The relevant difference is the hydraulic connection between soil and root, not the total mass of water.

As water moves toward an absorbing root, the water potential close to the surface can become more negative than the potential in the bulk soil. This local gradient drives replenishment. Its magnitude depends on how rapidly the root removes water and how easily the soil supplies it. When uptake is slow relative to soil conductivity, the gradient is small. In a drying soil the same uptake demand may require a much larger difference. Eventually the hydraulic pathway cannot sustain the requested flow. Thus the root surface can be in a substantially drier microenvironment than measurements of the surrounding bulk soil suggest.

Consider two soils whose matric potentials are both −0.15 MPa. In the first, assume a negligible solute contribution; in the second, assume a solute potential of −0.35 MPa caused by dissolved salts. At the same reference elevation and with negligible hydrostatic pressure contribution, the total potentials are approximately −0.15 and −0.50 MPa. A root interior at −0.40 MPa can receive water from the first under the assumed conditions, but the second presents an outward water potential difference. This numerical example explains the initial osmotic effect of salinity before introducing any injury caused by particular ions. Salt tolerance involves additional processes treated in stress physiology, but here the important point is that wet saline soil is not equivalent to a water supply of high water potential.

Soil aeration provides a second independent constraint. Water filling nearly all pores can restrict diffusion of oxygen from the atmosphere to respiring roots. Although the water potential may then be comparatively high, ATP-dependent nutrient acquisition can become impaired if oxygen limits respiration. Root growth and membrane transport may also change. Therefore the expression 'adequate water' should not be taken to mean that all other conditions for resource acquisition are favourable. A comparison between saturated and well-drained soil must account for oxygen availability as well as water content.

Nutrient concentration at the root surface is also affected by the exchange properties of soil particles. Many clay minerals and organic substances carry negative charge and can retain cations electrostatically. Cations such as potassium may exchange between these surfaces and the soil solution. The total exchangeable pool can act as a reserve, but the membrane carrier encounters the activity of the ion in solution, not the amount adsorbed to a mineral surface. Nitrate is commonly more mobile because it is an anion and is not strongly retained by many negatively charged soil surfaces. These are useful generalizations; soil mineralogy and pH can produce exceptions.

The balance between dissolution, adsorption, biological transformation and uptake changes over time. An initially available nutrient may become less available after addition to the soil, and a previously poorly available reservoir may replenish the solution gradually. Students should distinguish a depletion experiment lasting minutes from a growth experiment lasting weeks. The former may chiefly measure the transport capacity of root surfaces under controlled conditions. The latter integrates soil chemistry, changing root architecture, microbial activity, mineral allocation and metabolic demand.

3. How water and nutrients reach the root surface

Before a nutrient can cross a membrane it must reach that membrane. Three processes contribute to delivery: interception as roots grow through new soil, mass flow with moving soil solution and diffusion down a concentration gradient. These categories describe transport outside cells and should not be confused with the mechanisms that carry ions across the plasma membrane.

Mass flow transports dissolved ions along with a volume of moving water. For example, if the soil water contains nitrate at 2 millimoles per litre and a root system receives 0.25 litres of that solution, the moving water brings 0.50 millimoles of nitrate toward the surface under the assumed uniform concentration. This is a delivery estimate rather than a measurement of uptake. Some nitrate may remain outside the root, be consumed by microbes, or enter a part of the root that does not efficiently transport it. When transpiration increases, the mass-flow contribution can increase, but membrane transport may eventually limit net accumulation.

Diffusion becomes particularly important when absorption reduces nutrient concentration immediately adjacent to the root. In one spatial dimension an approximate diffusive flux is J = −D(dC/dx), with D the effective diffusion coefficient and C the concentration. The minus sign indicates movement toward lower concentration. In real soil, tortuous pores, adsorption and incomplete water connectivity reduce effective mobility relative to a freely mixed solution. Phosphate commonly develops a strong depletion zone because the surrounding soil cannot replenish it rapidly enough. Uptake from the existing solution may be fast compared with its replacement.

Interception occurs when a growing root physically reaches additional soil surfaces and pore water. Root hairs extend the absorptive surface beyond the epidermis, shorten distances over which poorly mobile nutrients must diffuse, and bring membranes into contact with additional microsites. They are extensions of individual epidermal cells, not small lateral roots with independent vascular tissues. Root hair formation can be particularly valuable under low phosphate availability, but the contribution depends on soil conditions, plant species and fungal association.

Mycorrhizal fungi offer an additional route for resource acquisition. Fungal hyphae can explore soil beyond root depletion zones and transport nutrients to an exchange interface associated with the plant root. The plant supplies carbon compounds to support the fungus. In arbuscular mycorrhiza, the fungal arbuscule is separated from host cytoplasm by a host-derived membrane and interfacial space, so transfer still involves regulated exchange rather than unrestricted mixing of plant and fungal cytoplasm. A plant with reduced root hairs may consequently show different nutrient acquisition in sterile culture and in soil containing suitable fungi. A growth phenotype in one environment should not automatically be treated as universal evidence of a transporter defect.

The geometry of the root system matters because resource capture occurs at many surfaces distributed throughout the soil. A plant with a long, branched root system can sample a greater volume than a plant with an equal mass of root tissue concentrated in fewer thick roots. However, the relevant property depends on the resource. A deeper root may encounter water that is absent near the surface; a dense shallow system may access phosphate concentrated in upper soil layers. A plant cannot maximize capture of every nutrient merely by increasing one geometric trait.

A simple nutrient balance at the absorbing surface helps distinguish supply-limited and transporter-limited conditions. Let the external supply rate be S, expressed in micromoles per hour, and the net membrane uptake capacity under the prevailing conditions be U. Over a short period during which stored surface solution is negligible, sustained uptake cannot exceed either limit. If S is 4 and U is 12, increasing the number of uptake proteins may have little immediate effect because only 4 micromoles per hour reach the surface. Increasing external delivery could improve uptake. If S is 12 and U is 4, the transporter system is the more immediate constraint. Real rates vary continuously with concentration and gradients, but the limiting-rate model is useful for interpreting an experimental response.

Root hairs are especially effective at changing the spatial relationship between a plant and nutrients with low effective mobility. A root hair projects into a region not occupied by the main cylinder. Its membrane is thus positioned beyond the strongly depleted solution close to the root body. If neighbouring roots or hairs are crowded together, however, their depletion zones may overlap. Doubling the number of hairs does not necessarily double phosphorus uptake because the newly sampled volume may already be influenced by existing hairs. At equal total surface area, the spatial distribution of the absorptive surfaces can matter.

An experimental design can separate root hair function from general plant size. Grow a wild type and a hair-defective mutant in a controlled medium with low and high phosphate availability, and compare uptake normalized to root length or appropriate surface measurements alongside total uptake. If the mutant performs poorly only under low phosphate conditions, the interaction between genotype and external availability supports a contribution to resource acquisition under limiting supply. The result is stronger when root growth and viability are similar and when restoration of the altered gene restores hair development and uptake. A difference in shoot mass alone is less informative because developmental effects could alter demand.

A second comparison might grow the same plants in a well-mixed hydroponic solution and a soil substrate. In a well-mixed solution, depletion near a surface may be reduced by movement of water. A phenotype that is severe in soil but mild in solution can therefore arise from a difference in external diffusion and access, rather than a change in the biochemical ability of individual cells to transport an ion. Conversely, an uptake carrier defect may remain evident in both environments under appropriately limiting substrate concentrations.

Mycorrhizal exchange adds another spatial scale to this problem. Hyphae can explore narrow pores and extend well beyond the root surface. In arbuscular mycorrhizal plants, nutrients transported through the fungal network must eventually cross an interface to reach the host. The resulting gain is not simply free extra mineral: the plant supplies photosynthetically derived carbon to the association. The net physiological outcome depends on nutrient availability, host and fungal identity, and the carbon cost. In nutrient-rich conditions an association may confer less benefit than under severe nutrient limitation. This is why sterile experimental cultures and natural soil communities should not be treated as interchangeable environments.

4. The radial arrangement of root tissues

The root epidermis is the outermost cell layer in a typical young absorbing root. Some of its cells differentiate into root hairs, which expand the surface available for exchange. Inside the epidermis lies the cortex, commonly composed of several layers of parenchyma cells. These cells are alive and contain membranes, cytoplasm, vacuoles and cell walls. Their walls form extracellular routes, while their interconnected cytoplasmic compartments form intracellular routes.

The innermost cortical layer is the endodermis. It surrounds the central vascular cylinder, or stele, and is positioned to control exchange between outer tissues and vascular fluid. Just inside it is the pericycle, which includes living cells and gives rise to lateral roots in many plants. The stele also contains conducting xylem and phloem and associated living cells. The xylem transports water and minerals toward the shoot, whereas the phloem provides assimilates and other solutes needed by heterotrophic root tissues.

A cross-section is a snapshot at a specified location along the root axis. Young cells near the apex are still differentiating; further back, elongation and tissue maturation produce characteristic zones. Root hairs appear in differentiated regions rather than at the extreme tip. Endodermal wall modifications also develop along the root length. The timing of these events affects which surfaces are available for absorption and which barriers water and ions encounter. A drawing of a mature root cannot be applied unchanged to every longitudinal position.

The distinction between epidermis and endodermis is functional as well as anatomical. Both consist of living cells with selective membranes, but the endodermis develops specialized extracellular barriers. The cortex is not merely inert packing: its cells can store nutrients, modify solute distribution and provide resistance to water movement. The pericycle and xylem-associated parenchyma participate in the final regulation of mineral release into vascular conduits.

The detailed shape and number of cortical layers vary among species. Some roots develop an exodermis near the outer surface that adds another barrier to extracellular movement. Mature or secondarily thickened roots can undergo substantial changes in peripheral tissues. For an examination question, first identify the developmental region and species if specified; only then infer which pathway or barrier is likely to dominate.

The longitudinal arrangement of a root adds a dimension that a cross-section alone cannot reveal. At the growing tip, the root cap protects the meristem as the root advances through the soil. Cells in the meristem divide, and cells displaced farther from the tip subsequently elongate and differentiate. The boundaries between these regions are not perfectly sharp, and different tissue layers can mature at different positions. A single root therefore contains surfaces with different ages and transport properties at the same time.

The young tip has living cells and can exchange substances with its surroundings, but its conducting tissues are still developing. A segment may possess relatively permeable outer tissues yet contribute little to axial delivery if its xylem elements are not fully differentiated and connected to the conducting stream. Conversely, a region with mature xylem can have higher radial resistance owing to deposition of apoplastic barriers. Thus the position of greatest effective water entry is determined by the combined availability of radial access and axial transport capacity. It cannot be inferred from the presence or absence of root hairs alone.

A root hair develops from an epidermal cell and remains continuous with that cell's cytoplasm and plasma membrane. It does not acquire an independent endodermis or xylem vessel. Its contribution is to place more cell surface into contact with the surrounding environment. After water or an ion enters the hair, the subsequent movement toward the stele still occurs through the ordinary tissue architecture. In contrast, a lateral root contains organized tissues of its own and can establish a vascular connection to the parent root. Lateral roots increase exploration of soil through an increase in root system extent and branching rather than through the same mechanism as an individual hair.

In a typical primary root with a radial vascular arrangement, xylem and phloem occupy distinguishable positions within the stele. However, the number and pattern of xylem poles differ among species and stages of development. The cross-sectional drawings used in this chapter are functional schematics. The important relationship is that the endodermis encloses the vascular cylinder and the pericycle lies inward of the endodermis. A diagram showing one particular xylem shape should not be treated as a universal anatomical template.

Mature roots of woody plants can undergo secondary growth that changes their original tissue arrangement substantially. Some older portions become important for transport or storage while younger branches provide much of the absorptive surface. Even in herbaceous plants, differences in root age, suberization and local conditions can produce variation along the root system. A physiological experiment on an excised older segment may therefore not represent uptake by the entire living system. Measurements should identify which root types and segments were used.

When comparing root cross-sections from two plants, determine the order of layers before identifying their functional properties. For example, a thickened outer layer might be an exodermal barrier in a species that develops one; a lignified ring around the vascular cylinder might represent the endodermal Casparian domain. Staining for a wall polymer can support anatomical identification, but position and developmental context remain important. This prevents confusing an outer protective layer with the inner selective interface simply because both contain hydrophobic wall modifications.

5. Apoplast, symplast and transmembrane pathways

Every plant cell has a plasma membrane that separates its cytoplasm from its surroundings. The space outside that membrane includes hydrated cell walls and extracellular spaces. Collectively, these extracellular compartments form the apoplast. Mature xylem lumina also belong to the apoplastic compartment. Water and dissolved ions can move through connected walls without crossing a plasma membrane at every cell boundary. This is the basis of the apoplastic radial route.

The symplast comprises the cytoplasm of living cells connected by plasmodesmata. A molecule entering the symplast from the soil must first cross a plasma membrane, for example at the epidermis. Once inside, a suitably mobile molecule can pass to neighbouring cells through plasmodesmata. These channels are membrane-lined intercellular connections and their permeability is regulated. They do not make every cell identical or allow every macromolecule to pass freely. In a purely symplastic segment of the pathway, successive cells are traversed without repeated crossings from cytosol into cell wall space.

In the transmembrane or transcellular route, water or a solute crosses a series of plasma membranes as it moves between cells. For water this may involve aquaporins and, depending on the actual path, crossing the tonoplast around a vacuole. For an ion, distinct uptake and efflux proteins can be required at successive interfaces. A polar arrangement of carriers, with entry proteins enriched on one side of a cell and exit proteins on another, can make transcellular transfer directionally favourable. This route differs from symplastic movement, which uses cytoplasmic connections between cells.

In real roots the three routes are not three permanently separate pipes. Water may begin in the cortical apoplast, enter a cell, traverse several plasmodesmata and then leave again. A nutrient ion may be taken up by a root hair, exchanged with cortical cells and later be exported into the xylem. Describing a route is therefore a way of identifying the compartment and membrane crossings along a chosen segment, not assigning a single unchanging label to an entire molecule's journey.

Consider a membrane-impermeant fluorescent dye placed outside an intact root. The dye can spread within accessible wall spaces but cannot follow a symplastic path in living cells. If its signal terminates at the outer edge of the stele, the result provides information about continuity of the apoplastic route. It does not prove that water, a much smaller molecule, is blocked to the same degree. The transport properties of the tracer determine the conclusions that can legitimately be drawn.

One way to classify a pathway correctly is to track the membrane boundary rather than the visible outline of a cell. A solute can be inside the physical outline of a tissue yet still remain extracellular if it occupies the cell wall. Conversely, a molecule within the narrow cytoplasmic layer of a highly vacuolated cortical cell is inside the symplast if the cell is connected to its neighbours. The vacuolar interior is a distinct intracellular compartment separated from cytosol by the tonoplast. Crossing into a vacuole is not equivalent to passing directly through a plasmodesma into the cytoplasm of the adjacent cell.

The three-route description also clarifies why different tracers give different results. An extracellular fluorescent compound that cannot cross intact membranes may reveal the continuity of wall spaces. A membrane-permeable compound can enter cells and no longer provides a clean map of the wall pathway. A dye introduced into the cytoplasm and able to pass through plasmodesmata can report symplastic connectivity, but its ability to move depends on molecular properties and regulated aperture. The failure of a large fluorescent molecule to move between cells does not demonstrate that small ions are unable to cross the same junction.

Water is difficult to assign to a route merely by following a static image because it exchanges between compartments and may pass through the lipid bilayer as well as water channels. Unlike a membrane-excluded dye, water can leave an extracellular route at many positions. A measured volume flux represents the integrated movement of water through the entire available network. The network can change its effective properties when one pathway is blocked or when an aquaporin closes; the remaining paths may carry a greater share of the total flux.

An ion entering a root hair may subsequently be sequestered in the vacuole, incorporated into an organic compound, released into neighbouring extracellular spaces or transferred further through the symplast. Uptake into the first cell is therefore not a guarantee of immediate delivery to the xylem. Storage can buffer fluctuations in nutrient availability. For example, a transient increase in nitrate acquisition need not be matched by an equally rapid increase in nitrate delivery to the shoot. Compartment-specific measurements are necessary to tell these events apart.

For directionality in a coupled transcellular pathway, imagine a cell with a substrate influx protein preferentially located at the soil-facing membrane and an efflux protein preferentially located at the stele-facing membrane. The two proteins do different tasks: entry raises intracellular substrate availability, while exit transfers it into the next extracellular compartment. An adjacent cell can repeat the arrangement. Such polarity can favour net radial transfer provided the relevant gradients and energy sources support it. Polarity alone does not create energy, and a gradient reversal or removal of the driving ion gradient can impair the intended direction of transfer. This conceptual distinction prevents the mistaken inference that a carrier is an irreversible one-way valve.

The term symplastic transport should also be used with appropriate scale. Selected proteins and RNAs can move through regulated plasmodesmata or specialized long-distance pathways in plants. Their existence does not imply that arbitrary proteins present in the soil enter roots intact and travel freely to leaves. The root surface, membrane selectivity, degradation and intercellular gating constrain the acquisition and redistribution of such molecules. Distinguish experimentally established movement of a particular endogenous signal from a broad claim about all macromolecules.

6. The Casparian strip as a diffusion barrier

The endodermal Casparian strip is a narrow wall modification that runs around each endodermal cell, particularly along radial and transverse walls. Together the strips of neighbouring cells form a continuous barrier around the stele. In Arabidopsis the primary strip is made from a localized lignin polymer. It is not a plug that fills the entire cell interior. Its strategic position interrupts movement through the extracellular walls at the point where the outer tissues meet the central vascular compartment.

Why does this arrangement affect nutrient selectivity? A dissolved ion that arrives via the cortical apoplast would otherwise be able to diffuse along cell walls into the vascular apoplast without encountering a selective membrane. With an intact strip that shortcut is restricted. For typical radial acquisition, an ion must enter living cells through a membrane somewhere before it reaches the inner apoplastic compartment. That initial crossing can occur at the epidermis or cortex; it need not occur specifically at the endodermal cell membrane. A student who equates the strip with compulsory uptake exclusively into endodermal cells misses the interconnected symplastic route.

The barrier also helps retain substances that the root has already delivered to the stele. Without restriction on extracellular back-diffusion, ions loaded into the xylem could leak outward, reducing the efficiency of energy-dependent nutrient acquisition. The strip therefore supports both selectivity of entry and compartmentalization of the vascular interior. It is a passive structural restriction, not an active molecular pump. The living cell membranes and their proteins provide much of the molecular discrimination.

The chemical identity of the strip was historically difficult to establish because hydrophobic wall modifications were often grouped together. Naseer and colleagues (2012) examined Arabidopsis endodermal differentiation using chemical and microscopic approaches alongside manipulation of wall polymer production. Their results supported the conclusion that the early functional strip is lignin-based and does not require suberin. This is important evidence for separating the primary wall seal from the later deposition of a different hydrophobic polymer.

The development and continuity of the barrier matter as much as its chemical composition. A gap in a ring of otherwise normal strips could create an extracellular bypass. In a mutant with defective strip formation, greater tracer penetration into the stele would support impaired diffusion restriction. However, the composition of xylem sap or total shoot mineral content may not change in a simple proportional manner because living roots can alter transporter expression and compensatory barrier deposition. Loss of a structural barrier is therefore not equivalent to loss of all regulation.

In a root cross-section, the strip should be imagined as part of a three-dimensional seal rather than as an isolated spot in each endodermal cell. A ring drawn in a single plane represents the connected wall modifications of many cells. Along the length of the root, the strip surrounds individual cells at the relevant wall faces. Its functional effect arises because an extracellular solute cannot simply move around a single isolated point of deposition when the surrounding strips form a continuous network.

This geometry makes the endodermis analogous in one limited respect to a selective epithelium: it divides the extracellular environment into compartments. The analogy does not imply that animal tight junctions and plant Casparian strips have the same molecular composition. Plant strips are specialized wall domains; animal epithelial junctions are protein-based structures between cells. The shared principle is that selective transport is undermined if unregulated passage between cells bypasses the controlled cellular pathway.

Distinguishing a wall seal from a plasma membrane is essential for reasoning about selectivity. A lignified wall domain is not expected to bind nitrate selectively in the way a nitrate transporter does. It instead restricts a broad class of extracellular movement. At the plasma membrane, the availability and selectivity of transport proteins determine which molecules enter or leave living cells and at what rates. The combination of nonselective barrier and selective transport machinery gives the root greater control than either component would give alone.

A test of a barrier's function needs to distinguish delayed tracer penetration from permanent exclusion under the conditions tested. A small amount of fluorescence detected after prolonged exposure can have several explanations, including a limited bypass, tissue damage or movement through a route unavailable over shorter times. Comparing time courses and anatomical localization is more informative than recording a single endpoint. The presence of label inside the stele does not by itself establish which exact wall segment it crossed. Serial imaging and appropriate controls are required to locate the possible defect.

A simple thought experiment concerns nutrient loss. Imagine two roots with identical ion uptake and xylem-loading capacity but different resistance to outward extracellular diffusion. After active loading, their vascular ion concentrations initially rise at similar rates. As the concentration difference between the vascular apoplast and the external medium grows, the root with the leakier barrier loses more ions back to outer tissues. To sustain the same vascular concentration it must acquire and reload ions more rapidly. Thus a barrier defect may increase energetic demand even when short-term influx measurements remain normal.

When interpreting plants with defective Casparian strip formation, remember that developmental regulation can introduce secondary effects. The root may increase suberin deposition, change nutrient transporter abundance or alter growth in response to disturbed internal nutrient status. A reduction in mineral delivery in one mutant is therefore compatible with a structurally leakier primary barrier if compensatory changes restrict other paths. A direct structural assay, such as tracer exclusion in a matched developmental zone, addresses a different question from whole-shoot element composition. Both measurements are useful, but they cannot substitute for each other.

7. Suberin, passage cells and developmental variation

In addition to the localized Casparian strip, many endodermal cells deposit suberin lamellae over much broader areas of their cell walls. These layers lie between the primary wall and plasma membrane and restrict access from the extracellular space to the membrane. Their distribution and timing differ from the strip. A strip partitions extracellular spaces, whereas extensive lamellae affect how readily an extracellular substance can reach the membrane surface of an endodermal cell. A cell can possess a functional Casparian strip without having deposited extensive suberin.

In some roots scattered endodermal cells remain unsuberized among their more extensively suberized neighbours. These are called passage cells. Their membranes may remain accessible to solutes arriving through the apoplast, enabling regulated exchange in regions where adjacent cell surfaces are more extensively covered. Passage cells are not necessarily gaps in the original Casparian strip. The absence of a suberin lamella does not imply a freely open extracellular route through the endodermal layer.

Andersen and colleagues (2018) showed in Arabidopsis that the distribution and abundance of passage cells relate to developmental patterning and nutrient status. Their work supports a dynamic view of the endodermis: the barrier is not simply a fixed wall installed once during growth. Nutrient conditions can alter the cellular pattern and the transport properties of the root. This does not establish a single universal direction of change in every species exposed to every mineral deficiency.

An exodermis in some species provides an additional outer barrier that may influence access to the cortex and decrease leakage from the root. When multiple barriers are present, water encounters them in series and may redistribute among alternative routes. The root system's effective conductivity depends on the arrangement of tissues, their developmental state and the properties of their cells. Older root regions should not automatically be classified as incapable of water uptake; the actual contribution depends on species, age, suberization and local environmental conditions.

Lateral roots eventually emerge through the outer tissues of the parent root. This process changes local anatomical continuity, but local barrier formation and repair can limit uncontrolled exchange. A tracer signal around an emergence site must be interpreted in its developmental context. It is misleading to extrapolate observations from a wounded root to an undamaged root without controlling for tissue disruption.

The barrier state of a root can be described using the idea of access to successive compartments. A solute outside a non-suberized endodermal cell can reach the plasma membrane through accessible wall space. It may then enter that cell through a suitable transporter. When extensive suberin is deposited between the primary wall and the membrane, access from the wall to large regions of membrane becomes more restricted. A solute that entered the symplast farther outside the endodermis may still approach the endodermis through plasmodesmatal connections, so broad membrane coating does not necessarily eliminate every inward route.

The difference between the two barrier modifications is particularly important in a hypothetical comparison of two mutants. Mutant A lacks a continuous lignified strip but retains extensive suberin. Mutant B retains a continuous strip but has reduced suberin. In A, the possibility of an extracellular bypass is increased where the strip is defective; in B, wall continuity can remain restricted while membrane accessibility changes. A membrane-excluded wall tracer and a substrate that is transported by a cell membrane protein may therefore respond differently in these mutants. The test must establish whether the tracer itself can traverse the relevant modification.

It is also important not to treat greater suberization as inherently better or worse for the plant. A stronger barrier can reduce uncontrolled movement of potentially harmful substances and restrict nutrient leakage, which may be beneficial under certain conditions. At the same time, it can restrict access to useful resources and increase resistance to water flow. The physiological value of a particular barrier state depends on what the root needs to acquire, what it needs to exclude, and the environmental pressures acting on it. Plasticity allows the root to adjust this balance rather than maintain a single fixed permeability.

Passage cells illustrate this balance at the cellular level. A limited number of less-suberized cells can preserve accessible membrane regions within an otherwise more extensively suberized layer. Their location relative to the vascular pattern may be nonrandom, reflecting developmental regulation. A change in their abundance could change the available sites for exchange without abolishing the surrounding diffusion barrier. In an examination question, it is therefore reasonable to predict a change in membrane-accessible area from a change in passage-cell number, but not to conclude without further data that every nutrient flux must change in direct proportion.

Developmental comparisons are most informative when different zones are aligned by a suitable marker. Suppose a treatment slows root elongation. Sampling both treated and control roots exactly two centimetres from their tips may then select cells of different developmental ages. A difference in suberin deposition at that position might reflect a general developmental shift rather than a direct regulation of suberin synthesis by the treatment. Comparing zones with similar cell differentiation, or following the progression along the root, helps distinguish these explanations.

The endodermis also protects the plant against fluctuations in external solution. If roots alternately encounter dilute rainwater and more concentrated soil solution, a continuous barrier helps prevent the inner extracellular compartment from simply equilibrating with the surroundings through an uncontrolled shortcut. The living tissue can adjust uptake and efflux according to need. This does not imply that the stele is completely insulated from environmental changes: the water potential, solute gradients and regulatory responses of the root remain coupled to the soil. The barrier provides a degree of control, not isolation from physical laws.

8. Selective mineral acquisition and membrane energetics

The hydrated lipid bilayer presents a substantial barrier to inorganic ions because moving a charged species from water into the membrane interior is energetically unfavourable. Membrane proteins provide selective routes. Some operate as channels, which permit ions to move down their electrochemical gradients. Others bind and translocate particular substrates. Certain carriers couple the movement of one substance down its electrochemical gradient to the energetically uphill transport of another.

Plant plasma membrane proton ATPases use energy from ATP hydrolysis to export H⁺. This tends to acidify the extracellular space and make the cytoplasmic side electrically negative relative to the outside. The resulting electrochemical proton gradient can drive nutrient uptake through H⁺-coupled symporters. Nitrate uptake is a useful example: the negative charge of nitrate can make inward movement electrically unfavourable even when the chemical concentration gradient is favourable. Coupling entry to protons can make the total transport process energetically favourable. This is secondary active transport, not direct ATP hydrolysis by the nitrate carrier itself.

For a monovalent cation at temperature T, the free-energy change for transfer from outside to inside can be approximated by ΔG = RT ln(Cin/Cout) + zF(Vin − Vout), where z is charge number, F is the Faraday constant, and concentrations approximate activities in dilute solutions. The logarithmic term is positive for uphill accumulation; the electrical term is negative for a cation entering a negatively charged cell. These terms must be considered together. A tenfold concentration increase is not sufficient evidence that a cation requires an active transporter.

Take a hypothetical root cell at 25 °C with internal potassium concentration 100 mM, external potassium concentration 1 mM and membrane potential −150 mV. The concentration contribution is about 2.478 kJ mol⁻¹ × ln(100), or 11.4 kJ mol⁻¹. The electrical contribution is approximately 96.485 kJ V⁻¹ mol⁻¹ × −0.150 V, or −14.5 kJ mol⁻¹. The net change is approximately −3.1 kJ mol⁻¹, so inward potassium movement is thermodynamically favourable under these assumptions. The result does not establish a high uptake rate: it predicts direction through a suitable open pathway, not abundance or conductance of channels.

Plants express transport systems with different apparent affinities and capacities, enabling acquisition over varying external concentrations. An illustrative saturable component follows v = Vmax C/(Km + C). At C = Km the rate equals half Vmax. Actual root uptake may include several transport systems, outward leakage, changing membrane voltage and feedback regulation; a single curve is a model rather than a universal law. Lower apparent Km indicates higher apparent affinity in this model, but does not imply a greater maximum rate.

Rhizosphere pH changes nutrient chemistry and affects electrochemical gradients. Proton export may help mobilize certain nutrients; the balance of cation and anion uptake can also alter the net pH response. Nitrate and ammonium nutrition may therefore have different effects on soil pH, depending on transport and assimilation. A deficiency of nitrate uptake proteins reduces entry of nitrate from the medium, whereas a nitrate reductase deficiency impairs conversion of nitrate to nitrite after entry. These lesions affect different steps and can produce different internal nitrate pools.

For an anion, the sign of the electrical contribution reverses relative to that for potassium. Suppose nitrate is 10 mM inside and 1 mM outside a cell with an interior voltage of −120 mV at 25 °C. The chemical term is RT ln(10), approximately +5.71 kJ mol⁻¹. With charge number −1, the electrical term is also positive: −1 × 96.485 × −0.120 equals +11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. The combined inward free-energy change is approximately +17.29 kJ mol⁻¹. Passive nitrate entry through an open channel would not be energetically favourable for this specified case.

Consider the same cell with pH 5.5 outside and pH 7.0 inside. The ratio of internal to external proton activities is approximately 10⁻¹·⁵. A proton entering the cell has a chemical free-energy contribution of RT ln(10⁻¹·⁵), about −8.56 kJ mol⁻¹. Its electrical contribution at −120 mV is −11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. The total is about −20.14 kJ mol⁻¹. In a hypothetical tightly coupled one-proton/one-nitrate symport, adding the two changes gives roughly −2.85 kJ per mole of coupled transport events. The combined process is favourable under these illustrative conditions. This is a demonstration of energy coupling, not a claim that all plant nitrate transporters have the same stoichiometry or behaviour.

The proton gradient is not an inexhaustible source of energy. Each coupled inward flux tends to dissipate the electrochemical difference that drives it. The ATPase and other cellular processes help maintain the gradient. Inhibition of ATP synthesis can therefore impair uptake indirectly, even though the nutrient carrier does not hydrolyse ATP. The same reasoning explains why changing external pH can alter nutrient acquisition. Acidification can increase the inward chemical driving contribution for protons, but an excessive pH change may affect protein function, membrane integrity and solute chemistry. A single thermodynamic prediction must be understood under stated conditions.

It is useful to distinguish gross influx from net uptake. A root cell may absorb potassium through one protein while releasing potassium through another. If gross influx is 10 and gross efflux is 3 micromoles per hour, net uptake is 7. If a treatment leaves influx unchanged but raises efflux to 8, net uptake falls to 2. A depletion measurement in the external solution primarily reports net removal from that solution. A carefully designed tracer experiment may give additional information about inward movement, particularly over short times when redistribution of label is limited. One should not equate a reduced net uptake rate with a reduced gross influx without examining the possibility of increased loss.

Nutrient demand can also change transport activity. A plant that has accumulated a sufficient internal nutrient pool may reduce expression or activity of some uptake systems. Under deficiency, enhanced uptake capacity or changes in root architecture can help increase acquisition. However, uptake is constrained by the availability of energy, external substrate and other resources. A deficiency-induced increase in transporter abundance does not prove that actual uptake has increased if soil supply remains limiting.

The pH of the rhizosphere can reflect an overall charge balance. Uptake and export of charged solutes must be accompanied by other ionic movements or changes in chemical species to prevent indefinite accumulation of charge. A plant supplied predominantly with ammonium may acidify the surrounding medium under many conditions, while nitrate nutrition can produce a different net pH response. The outcome integrates membrane transport and assimilation, and is not explained by an isolated transporter alone. This provides a link between nutrient acquisition and subsequent biochemical processing without requiring the full pathways of nitrogen assimilation here.

A closer look at individual nutrients demonstrates why a single phrase such as 'active absorption of minerals' is inadequate. Potassium is a positively charged ion required for osmotic regulation and numerous cellular functions. Depending on the external concentration and membrane voltage, inward movement can be energetically favourable through channels or can require other transport systems to maintain acquisition at low availability. The same plant may use more than one type of potassium transport protein. Under extremely low external potassium, the chemical cost of accumulation rises, so the electrochemical calculation rather than the ion's name determines whether a particular inward movement is favourable.

Phosphate is absorbed in ionic forms whose relative abundance depends on pH. Because phosphate availability in soil is often limited by adsorption and precipitation, both external delivery and membrane acquisition are important. Plant phosphate transport proteins commonly use the proton electrochemical gradient to acquire phosphate. Increasing root hair exploration or forming a suitable fungal association can improve access to phosphate in soil, while increasing transporter activity can improve capture from a given accessible solution. These are distinct possible responses to deficiency. The former changes the physical resource interface; the latter changes the properties of its membranes.

Calcium illustrates another consideration. It is essential for cell wall properties and cellular signaling, yet high free cytosolic calcium must be carefully controlled. Calcium can move with the transpiration stream and reach different tissues, but its entry into living cytoplasm is regulated. Channels and active removal or sequestration help maintain cytosolic homeostasis. Its physiological roles therefore cannot be understood simply by equating external supply with a high intracellular concentration. In a question about calcium delivery to a rapidly growing organ, both root acquisition and water-dependent vascular movement may be relevant.

For nitrate and ammonium, the route to assimilation differs after membrane entry. Nitrate may be transported into the root, retained in a storage compartment, sent toward the shoot or reduced through the nitrogen assimilation pathway. Ammonium is already in a more reduced nitrogen state and is handled by different transport and assimilation processes. A comparison of equal molar concentrations of the two nitrogen sources does not guarantee equal acquisition rates, energy costs or pH effects. Those outcomes depend on the available transport systems and subsequent metabolism. The biochemical reactions themselves belong to Subunit 6.C.

An examination may ask why increasing external nutrient concentration eventually fails to produce a proportionate increase in uptake. If a specific carrier has a finite number of transport sites and a limited rate of turnover, the transport component can approach saturation. For the illustrative model with Vmax = 12 micromoles per hour and Km = 0.5 mM, uptake at 0.5 mM is 6 micromoles per hour, whereas at 4.5 mM it is 10.8. The ninefold increase in concentration yields only a 1.8-fold increase in rate. At 0.05 mM, the predicted rate is about 1.09, so changing affinity or supply near low concentrations can have substantial effects.

Now compare two hypothetical uptake systems with the same Vmax of 12 but apparent Km values of 0.5 and 2.0 mM. At an external concentration of 0.5 mM, the first yields 6 and the second 2.4 micromoles per hour. At 20 mM, the rates are approximately 11.71 and 10.91. Their difference is much less pronounced at high substrate concentration. This is why a concentration series is necessary to distinguish changes in apparent capacity from changes in apparent affinity. In real roots, the observed curve can reflect a mixture of systems plus net efflux; the parameters should be interpreted as physiological descriptors unless the individual transport proteins have been isolated.

Another important distinction is between transport specificity and competition. A carrier or channel may discriminate among ions, but selectivity is seldom a statement that every alternative substrate is excluded absolutely. An abundant external ion can influence the activity of a transport system, change membrane voltage or affect the chemical activities of other ions. Saline soil can therefore interfere with nutrient acquisition through more than its osmotic effect. To identify direct competition, experiments should vary the potentially competing ion while maintaining relevant osmotic and electrical conditions as far as possible. A reduction in uptake following addition of a salt does not by itself prove that the added ion binds the same site as the nutrient.

An uptake curve measured in an intact root also reflects the plant's internal demand. A nutrient-starved plant and a nutrient-replete plant may express different quantities of transport machinery or maintain different gradients. In an experiment comparing their capacities, the nutritional pre-treatment is an important independent variable. It is not sufficient to record only the concentration in the assay solution. The physiological history of the plant can influence how it responds to the same external concentration.

9. Aquaporins and radial hydraulic conductivity

Water channels facilitate the passage of water across membranes. In plant roots, plasma membrane intrinsic proteins, or PIPs, are particularly relevant to cell entry and exit. Tonoplast intrinsic proteins, or TIPs, influence water movement between cytoplasm and vacuole. An aquaporin does not create a water potential gradient; rather, its presence and activity can change the rate at which water responds to an existing gradient. Under reversed driving forces, a passive water channel can support net movement in the opposite direction.

At the scale of a root segment, a useful phenomenological expression is Jv = Lp(Ψsoil − Ψxylem), where Jv denotes inward volume flux per unit reference area, Lp is effective hydraulic conductivity, and the water potentials are expressed in consistent pressure units. This simplified relation treats the root as a single hydraulic element at steady state. It neglects details such as distributed storage, spatially varying solute permeability and the influence of multiple driving forces. Nevertheless, it makes a critical distinction between the driving gradient and the conductivity of the pathway.

Suppose a root has effective conductivity 2 × 10⁻⁷ m s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ and the soil and xylem water potentials are −0.20 MPa and −0.70 MPa respectively. The inward difference is 0.50 MPa, so the predicted flux is 1 × 10⁻⁷ m s⁻¹. If a treatment halves the conductivity and the potentials remain fixed, the predicted flux halves. A reduction in root water uptake is therefore not sufficient evidence for an altered soil water potential. The same change can arise from greater hydraulic resistance. Conversely, a greater gradient can compensate for some loss of conductivity.

The composite transport model explains how extracellular and cellular pathways can act partly in parallel across the root cylinder, while successive tissues impose resistances in series. As a simple analogy, two parallel paths with conductances 2 and 3 in identical arbitrary units have total conductance 5 under the same imposed potential difference. If they are in series instead, their effective conductance is 1/(1/2 + 1/3), or 1.2. Actual root tissues are three-dimensional networks, so this example illustrates network logic rather than giving a numerical estimate of a real root.

Root hydraulic behaviour can differ when driven by externally applied hydrostatic pressure and when driven by osmotic gradients. Water movement through an extracellular pathway that allows solutes to pass does not respond to an osmotic difference in the same way as water movement across an ideal semipermeable membrane. Consequently hydraulic conductivity measured with one experimental driving force need not equal that measured with another. Aquaporin contributions vary with species, root zone, barrier development and treatment conditions; a single percentage should not be memorized as the universal fraction of root uptake through water channels.

Cells can regulate aquaporin activity through changes in channel gating, abundance or membrane localization. Environmental changes such as oxygen limitation can influence root metabolism and hydraulic properties. A chemical treatment that reduces root conductivity must be evaluated with appropriate controls because it may alter membranes, respiration or cell viability as well as channel activity. A decrease in conductivity is a physiological result; identifying the molecular cause requires additional evidence.

The distinction between hydraulic conductivity and total conductance is frequently examined. Conductivity in a root-level flux equation is normalized to a chosen area. Conductance relates the total volume flow to the driving difference without area normalization. If a root system has twice the effective absorptive area and all local hydraulic properties and gradients remain identical, its total conductance may be approximately doubled even though its conductivity per unit area remains unchanged. Differences in root size must therefore be accounted for before interpreting conductivity measurements.

As an illustration, suppose a root system receives 2 × 10⁻⁹ m³ s⁻¹ under a difference of 0.40 MPa. Its total conductance in this simplified steady-state case is 5 × 10⁻⁹ m³ s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹. If its effective reference area is 0.02 m², the corresponding conductivity is 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ m s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹. A root with the same conductance but twice the area would have half this normalized conductivity. Reporting only the total flow could conceal differences in local transport capacity.

The ideal water-potential expression works particularly well when both sides of a barrier are effectively separated with respect to the dissolved solute under consideration. In real roots, some solutes may cross through carriers or extracellular routes. An approximate membrane transport relation is Jv = Lp(ΔP − σΔπ), where ΔP is the pressure difference between the two sides and Δπ is the osmotic pressure difference with the same orientation. The reflection coefficient σ represents how effectively that solute is excluded by the barrier in the model. A value near one means the osmotic difference acts almost fully; a value near zero means the solute contributes little to the sustained osmotic driving term across that pathway. This is a local simplified equation and should not be applied blindly to an entire heterogeneous root.

For example, let external pressure and internal pressure be equal and let internal osmotic pressure exceed external osmotic pressure by 0.30 MPa. With the outside-to-inside convention, Δπ is −0.30 MPa, so the inward driving term is +0.30σ MPa. When σ is one, the full osmotic difference promotes inward flow. When σ is 0.2, the effective contribution is only 0.06 MPa in this simplified model. This helps explain why routes with different solute permeability respond differently to osmotic forcing even if their response to hydrostatic pressure is similar.

A whole-root hydraulic experiment may involve storage of water in living cells and elastic tissue changes, especially immediately after an imposed pressure step. The initial transient flow may partly reflect filling or emptying of these compartments rather than steady transport to the xylem. Measurements of steady flow under maintained driving conditions are therefore valuable for estimating conductance. When comparing treatments, note whether a reported measurement describes a transient response or a steady-state relationship.

Suppose an aquaporin alteration decreases cell membrane permeability but an extracellular route remains comparatively conductive. The total root response can be smaller than the decrease measured at an individual cell. Conversely, if a strong apoplastic barrier requires much of the water to use cellular paths, the same aquaporin alteration could have a larger whole-root effect. These predictions follow from the network arrangement and explain why it is hazardous to generalize from one species or root zone to every plant. The molecular property of a channel and the physiological property of an organ must be related through anatomy.

To connect water movement at the membrane scale to movement across a root, consider a sequence of outer tissues, endodermis and inner vascular tissues. Each segment requires a difference in water potential to support a steady inward flow. If the same volume passes through each segment without accumulation, the individual potential drops sum to the overall difference. A high-resistance segment consumes a relatively large fraction of the total driving difference. This is the physical meaning of a hydraulic bottleneck.

For a simplified example, take an outer tissue resistance of 2 units and an endodermal resistance of 8 units, arranged in series. Under a total potential difference of 5 units, the steady flow is 5/(2+8), or 0.5 flow units. The outer tissue accounts for a drop of 1 potential unit and the endodermis for a drop of 4. Reducing outer tissue resistance from 2 to 1 increases the total flow to about 0.56, whereas reducing endodermal resistance from 8 to 4 raises it to about 0.83. In this model, changing the dominant resistance has the greater effect. This is a useful quantitative way to interpret why the same molecular intervention in different tissues can have different organ-level consequences.

A resistance model also explains why root anatomy and aquaporin activity cannot be interpreted independently. Suppose an endodermal tissue contains a relatively permeable extracellular route in a young region but a much more restrictive wall route in a mature region. If cellular pathways provide much of the remaining conductance in the mature region, changes in cellular water permeability can have a relatively large effect there. In the young region, water may redistribute between alternative paths when one membrane route is altered. The relative importance of each route is a property of the whole network under the specified driving conditions.

The living root may also have internal water storage. When water uptake from the soil temporarily falls below loss from the shoot, cells can release stored water as their pressure and volume change. Conversely, after an increase in soil water availability, part of the incoming water may refill tissues before the corresponding change appears in the xylem outflow. A transient mismatch between inflow and outflow does not violate conservation of water; it reflects the changing amount stored within the plant. At steady state with negligible changes in storage, inflow and outflow must approximately balance at the scale chosen for the analysis.

To see the significance of this distinction, imagine a measurement lasting ten minutes just after a root has been rehydrated. The uptake rate at the surface might exceed the volume collected from a cut xylem end because part of the incoming water increases tissue hydration. During a later interval, when storage changes become negligible, the rates may approach one another. Measurements under transient conditions can be valuable for understanding tissue elasticity and water storage, but should not automatically be used as a steady-state conductance estimate.

The connection between cell turgor and water potential also matters. A living root cell with solute potential −0.75 MPa and pressure potential +0.35 MPa has an approximate total water potential of −0.40 MPa if other terms are negligible. If the surrounding wall solution has water potential −0.25 MPa, water tends to enter. Entry may raise turgor and dilute intracellular solutes, reducing the original difference. If the wall solution changes to −0.60 MPa, water tends to leave and turgor can fall. This is ordinary water-potential equilibration, not active adjustment by an aquaporin. Regulation of solute content may subsequently change the cell's water potential, but it is a separate process.

A larger aquaporin conductance can accelerate the approach toward equilibrium without changing the equilibrium condition if all other variables remain fixed. For example, two isolated cells with identical initial solute contents and identical wall mechanical properties may equilibrate with the same external solution at different rates if their membrane permeabilities differ. Once their water potentials equal the surrounding solution, neither should maintain a net water flux solely because it has more open aquaporins. This distinction between rate and equilibrium is especially useful in questions that ask about the immediate and final effects of altered water channel activity.

10. Delivery into the xylem and root pressure

When nutrients reach living cells of the stele they are not yet necessarily in the conducting xylem sap. Mature conducting xylem elements are extracellular compartments, so solutes carried through living cytoplasm must be released across a final membrane. Transport proteins in xylem-associated cells can mediate this export, while other proteins retrieve ions from the vascular apoplast. The net composition of xylem sap reflects influx, efflux, retrieval, metabolism and the volume of water flowing through it.

The concentration of an ion and the amount delivered per unit time are distinct quantities. If a xylem stream carries an ion at 3 mmol L⁻¹ and flow is 0.20 L h⁻¹, the transport rate is 0.60 mmol h⁻¹. A second condition with 2 mmol L⁻¹ but flow of 0.50 L h⁻¹ delivers 1.00 mmol h⁻¹. The second sap is more dilute yet carries a greater quantity per hour. Measurements of sap concentration must therefore be interpreted together with water flow when assessing vascular delivery.

At low transpiration, solute accumulation within the stele and xylem can lower local osmotic potential and promote inward water movement. If outward water loss is limited, incoming water can raise hydrostatic pressure in the vascular system. This positive pressure is known as root pressure. A cut stem may exude sap and some intact plants release liquid through hydathodes as guttation. Root pressure differs from transpiration-driven tension and cannot generally account for the ascent of water in tall transpiring plants. The next node considers axial xylem movement in detail.

The root's energy supply influences many of its transport activities. Mineral acquisition through proton-coupled carriers depends indirectly on ATP production, and living vascular parenchyma requires energy to maintain ionic gradients. Water itself is not normally transported by ATP-driven pumps. Metabolic inhibition can nevertheless reduce osmotic water entry by impairing solute accumulation, and can modify membrane water permeability. A reduced water uptake rate following oxygen deprivation therefore does not prove direct active pumping of water.

Xylem loading can be viewed as a membrane transfer process followed by entry into a physically connected extracellular conduit. A nutrient in the cytoplasm of a stelar cell is separated from the xylem lumen by the membrane of the living cell and associated wall space. Once the nutrient enters the lumen, longitudinal bulk movement can carry it toward the shoot. This last transfer may require an efflux transporter whose direction and energy requirements depend on the electrochemical gradient and the transported species. A protein involved in initial influx from soil is not necessarily the protein responsible for final vascular export.

Retrieval is the complementary process in which living cells remove a substance from xylem sap. It provides a means to regulate the composition of fluid that ultimately reaches the shoot. A root may limit delivery of a harmful ion by retaining or retrieving it, while allowing other ions to continue in the conducting stream. These processes require appropriate membrane transport activities in living cells associated with the vascular tissues. The mature conduits themselves do not actively select each ion as fluid flows along their lumina.

A mass balance makes the distinction explicit. Consider a hypothetical nutrient acquired from the soil at 10 micromoles per hour. If the root retains 3 micromoles per hour in storage and growing tissue, loses 1 micromole per hour back to the medium and otherwise remains in steady operation, the net amount available for xylem delivery is 6 micromoles per hour. A treatment could decrease delivery to 3 while leaving gross soil influx unchanged by increasing root retention or outward loss. Measuring only shoot nutrient accumulation would not establish which term in the balance had changed.

The root pressure phenomenon provides an example of coupling between solute transport and water movement. In a simplified system, if a selectively retained solute is accumulated in the vascular compartment, its concentration lowers the water potential of that compartment at a given pressure. Water enters from surrounding tissue and can generate a positive pressure if the volume of the compartment cannot expand indefinitely and the outflow is restricted. As pressure rises, the pressure contribution to water potential counteracts the solute contribution. Net inflow can diminish as the driving difference decreases.

For example, suppose the external water potential is −0.10 MPa, the xylem solute potential is −0.50 MPa and gravitational terms are negligible at the local scale. If the xylem pressure contribution initially equals zero, the simplified potential difference favours inward flow by 0.40 MPa. If the pressure rises to +0.40 MPa while the solute concentration remains fixed, the xylem water potential becomes −0.10 MPa. Under these assumptions the net driving difference becomes zero. Actual roots have leakage, changing concentrations and outflow, so the numerical case demonstrates the principle rather than predicts a universal root pressure.

Positive pressure can be demonstrated by exudation from a cut stem or pressure measurements under suitable conditions. Cutting the stem removes the normal transpiring shoot, however, and changes the system's boundary conditions. The measured exudation should not be interpreted as an unchanged representation of daytime flow in the intact plant. In an intact transpiring plant, xylem pressure may instead be negative relative to atmospheric pressure, and a different balance of driving terms operates. The existence of root pressure is compatible with the cohesion–tension explanation of sap ascent; it is not evidence that tension never occurs.

A practical question is whether higher nutrient concentration in root exudate indicates more effective loading. It may, but an alternative is reduced water dilution. In order to distinguish these possibilities, measure both the solute concentration and the volume collected over a specified period. If a treatment produces a twofold increase in concentration and a fourfold decrease in volume flow, total nutrient delivery has actually decreased by half. This elementary conservation principle applies to many experimental datasets involving vascular sap.

11. Experimental reasoning: distinguishing transport routes

The strongest experimental conclusions come from measurements that separate the variables of interest. A fluorescence microscopy experiment with a wall-accessible, membrane-excluded dye can reveal whether extracellular spaces are connected across a developing endodermis. Compare intact roots of equivalent age and inspect the distribution of the dye relative to the barrier. Include a tissue viability check, because damaged membranes may admit dye directly into living cells and complicate interpretation. If a mutant displays fluorescence within the stele while an intact control does not, the result supports increased accessibility of the vascular apoplast to that tracer.

A radioactive or stable isotope tracer can help identify mineral acquisition and redistribution. A short exposure to labelled nitrate followed by washing and measurement can indicate acquisition of label by the root. However, wall adsorption, extracellular solution trapped in tissues and metabolically converted label complicate interpretation. A measurement of total radioactivity cannot by itself distinguish nitrate from an amino acid containing nitrogen originally supplied as nitrate. Chemical separation or ion-specific measurements may be needed for that distinction.

To investigate hydraulic properties, measure flow under a controlled pressure or osmotic gradient and compare the slope of the flux response. A single flux value is ambiguous if the driving difference changes between samples. A root pressure probe or suitable pressure-based measurement can help separate the hydraulic response from solute transport. Compare specimens at matched temperature, root area and developmental stage. Changes in temperature affect water viscosity and biological activity, so they must not be ignored when comparing conductivities.

Consider four hypothetical plants supplied with identical labelled nitrate. A wild type accumulates root nitrate and delivers it to the shoot. A nitrate-influx mutant shows very low label entry. A nitrate-reductase mutant may accumulate nitrate while showing reduced incorporation of label into organic nitrogen. A defective xylem-loading mutant may acquire label normally but deliver less to the shoot. These patterns are predictions under simplified assumptions, not universal phenotypes. Compensatory transporters, nitrate storage and feedback can modify each result.

To interpret an unfamiliar dataset, identify the measured compartment first. Distinguish nutrient arrival at the surface, crossing of the first plasma membrane, transfer across the cortex, movement into vascular sap and metabolism after acquisition. Then ask which step the intervention targets. A mutation that reduces shoot ion content is not automatically a mutation in an epidermal influx carrier; it might alter retention, loading or transpiration. Experiments designed around this sequence can narrow the possible explanations.

A useful experimental approach is to compare a barrier mutant, a membrane transport mutant and a wild type under matched conditions using complementary readouts. First establish that the roots are viable and developmentally comparable. Measure the spatial distribution of a suitable extracellular tracer, the net uptake of a selected nutrient from solution, and the nutrient concentration and flow in collected xylem sap. Each readout answers a different question. The tracer concerns access through the wall compartment, nutrient removal concerns net acquisition and the vascular measurement concerns delivery.

For a mutant that shows excessive stele tracer penetration but relatively normal initial nutrient removal, a defect in extracellular restriction is plausible. For a mutant that excludes the tracer normally but shows reduced nutrient entry, a defect in selective uptake is more plausible. A third mutant may show normal nutrient removal with reduced vascular delivery, indicating that a later step deserves investigation. These distinctions are not logically exclusive because a gene can affect several tissues or induce compensatory responses. They help construct hypotheses rather than prove a molecular identity.

If the nutrient removal measurement is performed at several external concentrations, a plot of net uptake rate against concentration can reveal whether a saturable component is consistent with the data. At a single concentration, a change in rate may be compatible with a change in apparent affinity, maximum capacity or both. At high enough concentration, a simple saturable component approaches a maximum rate; at low concentration, its initial slope reflects the ratio Vmax/Km. Measurements across a concentration range allow more informative comparisons. Actual root data may require more than one transport component, and a fitted apparent Km should not automatically be interpreted as the binding constant of one purified protein.

Consider illustrative data for two genotypes. At external nutrient concentrations of 0.1, 1 and 10 mM, genotype A has net uptake rates of 1, 5 and 9 arbitrary units. Genotype B has rates of 0.2, 2 and 8. The increasing rates for B suggest that some uptake capacity remains. The relatively large difference at low concentration and smaller difference at high concentration are consistent with a defect affecting high-affinity acquisition or another low-concentration process. They do not demonstrate that exactly one named transporter has been lost. Additional experiments on expression, membrane localization or genetic complementation would be required to support that conclusion.

Quantifying root-to-shoot movement requires attention to time and tissue mass. If labelled nutrient accumulates more slowly in the shoot of a mutant, this may reflect slower acquisition, stronger retention in the root, reduced xylem flow or greater shoot dilution by growth. The percentage of recovered label in each organ can be compared with total recovery, while concentration per unit mass should be reported separately from total content. A plant with half the shoot mass may exhibit a higher tissue concentration despite a lower total quantity of nutrient. These distinctions are especially important in Part C questions that provide several types of measurements.

A convincing interpretation often combines a perturbation and a rescue. For instance, a mutant defective in a candidate transport protein can be compared with a line in which the functional gene is restored. Restoration of the transport phenotype supports a causal role for the gene, although the precise transport mechanism may still require direct physiological measurements. A chemical inhibitor can complement genetic evidence, but an inhibitor alone may have multiple targets. Independent evidence that points toward the same step provides a more robust explanation than several repetitions of one nonspecific assay.

A classic problem in the study of mineral uptake is separating absorption from adsorption. Absorption in this context refers to transfer into living root tissue, whereas adsorption refers to association with accessible surfaces such as cell walls or soil particles. A charged tracer may attach to extracellular material. If the root is simply removed from the solution and its total tracer content measured, the result may include material that never crossed a membrane. Suitable washing and controls are therefore essential, although a washing treatment must itself be chosen carefully because it may also cause loss of material that genuinely entered the tissue.

A comparison with nonliving tissue can help illustrate a surface-binding contribution, but it is not a perfect control. Killing cells changes membrane integrity and may alter binding properties of the walls. Similarly, low temperature can slow metabolic transport while changing membrane fluidity, water viscosity and diffusion. These treatments are useful as parts of a broader design but should not be treated as proof of a particular active uptake mechanism on their own. The strongest evidence for a membrane transport process combines appropriate physiological measurements with the expected substrate specificity, dependence on driving gradients and, where possible, genetic information.

A tracer pulse followed by a chase provides a way to investigate redistribution over time. During the pulse, a plant is exposed to a labelled nutrient; during the chase, the label supply is removed or replaced by unlabelled nutrient. Measurements at successive times can reveal how the label redistributes between the root and shoot or between soluble and metabolically incorporated pools. A pulse showing rapid root labelling followed by delayed shoot labelling is consistent with sequential acquisition and delivery, but the delay is not necessarily the transit time of every individual molecule. Retention, storage and exchange among compartments can also produce delays.

For a hypothetical pulse-chase experiment, suppose a plant has recovered label of 100 units after washing. At the end of the pulse, 90 units are in roots and 10 in shoots. After a chase, 55 remain in roots, 35 occur in shoots and 10 have been recovered in the external medium. Under this simplified complete balance, 25 units have accumulated additionally in the shoot and 10 have been lost to the external medium; total recovered label remains 100. If the root label alone had been measured, its decline of 35 might mistakenly be described as movement of 35 units to the shoot. The separate external measurement shows that this inference would overestimate internal delivery.

In a barrier-tracer experiment, spatial resolution is as important as total signal. Fluorescence outside the endodermis, inside the wall spaces of the stele and within living cytoplasm have different meanings. A thick optical section can superimpose signals from different depths and obscure the precise compartment. Appropriate imaging and anatomical markers help assign signal correctly. The absence of visible fluorescence also has a detection limit. It means the amount present is below the detection threshold under the conditions used, not necessarily that no molecules entered at all.

When a research result is summarized for an examination, the statement must match the actual variable tested. The experimental finding that early Arabidopsis Casparian strips are lignin-based addresses the chemical identity of a specific barrier. It does not show that suberin is unimportant for all root transport. The finding that passage-cell numbers respond to nutrient status addresses developmental plasticity. It does not quantify all water and nutrient flux through those cells. The composite transport model addresses how multiple physical pathways contribute to root hydraulics, but the relative numerical contributions can differ with conditions. Keeping these distinctions clear allows a student to use landmark results accurately without turning them into unsupported universal rules.

A rigorous problem-solving approach is to ask four questions whenever a root-uptake experiment is presented. First, what substance or property is being measured? Second, in which compartment and over which time interval? Third, which physical gradient or biological process is being changed? Fourth, what alternative processes could produce a similar observation? For example, reduced transpiration can change xylem delivery without a change in membrane uptake, reduced root area can lower total uptake without lower uptake per unit area, and increased backflow can lower net accumulation without lower gross influx. A conclusion that explicitly rules out these alternatives is more defensible than one based on a single familiar keyword.

12. Misconceptions and examination traps

Wet soil guarantees uptake
Water potential and hydraulic connection, not water content alone, determine availability. Salts and matric binding can lower potential. Exam reasoning: Compare total water potential rather than moisture percentages.
All nutrient supply is active transport
Mass flow and diffusion deliver solutes outside the root, whereas active transport refers to energetically uphill membrane transfer. Exam reasoning: Identify whether the process is in soil or across a membrane.
Root hairs are miniature lateral roots
A root hair is an extension of an epidermal cell; a lateral root is a multicellular organ with vascular connections. Exam reasoning: Examine the developmental origin rather than the shape.
The apoplast is only the cell wall
It includes extracellular spaces and mature xylem lumina. Exam reasoning: Locate the plasma membrane boundary.
The symplast includes the vacuole
The cytoplasm is interconnected by plasmodesmata; vacuoles are separated by tonoplasts. Exam reasoning: Distinguish cytosolic continuity from all intracellular spaces.
Every symplastic step crosses a membrane
Entry into the symplast requires a membrane crossing, but passage through plasmodesmata does not require repeated apoplast-to-cytosol crossing. Exam reasoning: Count actual membrane interfaces along the route.
The primary Arabidopsis strip is suberin
Its early functional barrier is lignin-based; suberin lamellae are distinct later modifications. Exam reasoning: Distinguish the localization and timing of the two polymers.
The strip blocks all water uptake
It restricts extracellular bypass; water can move through cellular paths. Exam reasoning: A barrier to a dye is not necessarily an absolute barrier to water.
Ions must first enter the endodermal cell
Ions may enter epidermal or cortical cells and reach the stele through the connected symplast. Exam reasoning: Identify where a membrane crossing occurs before vascular entry.
Passage cells lack all endodermal barriers
Unsuberized cells can retain a functional Casparian strip. Exam reasoning: Separate membrane access from wall continuity.
Aquaporins pump water uphill
They facilitate passive water movement driven by water potential differences. Exam reasoning: Compute the gradient independently of channel permeability.
A negative cell interior drives every ion inward
Electrical attraction favours cations but opposes anion entry; chemical gradients also contribute. Exam reasoning: Use the signed charge in the electrochemical equation.
All nitrate uptake is nitrate assimilation
Uptake transfers nitrate across membranes, while assimilation converts inorganic nitrogen to organic forms. Exam reasoning: Identify measured nitrate versus organic labelled nitrogen.
Higher xylem concentration means more delivery
Delivery rate equals concentration multiplied by volumetric flow. Exam reasoning: Compare quantities per unit time, not concentration alone.
Root pressure explains all sap ascent
Root pressure is a positive-pressure phenomenon that cannot explain typical daytime ascent in tall plants. Exam reasoning: Distinguish low-transpiration osmotic uptake from transpiration-driven tension.

13. Original examination practice

1. Part B

Which compartment comprises hydrated cell walls?

Answer and explanation

Apoplast — Cell walls lie outside the plasma membrane. Cytoplasmic continuity via plasmodesmata instead defines the symplast.

2. Part B

What is the principal polymer of the early Arabidopsis Casparian strip?

Answer and explanation

Lignin — Experimental evidence separates lignin deposition in the strip from subsequent suberin lamellae.

3. Part B

Do aquaporins establish water potential gradients?

Answer and explanation

No — They facilitate water permeation in response to existing gradients; solute and pressure changes establish the driving force.

4. Part B

Which cell layer surrounds the stele as the innermost cortex?

Answer and explanation

Endodermis — Its anatomical position enables it to restrict extracellular exchange between the cortex and vascular cylinder.

5. Part B

Which process primarily energizes proton-coupled nitrate uptake?

Answer and explanation

Plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase — ATP hydrolysis drives proton export. Proton re-entry can then be coupled to nitrate influx by a secondary transporter.

6. Part C

A root mutant admits a membrane-impermeant dye into the stele but has normal cell viability. Which property is directly implicated?

Answer and explanation

Extracellular barrier integrity — The tracer reports accessibility of the wall compartment. The experiment does not directly quantify aquaporin permeability or nitrate reductase activity.

7. Part C

Soil Ψ is −0.25 MPa and xylem Ψ is −0.65 MPa. If hydraulic conductivity is halved at fixed potentials, what happens to flux?

Answer and explanation

It halves — The potential difference stays 0.40 MPa. Under the linear relation Jv = LpΔΨ, only the conductivity changes.

8. Part C

Root nitrate is high but labelled organic nitrogen is low after nitrate exposure. Is a complete failure of nitrate uptake the best explanation?

Answer and explanation

No; impaired assimilation is more consistent — The nitrate pool indicates entry has occurred. Deficient reduction or downstream assimilation could explain low incorporation; additional measurements distinguish these possibilities.

9. Part C

Plant A exports 4 mM nitrate at 0.1 L/h and plant B exports 2 mM at 0.3 L/h. Which exports more nitrate each hour?

Answer and explanation

Plant B — A transports 0.4 mmol/h and B transports 0.6 mmol/h. Concentration alone is insufficient.

10. Part C

A passage cell lacks a suberin lamella. Must an extracellular dye cross the endodermal barrier at that site?

Answer and explanation

No — A functional Casparian strip may still block extracellular continuity; reduced suberin can allow membrane access without an open wall bypass.

11. Part B

What term describes movement from bulk soil toward roots with flowing water?

Answer and explanation

Mass flow — It is advective delivery of solute in moving liquid, not transmembrane uptake.

12. Part B

What property generally falls when soil pores become disconnected during drying?

Answer and explanation

Soil hydraulic conductivity — Reduced connectivity limits water delivery even if roots maintain a favourable potential gradient.

13. Part B

What cell boundary separates the vacuole from cytosol?

Answer and explanation

Tonoplast — The tonoplast controls exchange with the vacuolar compartment.

14. Part B

Which connections provide cytoplasmic continuity between adjacent living plant cells?

Answer and explanation

Plasmodesmata — They provide regulated channels between cells rather than an extracellular wall pathway.

15. Part B

What is an exodermis?

Answer and explanation

An outer cortical barrier in some roots — It can add resistance to radial exchange; not all species develop an equivalent barrier.

16. Part B

Does a functioning proton symporter directly hydrolyse ATP?

Answer and explanation

Not necessarily; secondary transport uses an electrochemical gradient — Primary ATPases maintain the gradient indirectly.

17. Part B

In a simple saturable uptake model, what is the rate at substrate concentration Km?

Answer and explanation

Half Vmax — Substitution gives Vmax Km/(Km+Km).

18. Part B

What is the vascular compartment immediately receiving water from root living cells?

Answer and explanation

Xylem apoplast — Mature conducting elements have extracellular lumina.

19. Part B

Which is the correct unit for area-normalized water flux?

Answer and explanation

m/s — Volume divided by area and time reduces to length per time.

20. Part B

Which mineral commonly forms a strong soil depletion zone owing to limited mobility?

Answer and explanation

Phosphate — Slow replenishment of dissolved phosphate can limit delivery near roots.

21. Part C

A root has Ψ = −0.4 MPa. Soil matric and solute potentials are −0.15 and −0.35 MPa, with other terms negligible. What is the predicted direction?

Answer and explanation

Out of the root — Soil Ψ is −0.50 MPa, more negative than root Ψ; the simplified gradient favours outward movement.

22. Part C

A soil delivers 5 micromoles/hour to a root whose uptake capacity is 20 micromoles/hour. What is the maximum sustained uptake under the assumed supply limitation?

Answer and explanation

5 micromoles/hour — Supply is limiting; transport capacity cannot create external substrate.

23. Part C

A root initially has influx 10 and efflux 3 units/hour. Efflux rises to 8 while influx stays fixed. How does net uptake change?

Answer and explanation

From 7 to 2 units/hour — Net uptake is influx minus efflux; reduced net uptake need not indicate reduced influx.

24. Part C

A line with twice the effective root area has the same total hydraulic conductance as control. How does its normalized conductivity compare?

Answer and explanation

Half the control, under equal definitions — Conductivity equals total conductance divided by reference area.

25. Part C

An ion has z = −1 and moves inward across −100 mV. What is the electrical free-energy contribution per mole?

Answer and explanation

Approximately +9.65 kJ/mol — Multiply −1 by 96.485 kJ/V/mol and −0.100 V. Inward movement is electrically unfavourable.

26. Part C

A mutant has normal tracer exclusion and normal root label accumulation but reduced shoot label delivery. Which later step warrants investigation?

Answer and explanation

Xylem loading or vascular flow — Initial acquisition and barrier exclusion appear intact in the reported assays.

27. Part C

A treatment reduces root conductivity but leaves cell viability uncertain. Does this prove aquaporin inhibition?

Answer and explanation

No — Membrane injury, metabolism and changes in other routes can produce similar organ-level outcomes.

28. Part C

If osmotic pressure inside exceeds outside by 0.30 MPa and the model reflection coefficient is 0.2, what is the inward osmotic driving contribution at equal pressure?

Answer and explanation

0.06 MPa — Multiply 0.30 by 0.2 in the simplified membrane equation.

29. Part C

A mutant shows a strong phosphate uptake defect in soil but little defect in well-mixed solution. Suggest a testable explanation.

Answer and explanation

Reduced exploration of soil or effective root hair access — Mixing reduces external delivery limitations; examine architecture and surface acquisition directly.

30. Part C

Root A has total conductance 5 units and B has 10. If the driving gradient of A is twice B's, which has greater steady flow?

Answer and explanation

Equal flow in the linear model — Flow is conductance times gradient, so 5 × 2 equals 10 × 1.
