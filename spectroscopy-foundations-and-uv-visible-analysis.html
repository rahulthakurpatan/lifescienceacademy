1. From a biological question to an optical measurement

A purified solution may contain millions of molecules that cannot be resolved individually with an ordinary microscope. Yet collectively these molecules can alter the passage of a beam of light. A solution of a nucleotide can appear completely transparent to the human eye while absorbing ultraviolet radiation strongly. A solution containing a respiratory coenzyme can be almost colourless and still provide a sensitive measure of the activity of an enzyme. Spectroscopy relates the optical signal to matter; biological interpretation determines whether that inferred property answers the experimental question.

Imagine comparing two clear tubes containing a purified enzyme. Both have the same volume, but one may contain more protein than the other. We cannot infer the amount from their visible appearance. If the enzyme contains aromatic amino acid residues, the two solutions may attenuate radiation at 280 nanometres by different amounts. When both solutions contain the same chemical form of the enzyme, have identical optical path lengths, and satisfy the assumptions of an absorption measurement, the difference can be translated into a concentration difference. The usefulness of the method depends on those conditions. If one solution instead contains a small amount of nucleic acid, which also absorbs ultraviolet light, the simple interpretation may fail. A detector cannot tell us which molecule removed a particular photon merely by recording the loss.

Spectroscopy studies interactions between radiation and matter as a function of a suitable spectral variable, such as wavelength or frequency. Spectrometry is associated with the quantitative measurement of a spectrum or other spectral property. These words overlap in normal laboratory usage. A spectrum is an ordered representation of a measured quantity against wavelength, frequency, energy or another appropriate variable. In UV–visible absorption spectroscopy the quantity is usually absorbance and the horizontal axis is wavelength. A spectrophotometer measures transmitted radiant power relative to an appropriate reference so that absorbance can be calculated. A single reading at a selected wavelength is useful for concentration measurements, whereas a wavelength scan reveals changes in the distribution of optical absorption.

The distinction between an observation and its explanation becomes especially important in cell and molecular biology. An increase in absorbance can indicate an increase in concentration, but it might also result from a change in the chemical form of an existing molecule, particle formation, a change in path length, or instrumental error. Conversely, failure to observe a spectral change does not establish the absence of a biological reaction. The reaction may transform molecules whose absorptions are indistinguishable at the selected wavelength. In that situation a different wavelength, an indirect assay, or an independent method is needed. Selection of the measurement is part of experimental design rather than a routine step that can be ignored.

The simplest useful experiment has a source of radiation, a reference representing the optical background, a sample with a well-defined optical path, and a detector. The reference establishes what reaches the detector without the analyte of interest. Comparison with the sample then measures the additional attenuation. A valid inference requires consideration of how the radiation was selected, how the sample was prepared, whether the background remains stable and what physical mechanism causes the signal. At this stage there is no need to know the identity of every molecule in the solution, but quantitative analysis of a particular component does require appropriate selectivity.

A major attraction of UV–visible analysis is that it is comparatively rapid and can be performed on small amounts of material. Repeated measurements may allow a time course without separating reaction products at each time point. However, the method is not intrinsically non-destructive under every condition. Radiation can induce photochemical changes in photosensitive samples; temperature changes or evaporation during prolonged measurements can alter a solution. A method that uses little material is not automatically a method that leaves the material unchanged. These possibilities become important when a researcher interprets changes during a scan or a kinetic experiment.

The physical origin of selective absorption, the mathematics of attenuation and the distinction between molecular signals and interference form the foundation of quantitative spectroscopy.

2. Electromagnetic radiation, wavelength and photon energy

Light is electromagnetic radiation. In the classical description, oscillating electric and magnetic fields propagate through space and transport energy. In a plane wave travelling through a vacuum, the electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation. This description explains interference, polarization and propagation. In a complementary quantum description, radiation exchanges energy with matter in discrete quanta called photons. Both descriptions are necessary: wavelengths characterize propagation, whereas photon energies help explain electronic absorption.

Wavelength, denoted by λ, is the spatial separation of successive points in the same phase of a periodic wave. Frequency, ν, is the number of oscillations per second. Frequency is measured in hertz, equivalent to inverse seconds. In vacuum the product of wavelength and frequency is the speed of light, c = 2.99792458 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. If a wave has a shorter vacuum wavelength, its frequency is higher. In a transparent medium its speed and wavelength generally change but its frequency remains fixed at a stationary interface. Spectroscopic wavelength settings conventionally refer to wavelengths in vacuum or air according to the instrument specification, rather than the shorter wavelength that exists inside a liquid.

The Planck relationship E = hν describes the energy of a photon, where h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant. Combining this equation with c = λν gives E = hc/λ for vacuum wavelength. A single photon of ultraviolet radiation carries more energy than a single photon of visible radiation at a longer wavelength. This does not mean that every ultraviolet beam delivers more total energy than every visible beam. The power of a beam also depends on the number of photons arriving per unit time. Two sources emitting equal radiant powers at different wavelengths emit unequal numbers of photons each second.

A nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m. Radiation of vacuum wavelength 260 nm has a wavelength of 2.60 × 10⁻⁷ m. Dividing c by that value gives a frequency of approximately 1.153 × 10¹⁵ Hz. The corresponding photon energy is about 7.64 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, or 4.77 eV using 1 eV = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Multiplication by the Avogadro constant gives approximately 460 kJ per mole of photons. The molar value is not the thermal energy of a mole of molecules in the solution. It represents the energy delivered if one mole of photons of this wavelength is supplied. A molecule may relax by several pathways after acquiring such energy.

At 520 nm, twice the wavelength, a photon has half the energy. A 1 mW monochromatic beam at 520 nm consequently contains twice as many photons per second as a 1 mW beam at 260 nm. The result matters when comparing photochemical or photobiological responses at different wavelengths. Equal radiant power does not provide equal incident photon flux. For an ordinary absorption measurement using a reference at each wavelength, the ratio of powers at the same wavelength is usually sufficient, because the incident and transmitted photons have the same individual energy.

The electromagnetic spectrum extends from radio waves through microwaves, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. Its divisions are conventional ranges rather than sharp boundaries dictated by nature. Routine solution UV–visible spectrophotometry commonly covers approximately 190–800 nm, although the working range depends on the source, optical components, detector and solvent. The visible region is roughly 380–780 nm depending on the definition used. The ultraviolet region lies at shorter wavelengths than visible light. Very short ultraviolet wavelengths are challenging in ordinary aqueous measurements because the solvent, air and optical materials may absorb strongly.

Different regions tend to probe different forms of molecular energy. Radiofrequency excitation in a magnetic field is used in nuclear magnetic resonance; infrared frequencies commonly probe molecular vibrations; ultraviolet and visible photons often excite electrons between molecular electronic states. These are useful tendencies, not exclusive rules. An electronic transition is accompanied by changes in vibrational and sometimes rotational states, and a molecule may possess more than one accessible transition. This explains why the spectrum of a complex dissolved molecule is usually broader than a single idealized electronic line.

Radiant power is energy transferred per unit time, measured in watts. Irradiance is radiant power incident per unit area, measured in W m⁻². These quantities should not be confused with photon flux, the number of photons per unit time, or photon flux density, the number per area per time. Textbooks often denote the beam intensity entering and leaving a sample by I₀ and I, respectively. In a spectrophotometer it is more precise to speak of spectral radiant powers entering the ratio at a selected wavelength, provided geometry and collection efficiency are controlled. We will use P₀ and P to avoid confusing irradiance with total power.

A wave of a single exact frequency is an idealization called monochromatic radiation. Practical spectrophotometers transmit finite wavelength intervals. A nominal wavelength of 260 nm therefore usually represents a narrow band centered near 260 nm. The width of that band matters when the absorption changes sharply over the interval. When the interval is very narrow compared with the variation in the molecular spectrum, the measurement approximates the single-wavelength theory. When the interval is broad, contributions from different wavelengths are mixed and the result need not obey a simple linear concentration relation.

Polarization describes the orientation of the electric field oscillation. For ordinary isotropic solutions, molecules generally explore many orientations, and absorption measurements average over their interactions with the electric field. The orientation and polarization dependence of absorption become important in ordered materials and in circular dichroism. The present chapter concerns ordinary UV–visible transmission measurements and uses an isotropic solution as the starting model. It is not necessary to treat a dissolved protein as an aligned crystal.

The relationship between radiation and molecular structure is reciprocal in the sense that known structures can explain spectral behaviour, while spectral behaviour can constrain models of structure. But constraints are not complete structures. Many molecules have overlapping absorption bands, and a given wavelength does not uniquely encode a molecular identity. The power of spectroscopy comes from connecting its signal with a specified hypothesis and combining it with complementary evidence when the optical observation alone cannot identify the cause.

3. Electronic absorption, chromophores and the origins of spectra

A molecule has electrons arranged in allowed quantum states. The energies of these states depend on the nuclei, electron distribution, chemical bonds and surroundings of the molecule. When radiation interacts with the molecule, a photon may be absorbed if the photon energy matches the difference between an occupied initial state and an accessible higher-energy state, subject to quantum-mechanical transition rules. The molecule is then in an excited state. Absorption in the ultraviolet and visible regions often corresponds to transitions involving valence electrons, rather than removal of electrons from the molecule.

The probability of a transition is not determined by the energy difference alone. It also depends on how strongly the radiation couples to the change in charge distribution between the states. Electric-dipole selection rules help explain why some transitions are intense whereas others are weak. For exam reasoning, a sufficient starting point is that an energetically possible transition may have a small probability and therefore a small absorption coefficient. A spectrum encodes both the energies and relative probabilities of transitions. One cannot simply count peaks to count all occupied molecular orbitals.

Chemical bonding offers a useful language for these transitions. A σ bond has electron density associated with overlap along a bond axis, whereas a π bond involves lateral overlap of orbitals, often above and below the plane of bonding atoms. Molecules may also contain nonbonding electron pairs, denoted n. In a simple molecular-orbital picture, an asterisk denotes an antibonding orbital. Transitions from π to π* and n to π* are common in molecules that absorb in the ultraviolet or visible regions. Transitions from σ to σ* often require higher energies and occur in the far ultraviolet. These assignments are models that organize chemical intuition; actual electronic excited states can involve more than one orbital configuration.

Consider an isolated double bond and a chain of alternating single and double bonds. The latter has a conjugated π system in which electrons are delocalized across several atoms. Increasing conjugation often reduces the energy separation of relevant electronic states, allowing absorption at longer wavelength. The tendency explains why extended conjugated biological pigments can absorb visible radiation whereas many small organic molecules absorb mainly in the ultraviolet. It is not a numerical rule that predicts a wavelength from the number of double bonds alone. Geometry, substitution, solvent and the identity of the transition can modify the outcome.

A chromophore is a portion of a molecule responsible for a characteristic absorption band. Examples include the aromatic rings of tryptophan, nucleotide bases, porphyrin systems in heme and the conjugated systems of carotenoids. A chromophore need not be a separate molecule; it can be covalently integrated into a large macromolecule. A protein may acquire a distinctive visible absorption band by binding a chromophore as a cofactor. The protein's surrounding residues can influence the absorption of that cofactor by changing its electrostatic environment, orientation or protonation state. Consequently a free cofactor and the same cofactor bound within a protein need not give identical spectra.

An auxochrome is a substituent that modifies absorption of a chromophore through electronic or environmental effects. The older term is often useful when discussing organic dyes. An ionizable group can change the electron distribution of a conjugated system after protonation or deprotonation. In a pH indicator, the protonated and deprotonated species have different relative populations and can have distinguishable absorption bands. The observed spectrum therefore reflects a weighted contribution from each population, which is one reason absorption measurements can report chemical equilibrium.

There are four common descriptive terms for spectral changes. A bathochromic shift, sometimes called a red shift, is a movement of an absorption band to longer wavelength. A hypsochromic or blue shift is movement to shorter wavelength. Hyperchromicity is an increase in absorption intensity under defined comparable conditions, and hypochromicity is a decrease. A red shift is not necessarily an increase in absorption at every wavelength. A band that moves to the red may decrease at its old peak and increase on the longer wavelength side. Likewise an increase in peak height does not necessarily indicate more molecules if molecular absorption coefficients have changed.

At ordinary temperatures, solution spectra often show broad absorption bands. An electronic transition is accompanied by a distribution of vibrational transitions, and collisions and interactions with solvent molecules alter energies. The observed shape can therefore result from contributions of many closely spaced transitions and molecular environments. A band maximum is designated λmax. Measuring at λmax is often sensitive because the absorption coefficient is large there. A broad maximum also makes a small wavelength-setting error less consequential than measurement on a steep slope. Nevertheless, the most suitable analytical wavelength may lie elsewhere if an interfering substance absorbs strongly at the maximum.

The fate of absorbed energy must be distinguished from the absorption event itself. An excited molecule can lose energy through nonradiative processes, emit light, transfer energy to another molecule, or participate in a photochemical reaction. UV–visible absorption measures the disappearance of radiation from the incident spectral beam under defined conditions. Fluorescence instead measures emitted photons from certain excited states. A molecule may absorb strongly and emit little fluorescence if most excited molecules relax without radiative emission. The two measurements answer different questions about molecular behaviour.

Biological absorption spectra are strongly influenced by chemical state. Binding of oxygen to heme in hemoglobin changes the electronic structure of the heme environment, producing distinct absorption spectra for oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin. The difference underlies methods that compare signals at more than one wavelength to estimate relative populations under controlled models. However, a complex tissue measurement involves light scattering and additional absorbing species, making it different from transmission through a homogeneous dilute solution. The simple example establishes the molecular principle without assuming that the laboratory equation can be applied unchanged to living tissue.

For nucleic acids, interactions among stacked bases modify the absorption of the bases relative to their isolated forms. Heating double-stranded DNA sufficiently to disrupt stacking and base pairing can produce an increase in ultraviolet absorbance near 260 nm, called the hyperchromic effect. No increase in the number of nucleotide residues is required. The same total mass can produce a larger absorbance because its optical response per residue changes. A measurement of DNA concentration that assumes a particular molecular state must take this dependence into account.

A final distinction is between absorption and an action spectrum. An absorption spectrum reports the optical response of a sample as a function of wavelength. An action spectrum reports the efficiency or magnitude of a particular biological or photochemical outcome as a function of wavelength under specified illumination conditions. Similarity between an action spectrum and a pigment's absorption spectrum can suggest the pigment participates in the response. It is not proof of unique causal identity, because multiple absorbers may have overlapping spectra and downstream processes can modify the relationship between absorbed photons and biological output.

4. Transmittance, absorbance and derivation of the Beer–Lambert relationship

An optical experiment begins with the fraction of incident radiation that reaches the detector. Let P₀ represent the reference radiant power at a selected wavelength, and let P represent the transmitted radiant power for the sample after appropriate background handling. The transmittance T = P/P₀ is dimensionless. Percent transmittance is 100T. Thus a sample that transmits one quarter of the reference power has T = 0.25 and percent transmittance of 25. These quantities describe passage of radiation rather than the concentration of the sample.

Absorbance A is defined as −log₁₀T, equivalently log₁₀(P₀/P). It is a dimensionless logarithmic quantity. The historical phrase optical density is commonly used for absorbance readings, but a measured optical density can include effects of scattering as well as absorption. In a homogeneous non-scattering solution with corrected backgrounds, the reading may be interpreted as molecular absorbance. In a cell suspension the same calculation may instead describe attenuation by several mechanisms.

When T = 1, the sample and reference transmit equal powers and A = 0. When T = 0.1, A = 1. If T = 0.01, A = 2. An absorbance of one is not equal to 100% absorption: it corresponds to 90% removal of the reference beam, assuming other losses are absent. Because the relationship is logarithmic, each increase of one absorbance unit corresponds to a tenfold decrease in transmittance. There is no finite absorbance corresponding to exactly zero transmitted power in the ideal mathematical definition. Instruments have finite detection limits and can report only a limited range of values.

Why is a logarithm useful? Imagine a homogeneous solution in a long optical cell divided into very thin layers. Within each layer the number of absorbers per unit volume is proportional to their concentration c. For a sufficiently thin layer dx, the small fraction of the incoming beam that is removed by molecular absorption is proportional to c dx. Thus the absolute loss of radiant power in that layer is proportional to the radiant power reaching it, not to the original incident power at the front of the cell. Writing k for the proportionality constant under fixed conditions, dP = −kcP dx. The negative sign indicates decreasing power along the direction of propagation.

Divide by P to obtain dP/P = −kc dx. Integration from the entrance, where x = 0 and P = P₀, to the exit, where x = l and P = P(l), yields ln(P/P₀) = −kcl. Therefore T = exp(−kcl). Taking the negative common logarithm gives A = (k/ln 10)cl. Define the molar decadic absorption coefficient ε = k/ln 10 when concentration and distance are expressed in the specified units. The resulting relation is A = εcl. The distinction between natural and common logarithms is important: a Napierian attenuation coefficient and a decadic coefficient differ by a factor of ln 10, approximately 2.303, if all other units are identical.

The derivation shows that transmitted power decreases exponentially along a path even though absorbance increases linearly with path length. Suppose equal successive layers each transmit 50% of the light reaching them. The first transmits 0.5P₀; the second transmits 0.25P₀; the third transmits 0.125P₀. The losses expressed as percentages of the original incident beam are 50%, 25% and 12.5%, respectively. But each layer contributes log₁₀2, approximately 0.301, to the total absorbance. Three layers give A = 0.903. The logarithm converts multiplication of successive transmitted fractions into addition of their absorbances.

Historically, Bouguer (1729) and Lambert (1760) investigated attenuation with distance. Beer (1852) studied coloured solutions, accounting for reflection and comparing transmission at different concentrations and cell thicknesses. The historical analysis distinguishes these early results from the later unified Bouguer–Beer–Lambert expression. Historical development matters because the two variables, concentration and path length, are experimentally distinguishable. Changing the length of the optical path tests the accumulation of attenuation along the beam, while dilution tests the dependence on the number of absorbers per volume and whether dilution preserves their chemical identity.

In the conventional biochemical form, c is measured in mol L⁻¹, l in centimetres, and ε in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, also written M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Multiplication cancels concentration and distance units so that A has no physical unit. A mass absorption coefficient may instead relate absorbance to concentration in g L⁻¹, with units L g⁻¹ cm⁻¹. The numerical value of a mass coefficient is generally not the same as a molar coefficient. Converting between them requires the molar mass. A protein concentration stated as milligrams per millilitre has the same numerical value as grams per litre, a convenient conversion when using mass-based coefficients.

A typical laboratory optical cell has a path length of 1 cm, but shorter cells are useful for highly absorbing samples. Microvolume instruments may use much shorter effective path lengths and report absorbance normalized to a reference path of 1 cm. The actual geometric length, and whether normalization has already been applied, must be known before entering data into the Beer–Lambert equation. Applying a path-length correction twice introduces a systematic error. A microplate well, in contrast, generally has a liquid height dependent on its volume and geometry, so its effective path is not automatically one centimetre.

Consider a purified chromophore with ε = 12,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ at a given wavelength. In a 1.00 cm cell a solution at 25.0 µM has A = 12,000 × 25.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.00 = 0.300. Its transmittance is 10⁻⁰·³⁰⁰, or about 0.501. At 50.0 µM, A = 0.600 and T ≈ 0.251 under the same assumptions. Doubling concentration doubles absorbance but approximately squares the original transmittance. Students who treat the fraction of light removed as proportional to concentration will predict the wrong result.

An absorbance reading can also be used in reverse. If a sample measured at the same wavelength in a 0.500 cm path has A = 0.240, then c = A/(εl) = 0.240/(12,000 × 0.500) M = 40.0 µM. If this was a fourfold dilution of an original sample, the original concentration is 160 µM. The factor of four describes final volume divided by the volume of original sample present in the diluted mixture. The dilution calculation refers to concentration, not absorbance measured directly on the undiluted sample. A highly concentrated sample might lie outside the valid instrumental range even when its diluted measurement is accurate.

The derivation assumes that the absorption coefficient remains independent of concentration and position. This requires a homogeneous medium with effectively constant molecular species and appropriate dilute-solution behaviour. It also assumes that the radiation is sufficiently monochromatic, that absorption is the relevant source of attenuation, and that the optical system measures the transmitted radiation without significant stray light. Instrument response must be calibrated adequately. The Beer–Lambert relationship is a conditional measurement model, not a universal guarantee that any reading on any sample is proportional to its concentration.

If two independently absorbing substances are present, a thin layer removes light according to the sum of their absorption contributions. The same integration gives A = l(ε₁c₁ + ε₂c₂) at a specified wavelength. The additivity follows from independent attenuation coefficients in the ideal solution, not from simple addition of percentages of light removed. It applies only when mixing does not alter the absorbers' optical properties. A reaction between the substances, a changed pH or complex formation can invalidate the coefficients measured for isolated components. In biological assays, a calibration based on the actual matrix may be more reliable than assuming that separately measured constants remain valid after mixing.

5. Molar absorption coefficients and biological concentration measurements

The molar absorption coefficient expresses how strongly one chemical species contributes to absorbance at a particular wavelength for a specified concentration and optical path. The coefficient is wavelength dependent; a molecule that absorbs strongly at 260 nm may have negligible absorption at 600 nm. It is also dependent on the physical and chemical conditions of the measurement. A reliable table of coefficients should specify the molecular form, wavelength, solvent and other relevant factors rather than attach a single number to a molecule without qualification.

One microscopic interpretation begins by defining an absorption cross section σ as an effective area characterizing the probability of absorbing a photon from the beam. For independent identical molecules at number density n, a thin layer attenuates power according to dP = −nσP dx. If n is molecules per cubic metre and σ is square metres, nσ has units of inverse metres. This interpretation connects the macroscopic coefficient to individual molecular interactions. It is an effective optical area rather than a physical cross-section that can necessarily be measured with a ruler. The concentration and path units must be made consistent when translating between molecular cross sections and molar decadic coefficients.

Proteins provide a useful biological example because their absorption at 280 nm is determined substantially by aromatic residues, particularly tryptophan and tyrosine, with a smaller contribution from cystine disulfide bonds. Pace and colleagues compared 116 measured coefficients for 80 proteins with residue-based estimates and developed an empirical equation for folded proteins in water. Expressed in M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, ε₂₈₀ ≈ 5500nTrp + 1490nTyr + 125nCystine. Here nCystine is the number of disulfide pairs, not the number of individual cysteine residues. This empirical relation is less reliable for proteins lacking tryptophan, and the exact coefficient can be affected by the local residue environment.

For an illustrative protein containing three tryptophan residues, five tyrosine residues and two disulfide bonds per molecule, the estimated coefficient is 3 × 5500 + 5 × 1490 + 2 × 125 = 24,200 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. If A₂₈₀ is 0.484 in a 1.00 cm cell, c ≈ 0.484/24,200 = 2.00 × 10⁻⁵ M, or 20.0 µM. A molar mass of 50,000 g mol⁻¹ corresponds to a mass concentration of 1.00 g L⁻¹, or 1.00 mg mL⁻¹. These calculations depend on the stated molecular composition and the assumption that other absorbing substances do not significantly contribute to the reading.

If a dimeric protein is present, the definition of molar concentration must be consistent with the coefficient. If a monomer contains one tryptophan, the coefficient calculated for that monomer is not the coefficient per mole of dimers. With unchanged independent residue contributions, a dimer has approximately twice the monomer coefficient per mole of dimer. The same absorbance could describe a monomer-equivalent concentration twice the molecular dimer concentration. An examination problem that supplies an oligomeric molecular mass and a subunit absorption coefficient must specify which amount is being counted.

Proteins with few aromatic residues may be difficult to measure accurately by direct 280 nm absorption. The peptide backbone absorbs at shorter ultraviolet wavelengths, but many buffer components and other solutes can also absorb there. A dye-binding or colour-forming assay may be more appropriate when direct absorption lacks sensitivity or specificity. Such assays generate a chromophore whose absorbance is calibrated against suitable protein standards. They introduce their own dependencies on protein composition and chemical interference. Different methods may consequently disagree without either instrument necessarily being defective.

Nucleotide bases absorb ultraviolet radiation near 260 nm. Nucleic acid concentration is therefore often estimated from A₂₆₀. For conventional 1 cm normalized absorbance, an A₂₆₀ of one is frequently taken to represent about 50 µg mL⁻¹ of double-stranded DNA, 40 µg mL⁻¹ of RNA, or roughly 33 µg mL⁻¹ of single-stranded DNA under the relevant conventional assumptions. These are approximate mass conversion factors, not universal constants. Sequence, strand state, base composition and solution conditions influence the coefficient. Short oligonucleotides are better quantified with appropriate sequence-specific extinction coefficients when accuracy matters.

A sample with normalized A₂₆₀ = 0.360 that was diluted tenfold would have an estimated original double-stranded DNA mass concentration of 0.360 × 50 × 10 = 180 µg mL⁻¹, or 180 ng µL⁻¹. If the original sample volume were 120 µL, the estimated total DNA yield would be 21.6 µg. This calculation gives a useful approximation but cannot distinguish double-stranded DNA from contaminating RNA, free nucleotides or other ultraviolet absorbers. It estimates DNA correctly only when the sample identity and the stated conversion assumptions are justified.

Ratios such as A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ and A₂₆₀/A₂₃₀ are often reported to screen nucleic acid preparations for interference. Typical values near 1.8 for relatively pure DNA and 2.0 for relatively pure RNA are useful reference expectations, not definitive quality criteria. The ratios depend on pH, ionic strength, blank quality, composition and measurement uncertainty. Wilfinger and colleagues found that changing the solution pH from about 5.4 to 7.5–8.5 shifted RNA A260/A280 from roughly 1.5 to 2.0, illustrating how the solvent can change a purity estimate without a corresponding change in RNA identity. A normal ratio does not prove molecular integrity, sequence identity or freedom from all contaminants. A low ratio does not uniquely identify protein contamination, because other species and background errors can alter both wavelengths.

If an absorbance denominator is very small, a ratio becomes unstable. Imagine two measurements of A₂₈₀ near 0.01 with a possible background error of 0.005. The resulting ratios may differ greatly even when the absolute signals differ only slightly. A purity ratio should therefore be interpreted alongside absolute absorbances, spectral baseline, replicate variability and independent evidence when the sample is dilute. Electrophoretic analysis, for example, can indicate fragmentation in a nucleic acid preparation that produces an apparently normal spectroscopic purity ratio.

A coefficient is measured most reliably by connecting optical absorption to an independently established concentration. One can determine ε from the slope of an absorbance-versus-concentration plot with known optical path length and valid standards. A single absorbance measurement can give an estimate of ε, but errors in concentration, blank or path length directly affect the result. When concentration itself has been calculated using an assumed ε, using that concentration to validate the same ε is circular reasoning. Independent concentration evidence is therefore central to coefficient determination.

The biological purpose of concentration measurement is often to normalize subsequent experiments. Enzyme activity per milligram of protein, binding stoichiometry, comparison of gene expression and analysis of purified macromolecule yield may all depend on an accurate estimate of the input amount. An apparent difference between two biological samples can originate in incorrect normalization rather than a real difference in function. It is important to record the coefficient used, the wavelength, the sample preparation and the assumptions under which a concentration was estimated. This makes an optical reading an interpretable part of the biological evidence rather than an isolated number.

6. Reading absorption spectra and resolving mixtures

A spectrum is a relationship, not a collection of unrelated absorbance readings. An absorption spectrum of a purified solute is obtained by measuring its optical attenuation over a series of wavelengths while maintaining the sample in a defined condition. The reference must be appropriate at each wavelength. Because lamp output, detector responsivity and the transmission of optical components vary across the spectral region, a raw detector voltage cannot usually be plotted directly as an absorption spectrum. A reference spectrum or equivalent measurement is required to distinguish the sample's optical contribution from the instrument's wavelength-dependent response.

On a conventional plot of absorbance against wavelength, an upward peak represents a region in which the sample transmits less radiation relative to its reference. On a transmittance plot, the corresponding feature points downward. The wavelengths of ideal maxima and minima are related because absorbance is a monotonic decreasing function of transmittance. The vertical shapes are not identical, since the logarithm changes the scale. A question may show an absorption band and a transmission trough in separate plots. Recognizing that the two plots describe the same optical process prevents the mistaken conclusion that they represent opposite molecular transitions.

The peak wavelength is often a useful descriptor, but several other features are informative. The height of a band can indicate absorption strength or concentration. The width describes the wavelength interval over which absorption remains substantial. A shoulder is a change of slope that may result from overlapping bands. The background away from the main band may reveal another absorber, scattering, or baseline error. A comparison of spectra must consider whether the data were measured in the same solvent, at the same temperature and concentration, with the same path length and wavelength bandwidth. Otherwise an apparent change in molecular structure may simply reflect unequal experimental conditions.

Suppose the absorption spectra of two aliquots of a purified pigment have identical peak wavelengths and the second spectrum is approximately twice as high throughout the useful wavelength interval. If both aliquots satisfy the Beer–Lambert relationship, a twofold difference in concentration is a simple explanation. If the concentration was independently known to be equal, the path length or a multiplicative instrumental scaling error becomes a competing explanation. A change in chromophore state might produce an approximately uniform increase in some spectral regions, so proportionality by itself is not an absolute identification of the cause. The experiment must constrain the alternatives.

By contrast, if the peak moves from 450 nm to 480 nm while the concentration and optical path remain unchanged, a change in the energies or relative contributions of absorption transitions is plausible. The shift may arise from changes in protonation, binding, solvent environment or conformation. A mixture can also produce a moving apparent maximum as its composition changes. The maximum of the sum of two overlapping bands is not necessarily the maximum of either component. Therefore a shifted peak does not prove that a particular covalent bond has changed. The interpretation must be based on an explicit model for the candidate chemical species.

A normalized spectrum is produced by dividing absorbances by a reference value such as the maximum or an integrated area. This procedure can make spectra with different absolute amplitudes easier to compare. For example, if two pigment samples have unknown concentrations, normalization to their respective maxima can reveal differences in relative band shape. It also destroys the absolute concentration information carried by amplitude. Two normalized spectra that coincide may have come from samples with very different amounts of pigment. Furthermore, normalizing noisy or weak spectra can amplify the apparent differences because small errors in the normalization denominator propagate into every plotted point.

A difference spectrum subtracts one spectrum from another at corresponding wavelengths. Consider the conversion of a reduced cofactor to its oxidized form. If the sample initially contains mostly reduced cofactor and later contains mostly oxidized cofactor, the difference spectrum may have both positive and negative bands. Negative values mean the final sample absorbs less than the initial sample at those wavelengths; they are not intrinsically negative molecular absorption coefficients. A properly designed subtraction uses matched paths, references and appropriate dilution corrections. Subtraction can reveal small changes superimposed on large signals but it can also exaggerate baseline mismatch.

To analyze a mixture quantitatively, consider two solutes X and Y whose coefficients have been measured independently in the same solvent. Their contributions at wavelength λ₁ are described by A₁ = l(εX₁cX + εY₁cY). At a second wavelength λ₂, A₂ = l(εX₂cX + εY₂cY). A single wavelength produces one equation for two unknown concentrations, so in general infinitely many pairs could explain the reading. The second wavelength provides a second constraint. To solve uniquely, the two equations must contain different information about the relative contributions of the two species.

For an illustrative dataset, let the path length be 1.00 cm and let the coefficients at λ₁ be 20,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ for X and 5,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ for Y. At λ₂, let the coefficients be 4,000 and 16,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, respectively. Measured absorbances are 0.450 and 0.240. It is helpful to express concentrations in micromolar units, multiplying each coefficient by 10⁻⁶. The first equation becomes 0.020cX + 0.005cY = 0.450, and the second becomes 0.004cX + 0.016cY = 0.240, with cX and cY now in µM.

Multiply the first equation by 3.2 to make its coefficient for Y equal to 0.016. Subtract the second equation from the result to obtain 0.060cX = 1.200. Thus cX = 20.0 µM. Substitute this value into the second equation: 0.080 + 0.016cY = 0.240, giving cY = 10.0 µM. Check against the original first measurement: 0.020 × 20 + 0.005 × 10 = 0.450. This final substitution is an important safeguard against swapped coefficients and arithmetic errors. The two readings are consistent with the specified model.

The determinant of the two-coefficient matrix is εX₁εY₂ − εY₁εX₂. If it is zero, the two coefficient vectors are proportional, meaning the second equation adds no independent information. Even a small nonzero determinant can be problematic when the spectra are nearly proportional. Small errors in the measured absorbances may then produce large errors in the recovered concentrations. This is why selecting wavelengths with contrasting relative absorption coefficients is more important than selecting two arbitrary wavelengths with large absorbances. The experiment should distinguish the species, not merely measure the total optical signal precisely.

A solution to the algebra that yields a negative concentration may indicate that noise, baseline error, incorrect coefficients, or the assumed two-species model is inconsistent with the measured data. It should not automatically be interpreted as evidence for a negative amount of material. If more than two species contribute independently, more independent measurements are generally needed. Recording a complete spectrum provides many data points, but overlapping spectral shapes can still limit the number of distinguishable components. More readings do not compensate for a failure of chemical specificity or inadequate independent information.

An isosbestic point is a wavelength at which spectra of a changing system intersect at approximately the same absorbance. For a simple interconversion X ⇌ Y with conserved total concentration and constant path length, if εX = εY at a particular wavelength, then A = εl(cX + cY) remains constant there as the proportions change. Such an intersection is consistent with a two-state model under these conditions. It is not conclusive proof that only two species exist. Additional species may also have matching coefficients or change in ways that preserve the total absorbance at that wavelength. An absence of a clean crossing point may arise from baseline drift, dilution or a more complex reaction mechanism.

A protonation equilibrium illustrates the usefulness of an absorbance measurement for chemical-state analysis. For a weak acid HA that dissociates to H⁺ and A⁻, the ratio of the two forms depends on pH. At a suitable wavelength where their coefficients differ, and with total analyte concentration and path fixed, the absorbance lies between the pure-form values AHA and AA. Under the two-state model the deprotonated fraction is (A − AHA)/(AA − AHA). If AHA = 0.10, AA = 0.70 and a sample has A = 0.40, the fraction is 0.50. Under ideal acid–base equilibrium conditions, equal concentrations of HA and A⁻ occur at pH equal to pKa.

This example demonstrates why absorbance can be sensitive to regulation-like changes without changes in total biomolecule abundance. A binding equilibrium, a protonation equilibrium or an oxidation–reduction reaction can alter the relative populations of absorbing states. The measurement identifies a population-weighted signal, not an individual molecular event. To recover the fractions, the coefficients or limiting signals must be known and the assumptions must hold. If a second ionizable group contributes significantly, the two-state model may be inadequate. The same logic will later be applied to interpreting changes in fluorescence and circular dichroism.

7. How a UV–visible spectrophotometer measures light

An instrument that measures absorbance has to perform several physical tasks. It must generate radiation over the required spectral interval, isolate or identify the wavelength of interest, direct light through a reproducible sample geometry, detect the transmitted radiant power, and transform the signal into a ratio against a reference. Different instrument designs distribute these tasks differently. A filter photometer may use a fixed spectral band and a simple detector. A scanning spectrophotometer changes the wavelength selected by a dispersive system. A diode-array spectrophotometer can record a range of wavelengths using a spatially distributed detector.

A common broad-band ultraviolet source is a deuterium discharge lamp. A tungsten or tungsten-halogen lamp is often used in the visible and near-infrared region. Some instruments use xenon flash lamps or other sources capable of spanning broad wavelength ranges. The correct general principle is that the source must provide sufficient stable radiant power over the intended region. It does not follow that every UV–visible instrument contains exactly two lamps or that one lamp is always superior. The stability, wavelength range, intensity and optical design together determine the usefulness of a source for a particular measurement.

A source may produce radiation at many wavelengths simultaneously. A wavelength selector separates or preferentially transmits a band of that radiation. A filter has a wavelength-dependent transmission profile. A monochromator often employs an entrance slit, collimating optics, a diffraction grating or prism, focusing optics and an exit slit. A diffraction grating separates wavelengths into different directions by interference. Changing the grating orientation changes the wavelength range directed through the exit slit. The slit width and dispersion determine the spectral bandwidth rather than the nominal wavelength alone.

Narrowing the bandwidth improves the ability to distinguish nearby spectral features, but it also usually reduces the radiant power reaching the detector. A weaker detected signal is more vulnerable to certain forms of noise. Widening the bandwidth may improve signal strength while reducing spectral resolution and introducing deviations when absorption varies strongly within the band. The best setting depends on whether the measurement aims to resolve the fine structure of a spectrum or quantify an analyte at a broad absorption maximum. A broad and well-separated peak may allow a larger bandwidth without sacrificing useful selectivity.

In a conventional scanning instrument, the selected light passes through the sample after wavelength selection. In many array instruments, broad-band light passes through the sample first, and a dispersive element separates the transmitted light across multiple detector elements. Each detector element corresponds to a limited spectral region. Recording many wavelengths rapidly can be valuable when a chemical reaction changes during measurement. It does not mean all array instruments have identical resolution or that simultaneous spectral acquisition eliminates all instrumental errors. The source spectrum, stray radiation, optical geometry and calibration remain relevant.

The sample is commonly held in a cuvette. Its optical path length is the distance the beam traverses through the solution. A conventional square cuvette may have a 1 cm path, but not every cell has that length. The transparent windows must transmit the wavelength of interest. Ordinary glass and many common plastics absorb strongly in the ultraviolet, making them unsuitable for measurements near 260 nm unless the specific material is certified for that range. Fused silica or quartz cells are widely used for ultraviolet work. In the visible region, suitable glass and plastic cuvettes are often acceptable. Choice depends on both optical transmission and chemical compatibility.

A blank cuvette filled with the same solvent or reagent matrix can correct for much of the background absorption of the solution and optical cell. However, if the cell material absorbs almost all incident radiation at the measurement wavelength, blanking cannot restore the lost photons or guarantee reliable readings. Very little power may reach the detector, producing a noisy ratio or an instrumental limitation. Blanking a glass cell at a wavelength where the glass is effectively opaque does not make the glass suitable for ultraviolet analysis. The correct response is to use a cell material and instrument configuration appropriate for the measurement.

Cuvette handling affects the optical measurement through geometry and unwanted attenuation. Fingerprints, droplets, scratches, bubbles and particles can scatter or absorb light. Changing the orientation of a cell whose windows are imperfect may alter the transmitted signal. A reproducible fill height must place liquid across the beam. A meniscus or trapped air bubble in the optical path changes refraction and scattering. These effects can be important even when the solution itself is chemically stable. Replicate readings obtained without repositioning the cell may fail to reveal a systematic error due to one contaminated window.

The transmitted radiation is converted to an electrical signal by a detector. A photodiode produces an electrical response when absorbed photons create mobile charge carriers within a semiconductor. A photomultiplier tube uses photoelectric conversion followed by multiplication of electrons to amplify weak signals. An array of photodiodes provides multiple detection channels for spectral acquisition. Each detector has a wavelength-dependent responsivity and a useful range over which its output relates predictably to incident radiant power. A source that emits ample radiation does not guarantee a strong measured signal if the detector is insensitive at the selected wavelength.

In a linear detector model, a measured signal S can be written as gP + Sdark, where g is a responsivity or gain factor and Sdark is an additive background measured without useful incident light. If the reference and sample have the same gain, and the dark term is accurately estimated, then T = (Ssample − Sdark)/(Sreference − Sdark). For example, if the reference signal is 1000 arbitrary units, the sample signal is 280 and the dark signal is 40, the corrected transmittance is 240/960 = 0.25. The absorbance is log₁₀4 ≈ 0.602. Ignoring the dark signal would give T = 0.28 and A ≈ 0.553, an underestimate in this illustrative case.

A single-beam spectrophotometer uses one optical path for reference and sample measurements, often measuring them sequentially. The design is conceptually simple and can be effective when the source and detector are stable. A change in lamp intensity between the reference measurement and the sample measurement can be misinterpreted as a change in transmittance. Repeated reference measurements and stable operating conditions help detect this problem. If reference and sample are measured far apart in time, the assumptions behind their ratio become less secure.

A double-beam instrument compares light in a sample channel with light in a reference channel, using a beam splitter or alternating optical paths depending on the design. Because variations in the common source can affect both channels similarly, the ratio may compensate for some lamp drift. But channel-specific errors can remain. Unequal cuvettes, different optical transmission in the two paths, detector differences and inappropriate reference composition can create systematic errors. A double-beam design improves certain aspects of stability; it does not provide automatic proof of correct concentration or remove the need for suitable blanks.

Consider a single-beam instrument whose lamp output falls by 10% after the reference reading. A sample with a true transmittance of 0.5 will then yield a power of 0.45 times the earlier reference power. The apparent absorbance is −log₁₀(0.45) ≈ 0.347 rather than the true value of about 0.301. In a perfectly common-mode double-beam measurement, the contemporary reference would also have fallen by 10%, preserving the ratio of 0.5. This numerical example explains which source of error is addressed by the reference channel. It does not assume that the double-beam instrument corrects every type of drift.

In a microvolume measurement, a short liquid column may be formed between optical surfaces without a conventional cuvette. This reduces the amount of sample needed and allows highly absorbing solutions to be measured over short paths. Some instruments adjust the path length automatically and report a normalized equivalent absorbance. The user must distinguish the physical transmission measured through the short liquid column from the scaled result reported for a standard path. A reading normalized to a 1 cm path is not the measured transmittance of a 1 cm cell. The reported quantity is a conversion based on the presumed linearity of absorbance with path length.

A plate reader measures multiple wells efficiently. In many absorbance plate readers the beam traverses the liquid vertically, so the path length changes with volume and well dimensions. If a standard and an unknown have different volumes, equal absorbance need not imply equal concentration. One can use equal volumes and matched wells or apply a validated path-length correction. Bubbles and differences in meniscus shape may contribute to well-to-well variation. The convenience of measuring many samples does not remove the need to design comparable optical geometries.

Several different quality checks address different parts of an instrument. Wavelength accuracy can be checked using materials with well-characterized spectral features. Photometric accuracy assesses whether known transmittance or absorbance standards are read correctly. Stray-light tests assess whether unwanted radiation reaches the detector under conditions designed to reveal it. Repeatability tests measure the variability of repeated observations. The National Institute of Standards and Technology describes traceability through reference materials for wavelength and transmittance or absorbance scales. A successful concentration calibration on one day is not a universal verification of every optical property.

A spectrophotometer therefore produces an interpretable number only through a chain of operations, each with its own limitations. The lamp supplies photons; the optics select or separate wavelengths; the sample cell defines the interaction geometry; the detector measures arriving radiation; the electronics and software handle the reference and logarithm. If an examination question describes an unexpectedly high absorbance, identify which part of this chain could plausibly cause it before assuming a molecular explanation. The instrument is a measurement model implemented in hardware rather than a direct counter of biomolecules.

8. Blanks, standards, calibration and analytical validation

There are two conceptually different ways to infer concentration from absorbance. The first uses a known absorption coefficient and a known path length. The second constructs an empirical calibration curve using samples of known concentration. Both approaches rely on comparable optical measurements, but the calibration approach can incorporate the behaviour of reagents, matrix components and the actual instrument. If a biochemical colour reaction generates a product whose yield depends on conditions, a standard curve prepared using the same assay procedure is often more appropriate than applying a coefficient measured for an unrelated system.

A standard is a sample whose concentration or other relevant property is known with adequate confidence. A calibration blank contains the components required to reproduce the optical and chemical background without the intended analyte. For a direct protein measurement, the reference may be the protein's buffer. For a colour-forming assay, a reagent blank may contain the assay reagents and buffer but no protein. These are different situations: some reagents themselves absorb, and a solvent-only blank may fail to account for that contribution. In a biological reaction, a control that contains all components except the active enzyme can also reveal a time-dependent background.

A blank correction is not necessarily a subtraction of a fixed absorbance from every observation. When a spectrophotometer establishes a reference power using a blank, the subsequent reported absorbance is already relative to that blank. Under suitable independent attenuation assumptions, separate measured absorbances can be subtracted to remove an additive background contribution. But if a blank changes the optical properties of the analyte or if the background scatters differently when analyte is present, a simple subtraction may not isolate the desired signal. Knowing how the instrument or assay processed the blank prevents double correction and false confidence in the resulting value.

Suppose an assay is used to estimate an unknown protein concentration. Standards at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 µg mL⁻¹ give illustrative measured absorbances 0.025, 0.125, 0.225, 0.325, 0.425 and 0.525. The difference of 0.100 absorbance for each 2 µg mL⁻¹ increment gives a slope of 0.050 absorbance per µg mL⁻¹. The intercept is 0.025. The empirical model is A = 0.025 + 0.050c. An unknown with measured absorbance 0.365 corresponds to c = (0.365 − 0.025)/0.050 = 6.80 µg mL⁻¹ under the same assay conditions.

The intercept in this example represents the constant background incorporated into the calibration. If a researcher used A = 0.050c without removing the background, the inferred concentration would be 7.30 µg mL⁻¹. The absolute error would be 0.50 µg mL⁻¹. The proportional impact of a constant blank error is larger for low-concentration samples than for high-concentration samples. Near the detection limit, a small intercept error can dominate the estimated amount. For this reason low-level measurements require particular attention to blank variability and bias.

Real data do not generally lie exactly on a straight line. Linear regression estimates the intercept and slope using the pattern of the observed standards. The ordinary least-squares approach selects the line that minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals, where a residual is the measured absorbance minus the fitted absorbance at that concentration. This model commonly treats the concentrations as sufficiently well known and the absorbance errors as independent with a suitable variance structure. If standards themselves have large concentration uncertainty, the assumptions of simple regression may not be satisfied. The details of statistical inference are developed later in the methods unit.

A residual plot provides information beyond a correlation coefficient. If low-concentration and high-concentration standards systematically fall below the line while intermediate standards fall above it, a curved relationship may be present. If all residuals are scattered randomly around zero but spread increases with concentration, the measurement variability is concentration dependent. A strong correlation coefficient or high coefficient of determination does not prove that the Beer–Lambert relationship holds perfectly, especially over a narrow concentration range or when a curved response remains strongly monotonic. Model adequacy depends on whether residuals and independent checks support the intended use.

An analyst should establish the concentration interval within which the calibration gives reliable estimates for the assay. An unknown whose absorbance lies within this validated interval can be estimated by interpolation. An absorbance far beyond the highest standard requires extrapolation, which assumes without direct evidence that the same model continues to hold. For a strongly absorbing sample, stray light or detector limitations may cause curvature. For a colour-forming assay, reagent depletion can limit product formation. Diluting the unknown into the validated range and correcting for dilution is usually more defensible than extrapolating a straight line beyond the standards.

Consider an illustrative calibration that is linear from 0 to 10 µM with A = 0.08c, and a sample giving A = 1.20. Direct extrapolation gives 15 µM. A fourfold dilution of the sample gives A = 0.400, corresponding to 5.0 µM in the diluted solution and 20.0 µM in the original sample. These estimates disagree. The disagreement shows that at least one assumption or observation is problematic. If the diluted measurement is independently shown to be within the validated range and dilution preserves the analyte form, it is more defensible. The lower direct reading is consistent with a negative deviation at high concentration, but additional controls are required to identify the mechanism.

Serial dilutions provide a useful internal test of proportionality. If a sample is diluted by factors of two, four and eight, the corrected absorbances should scale approximately as one-half, one-quarter and one-eighth, assuming linearity and unchanged chemical state. The concentration recovered after multiplying by each dilution factor should be approximately constant. A discrepancy at the largest concentration may indicate optical nonlinearity. A discrepancy at the lowest concentration may reflect noise or a poor blank. A continuous trend over several dilutions could indicate a change in molecular state, such as dissociation of an absorbing aggregate, rather than an optical failure alone.

Matrix effects occur when substances in the sample other than the analyte influence the measured response. A purified standard in water may behave differently from the same analyte dissolved in a buffered biological extract. Other components can absorb at the chosen wavelength, alter the analyte's chemical environment, react with assay reagents or change scattering. Matrix matching attempts to reproduce relevant background conditions in the standards. It does not require the standards to contain an unknown amount of the target analyte. The aim is to make the relationship between target concentration and measured response comparable.

Standard addition is one approach to a matrix-dependent response. A known amount of analyte is added to aliquots of the unknown sample, and the change in signal is used to estimate the original analyte concentration under a suitable linear model. For a simple illustration, maintain constant final volume and constant proportion of unknown matrix in all preparations. Suppose the blank-corrected absorbance is 0.200 before addition and 0.350 after adding 3.00 µM of the same analyte to the final mixture. The incremental slope is (0.350 − 0.200)/3.00 = 0.050 per µM. The unspiked preparation then contains 0.200/0.050 = 4.00 µM analyte under the model. Any dilution of the original unknown into the preparations must be corrected separately.

Standard addition does not automatically solve a selectivity problem. If the sample contains an unrelated absorber that contributes a constant 0.100 absorbance not removed by blanking, the unspiked signal includes both analyte and interferent. Adding known analyte estimates its incremental response, but attributing the entire intercept to the analyte would overestimate its concentration. The method can address certain multiplicative matrix effects when the added analyte behaves like the original analyte; it cannot generally distinguish indistinguishable additive optical signals without extra information. The assumptions of the chosen calibration method must be articulated.

A spike-recovery experiment tests whether a known increase in the amount of analyte produces the expected increase in the estimated concentration in a sample matrix. If an unknown gives 8.0 µM and an aliquot spiked by 5.0 µM gives 12.5 µM after accounting for all volumes, the recovered increment is 4.5 µM and recovery is 90%. This is evidence about the behaviour of the added analyte under those conditions. It does not by itself prove that the original 8.0 µM is correct, because the original analyte may exist in a different binding state or share the signal with an interferent. A recovery value must be evaluated against suitable method requirements rather than an assumed universal acceptable percentage.

Replicates help separate variability from systematic bias. Repeated optical readings of a single cuvette assess short-term instrumental repeatability and perhaps repositioning effects. Independent preparation of several aliquots includes variability introduced by pipetting, sample handling and assay chemistry. Independent biological samples represent variation among biological units. These replicate types answer different questions. Ten repeated measurements of the same cuvette do not establish how variable a biological population is. In an enzyme experiment, independent preparations and appropriate biological replication may be necessary for the intended biological conclusion.

Sensitivity describes how much the analytical response changes with concentration, commonly represented by the slope of the calibration. Selectivity describes the method's ability to measure the intended component in the presence of others. A very large slope does not guarantee selectivity if an interferent produces an equally strong response. Precision describes the closeness of repeated results; accuracy concerns closeness to an appropriate reference or true value. A method can be precise but biased if the calibration standards are incorrectly prepared. These terms must remain separate when evaluating an assay.

The limit of detection concerns whether an analyte signal can be distinguished from background variation according to a defined criterion. The limit of quantification concerns whether it can be estimated with acceptable precision and bias. As an elementary illustrative convention, if the standard deviation of blank absorbance is 0.006 and the calibration slope is 0.050 absorbance per µM, a three-standard-deviation concentration estimate is 3 × 0.006/0.050 = 0.36 µM, and a ten-standard-deviation estimate is 1.20 µM. These are approximate model-based criteria; actual detection and quantification limits require validated definitions, experimental design and performance evidence. They are not universal constants of the instrument.

An uncertainty calculation makes the calibration's limitations visible. For a fixed slope and intercept, c = (A − b)/m, so an absorbance uncertainty of 0.010 with m = 0.050 per µM corresponds to a concentration uncertainty of 0.20 µM. Uncertainties in slope, intercept, dilution factor and standards introduce further contributions. Near a small corrected signal, relative uncertainty becomes large. Reporting more decimal places than are supported by the measurement conceals rather than improves accuracy. A defensible result includes suitable units, dilution correction, and a reasonable statement of measurement variability or uncertainty.

These calibration principles apply across the life sciences. They allow a researcher to compare macromolecule concentrations, quantify colour-forming reactions and normalize biological experiments. But calibration is not a substitute for a correct model of the sample. When a spectrum changes shape after dilution, a linear fit to a standard curve may hide evidence of altered molecular state. When the sample scatters strongly, a low residual pattern in a limited concentration interval may still reflect attenuation rather than absorption. The next step is to examine the physical mechanisms responsible for such departures from the ideal model.

9. Scattering, stray radiation and departures from ideal absorption

When a beam passes through a transparent molecular solution, the ideal Beer–Lambert model treats the reduction in transmitted power as absorption by independent solute molecules. In a cloudy suspension the situation is different. Particles, droplets, organelles or aggregates can redirect light away from the detector without converting all of its energy to electronic excitation. The detector records less forward radiation, and the instrument may report a positive absorbance even when absorption by the particles is negligible at that wavelength. This is the distinction between molecular absorption and optical attenuation.

Scattering involves a change in the direction of propagation of radiation. In elastic scattering, the frequency of the scattered radiation is unchanged. The incident electric field induces a response in matter, which can radiate in other directions. The strength and angular distribution depend on the contrast between the optical properties of a particle and its surroundings, particle size, shape, wavelength and other factors. Very small particles compared with the wavelength can exhibit Rayleigh-like scattering, whose intensity under specified conditions increases strongly toward short wavelengths, approximately as the inverse fourth power of wavelength. Larger particles exhibit more complex scattering behaviour. The inverse-fourth-power relation cannot be applied universally to cell suspensions or protein aggregates.

Consider two suspensions at wavelengths where molecular absorption is negligible. One contains relatively small particles, the other larger particles. Both reduce the amount of radiation reaching the forward detector. Their measured attenuation may depend on how much scattered radiation enters the detector's acceptance angle. A detector with a wider acceptance angle may collect more forward-scattered light and therefore report lower apparent optical density than a narrow-angle system. Thus the reading is not solely a property of the suspension. It also depends on the measurement geometry, particularly when scattering is appreciable.

In a dilute suspension, attenuation can sometimes be approximately proportional to the particle number concentration over a limited interval. This permits relative monitoring of particle abundance under calibrated conditions. Stevenson and colleagues compared optical-density calibration across particle sizes and measurement geometries, demonstrating the need for system-specific interpretation. However, the proportionality can change with cell size, morphology, refractive index, aggregation and the instrument's collection geometry. At high particle density, light can undergo multiple scattering, and the simple independent-particle approximation breaks down. A value of OD600, for instance, is not an absolute count of cells and is not universally convertible into biomass. It should not be interpreted as the concentration of a particular intracellular chromophore merely because the instrument labels the output absorbance.

The distinction is equally important when examining a purified protein. Suppose a protein sample develops a broad increase in apparent absorbance that extends from the ultraviolet into a visible region where the native protein has negligible molecular absorption. An increase in particle scattering due to aggregation is a plausible explanation. Another explanation could be the formation of an absorbing chemical product, or background mismatch. The broad spectral baseline, the sample's visible appearance, independent particle measurements and comparisons of well-defined sample fractions can help discriminate these possibilities. The spectral change alone cannot establish the precise size or morphology of aggregates.

A commonly used screening strategy in nucleic acid or protein spectroscopy is to examine a longer wavelength at which the intended analyte is expected to absorb very weakly. A nonzero reading there can suggest background attenuation or baseline error. Subtracting that value from ultraviolet readings can be a useful empirical correction for a constant offset, but scattering often varies with wavelength. A single long-wavelength value cannot generally correct the entire spectrum. For Rayleigh-like scattering, for example, attenuation at 260 nm may be much greater than at 340 nm. A scientifically defensible correction requires a justified background model or an independently verified measurement strategy.

A wavelength-dependent scattering background can alter the apparent maximum of an absorption band. Adding a larger short-wavelength baseline raises the left side of a peak more than its right side, shifting the maximum toward shorter wavelengths even when the molecular absorption band has not moved. This is a particularly useful examination trap because it combines a plausible spectral signature with an alternative physical explanation. A red or blue shift should therefore be interpreted only after inspecting the baseline and verifying the comparability of optical conditions.

Scattering measurements can also be deliberate rather than unwanted. A nephelometric measurement collects radiation scattered in a particular direction, often away from the incident beam. A turbidimetric measurement monitors attenuation of the transmitted beam by suspended material. The two approaches detect related effects but use different geometries and may have different sensitivity ranges. UV–visible absorption analysis seeks to quantify molecular absorption when that is the intended signal; turbidimetry seeks to exploit particle-induced attenuation. More detailed analysis of scattering for molecular size and interactions belongs to a later chapter. Here the key is to recognize scattering as a competing cause of a transmission measurement.

Another important instrumental limitation is stray radiation. Ideally the detector receives only the intended spectral radiation transmitted through the defined sample path. In practice, imperfections in optical selection, reflections, leakage or other paths may allow unwanted light to contribute to the signal. At high sample absorbance, the correctly transmitted radiant power becomes very small, so an otherwise minor unwanted contribution can become comparable to or larger than the desired signal. The result is often a negative departure from the expected absorbance at high concentration.

An illustrative model makes the effect clear. Normalize the intended reference radiant power to one and suppose an additive stray contribution s reaches the detector both during the reference and sample measurements without being attenuated by the intended sample absorption. The measured transmittance becomes Tobs = (Ttrue + s)/(1 + s). If s = 0.001, the limiting observed transmittance as the intended transmitted light tends to zero is approximately 0.000999. The largest apparent absorbance in this idealized model is therefore about 3.0004, regardless of how large the true absorbance becomes. Actual stray-light behaviour depends on its spectral distribution and how much of it the sample attenuates; this simple expression illustrates the mechanism rather than describing every instrument.

For a sample with true A = 2, the intended T = 0.01. With s = 0.001, the observed ratio becomes 0.011/1.001 ≈ 0.01099 and Aobs ≈ 1.959. For true A = 4, T = 0.0001 and Tobs ≈ 0.001099, giving Aobs ≈ 2.959. The error is much greater at the larger absorbance. The relationship is not repaired by subtracting a constant absorbance, because the extra light is added to the radiant power before the logarithm is taken. A dilution or a shorter path length can reduce the true absorbance into a more reliable region, provided the chemical and optical assumptions remain valid.

Finite spectral bandwidth introduces a different form of negative deviation. Suppose the selected radiation contains two equally weighted narrow components, and a solution has absorption coefficients of 10,000 and 2,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ at those wavelengths. The transmitted fraction is the average of the two component transmittances, not the transmittance calculated from the average absorbance. If the path is 1 cm and concentration is 100 µM, the component absorbances are 1.0 and 0.2. Their transmittances are 0.1 and approximately 0.631. The combined transmittance is about 0.3655 and the apparent absorbance is approximately 0.437, not the arithmetic mean of 0.600.

As concentration increases in this example, the more strongly absorbed wavelength contributes progressively less to the transmitted signal. The weakly absorbed component comes to dominate the detector output, lowering the effective response per additional unit of concentration. The measured absorbance curve bends away from the simple monochromatic prediction. Using a narrower bandwidth or measuring at a broad peak where the coefficient changes little across the transmitted interval can reduce this problem. The example also explains why averaging absorbances across wavelengths does not describe the response of an instrument that physically combines the light before detection.

Chemical departures occur when the molecular species responsible for absorption change as concentration, pH, solvent or other conditions vary. Consider a weakly acidic pigment whose ionized and unionized forms have different coefficients at the measurement wavelength. If a set of standards is prepared in poorly buffered solutions, increasing pigment concentration can alter pH and therefore the relative amount of each form. The effective absorption coefficient is no longer constant across the standards. Positive or negative curvature can arise depending on the relative coefficients and the direction of the pH change. The correct response is to control and characterize the chemical state, not automatically blame the detector.

Association equilibria provide a second chemical example. If molecules form dimers at higher concentration, and the dimer coefficient per monomer equivalent differs from that of the monomer, absorbance will not necessarily be proportional to the total number of monomer equivalents. Dilution may shift the equilibrium and change the spectrum. A researcher could observe a different apparent concentration after applying dilution corrections even though the optical instrument is functioning perfectly. Measuring spectral shapes and considering the expected concentration dependence of the association equilibrium helps distinguish this mechanism from stray light. A short path-length measurement at the original concentration can be useful because it changes the optical attenuation without necessarily changing the chemical equilibrium.

At sufficiently high concentrations, interactions between solute molecules and changes in the surrounding medium can alter optical properties even in the absence of a simple association reaction. The dilute-solution assumption limits the range over which a constant coefficient is a good approximation. This distinction is often called a fundamental or real departure from Beer–Lambert behaviour. Chemical and instrumental departures may occur together. One cannot uniquely assign the cause of a curved calibration without additional information. The observed pattern is evidence of model inadequacy, not necessarily proof of a single error mechanism.

The noise of an optical measurement also varies with the signal level. A small absolute error in transmittance propagates through the logarithm according to the derivative dA/dT = −1/(T ln 10). At very low T, even a modest absolute uncertainty in T can cause a large absorbance uncertainty. At very high T, the absorbance itself is close to zero, so a small absolute absorbance error can be a large fraction of the signal attributed to the analyte. This is why the most useful measurement interval is instrument and assay dependent. A universal requirement that all valid absorbances fall between two fixed numbers would be an oversimplification; validated performance is the relevant criterion.

To illustrate, an absolute transmittance uncertainty of 0.002 around T = 0.5 gives an approximate absorbance uncertainty of 0.002/(0.5 × ln 10) = 0.00174. The same absolute uncertainty around T = 0.02 gives about 0.0434. The approximation assumes the uncertainty is sufficiently small relative to the transmittance and uses a local linear expansion. For very small transmitted powers where the uncertainty approaches the measured power, the logarithm becomes highly asymmetric and the linear approximation may be poor. A negative background-corrected detector signal cannot be converted into a meaningful ordinary absorbance by taking its logarithm.

A negative reported absorbance is possible when the sample transmits more radiation than the reference. This may result from a reference that absorbs more strongly than the sample, reference mismatch, instrumental drift or a difference-spectrum measurement. It does not establish that an ordinary passive molecule has a negative absorption probability. If the reference was intended to represent the sample background, an unexpectedly negative reading is a diagnostic signal to examine the measurement setup. In a deliberately defined difference spectrum, negative changes can carry valid information about the disappearance of an absorber.

A practical way to reason about unexplained curvature is to compare hypotheses that make different predictions. If the issue is excessive optical attenuation at the selected wavelength, a shorter path and a lower concentration may both reduce the error. If dilution changes the chemical species, reducing path length without dilution may preserve the original spectral composition whereas dilution changes it. If scattering dominates, the baseline and the response to collection geometry may differ from those of a clean molecular absorber. If reference drift is the major cause, repeated measurements of a stable standard and the blank can reveal time-dependent changes independent of the sample's concentration. The most informative tests change one relevant factor while holding others as constant as possible.

Use the Beer–Lambert relationship as a reference model; distinguish causes affecting molecular state, optical geometry, wavelength selection, reference power or detection.

10. Biological assays, reaction rates and experimental evidence

A central use of absorption spectroscopy in biochemistry is to translate a molecular reaction into a measurable optical time course. The reaction itself need not be visible to the eye. It is enough that a substrate, product, cofactor or reporter contributes a detectable and interpretable difference in absorbance at a selected wavelength. A well-designed assay links the change in signal to the reaction stoichiometry and demonstrates that the signal is not primarily caused by unrelated processes.

The nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide system offers a classical example. Oxidized NAD⁺ and reduced NADH both absorb in the ultraviolet, but NADH has a characteristic absorption band near 340 nm that is far more prominent than the corresponding absorption of NAD⁺. The band arises from the changed electronic structure of the reduced nicotinamide group. This optical difference allows changes in NADH concentration to be monitored in many dehydrogenase reactions. The commonly used molar absorption coefficient of NADH near 340 nm is approximately 6.22 × 10³ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹; use the coefficient appropriate for the assay conditions.

Consider an illustrative reaction in which NADH is consumed in a one-to-one relationship with a biochemical transformation. If the corrected A₃₄₀ falls from 0.620 to 0.496 during 60 seconds in a 1 cm cell, the absorbance change is −0.124 per minute. The corresponding NADH concentration rate is −0.124/(6220 × 1) M min⁻¹ = −1.99 × 10⁻⁵ M min⁻¹, about −19.9 µM min⁻¹. The reaction's rate of NADH consumption is conventionally expressed as a positive magnitude of 19.9 µM min⁻¹. The negative sign in the concentration derivative indicates that NADH is disappearing, not that enzyme activity is negative.

If the reaction volume is 1.00 mL, the amount of NADH consumed per minute is 19.9 µmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹ × 0.00100 L = 0.0199 µmol min⁻¹. If the reaction contains 0.100 mg enzyme protein, the corresponding specific activity under the assumed one-to-one stoichiometry is 0.199 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹. The volume conversion is essential because absorbance initially reports concentration, whereas an enzyme activity unit is conventionally an amount transformed per unit time under specified assay conditions. A question may supply a reaction volume and ask for activity; treating concentration rate as amount rate would yield a dimensional error.

The sign and stoichiometry become especially important in coupled assays. Suppose the reaction of interest does not itself produce a distinct optical change but generates a metabolite that a second enzyme consumes while converting NADH to NAD⁺. The reporter reaction can then reflect the rate of the first reaction if the coupling is quantitative and sufficiently rapid. If the coupling enzyme is limiting, a lag or underestimated rate can result. If two molecules of NADH are consumed for every one molecule of the target substrate converted, the substrate transformation rate is half the NADH consumption rate. The reporter is an indirect measure, and its validity depends on the reaction scheme.

A time trace commonly includes an initial mixing phase, a region of approximately constant initial rate and a later region in which the rate changes as substrate is depleted, product accumulates or the enzyme loses activity. A suitable slope is estimated from a time interval that represents the intended kinetic quantity. Taking the difference between the first and last readings over a long experiment may average together several phases. When investigating initial rates, the purpose is to measure a rate before appreciable changes in substrate or product concentrations have altered the reaction conditions. This principle connects optical measurement to enzyme kinetics without replacing the kinetic analysis developed in Unit 1.

A no-enzyme or inactive-enzyme control can reveal background changes that occur in the absence of the intended catalytic reaction. A reference without NADH can help identify optical contributions from other mixture components, depending on the experiment. An assay without the relevant substrate can test whether the reporter changes independently of the intended reaction. These controls answer distinct questions. A solvent blank corrects optical background but does not necessarily reveal spontaneous cofactor oxidation. Conversely, a no-enzyme time course may reveal a background rate without establishing the absolute absorption coefficient of NADH.

Suppose an experimental trace decreases at 0.090 absorbance units per minute and the no-enzyme control decreases at 0.020 per minute. If the background rate is additive and comparable in the two mixtures, the enzyme-associated decrease is 0.070 per minute. With a 1 cm path and ε = 6220 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, the corrected NADH consumption rate is approximately 11.25 µM min⁻¹. However, if the enzyme changes the background process itself, subtraction may be insufficient. For example, the presence of the protein could alter the chemical environment or bind the cofactor. The correction must be justified by the control design rather than automatically applied to every pair of time traces.

A different application is monitoring the thermal stability of nucleic acids through the hyperchromic effect. A double-stranded DNA sample is heated under controlled conditions while ultraviolet absorbance near 260 nm is recorded. As the population of duplex states decreases and base stacking is disrupted, the absorbance generally increases. The transition may be sigmoidal. Marmur and Doty (1962) compared thermal-denaturation midpoints across DNA samples of differing base composition, showing how an optical transition could constrain a molecular property. It does not imply that every base pair in a heterogeneous DNA preparation dissociates at the same temperature or that the process is always well represented by a simple two-state equilibrium.

To estimate a fraction melted from an absorbance curve, the baselines of the lower- and higher-temperature states must be defined appropriately. In an illustrative ideal two-state calculation with constant baselines Aclosed = 0.50 and Aopen = 0.70, an observed absorbance of 0.60 corresponds to a fraction of (0.60 − 0.50)/(0.70 − 0.50) = 0.50. In a real melting curve, the baselines themselves may vary with temperature, requiring a more careful treatment. Thermal expansion, buffer changes, evaporation, instrument stability and other optical effects can complicate the interpretation. The normalized optical transition is therefore an operational reporter of structural changes rather than a direct count of intact hydrogen bonds.

Ionic strength can influence duplex stability because ions partially screen electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged phosphate groups of the strands. Different sequences and strand lengths also differ in stability. When two DNA samples give different melting profiles, a valid comparison needs controlled buffer conditions and a clear definition of the molecules being compared. A higher apparent melting temperature can be consistent with a more stable duplex under those conditions, but the optical trace alone cannot uniquely establish sequence composition or the microscopic order of individual base-pair disruptions. This illustrates how spectroscopy provides useful structural constraints at an ensemble level.

Plant pigments provide another important use of visible absorption spectra. Chlorophylls absorb strongly in particular blue and red regions of the visible spectrum, whereas their absorption is comparatively weaker over much of the green region. Carotenoids have absorption bands mainly in the blue to blue-green region. The perceived colour of a sample depends on which wavelengths reach the observer, not simply which wavelengths are absorbed most strongly. A solution that absorbs mainly blue radiation can appear yellowish or orange under broad white illumination, while an absorbing leaf also reflects and scatters light in a complex tissue geometry.

Pigment spectra depend on molecular environment. A pigment extracted into an organic solvent can have different peak positions and coefficients from the same pigment bound in a photosynthetic protein complex. Concentrations of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b can be estimated from measurements at selected wavelengths when the coefficients appropriate for the chosen solvent are known and the mixture model is valid. Coefficients determined in one solvent should not be transferred indiscriminately to another. A question that supplies a set of simultaneous equations for pigment concentrations expects the student to use the coefficients as defined rather than substitute remembered values from an unrelated solvent system.

Colour-forming biochemical assays expand the range of molecules that can be measured optically. Many carbohydrates, for example, do not provide a strong convenient visible absorption band on their own. A suitable chemical or enzymatic reporter can generate a coloured product related to the amount of carbohydrate under defined conditions. In Bradford's 1976 assay, protein binding shifts the dye absorption maximum from roughly 465 to 595 nm, providing a measurable reporter. The calibration reflects formation of the reporter state, not necessarily the direct absorption of the original analyte. Differences in reaction completeness, reagent composition, sample matrix and molecular composition can affect the relationship between concentration and signal.

A dye-binding assay can give different responses for equal masses of two proteins because their amino acid composition or accessibility of binding sites differs. A calibration prepared with one protein is then an estimate relative to that standard rather than a universally exact mass determination of every protein. A protein with a known absorption coefficient may be quantified independently by A₂₈₀ if the sample is suitable. Comparing both methods can reveal discrepancies, but it does not automatically identify which is correct. A third independent measurement or better characterization of the sample can resolve competing explanations.

The interpretation of haem protein spectra illustrates another strength of multiple wavelengths. A change in ligand binding or oxidation state can modify the optical absorption of a metal-containing cofactor. If the limiting spectra of two known forms are available, an intermediate spectrum can sometimes be represented by a linear combination of those forms. Agreement across several wavelengths supports the mixture model more strongly than agreement at a single wavelength. Nevertheless, another species with a similar spectrum could contribute to the result. An independent biochemical or structural observation strengthens the assignment.

Spectrophotometric measurements are particularly useful when connected to another method that addresses a different property. A chromatographic separation can isolate components before measurement, reducing interference from overlapping spectra. Electrophoresis can reveal a mixture of molecular sizes or charge states that an absorption measurement cannot distinguish. A fluorescence-based assay can provide a more selective or sensitive signal for particular molecules when appropriate probes or intrinsic fluorophores are available. A structural method may reveal changes in molecular organization that a broad absorption band merely suggests. No single optical signal should be asked to establish abundance, purity, identity, structure and function simultaneously.

This principle can be expressed as a hierarchy of evidence. A wavelength-dependent transmission change is a direct instrumental observation after reference correction. Attribution of that change to a chromophore requires appropriate evidence about sample composition and optical interference. Conversion to concentration requires a coefficient or validated calibration. Interpretation as enzyme activity requires a reaction model, appropriate controls and stoichiometry. Interpretation as a change in biological function requires the experiment to connect the measured reaction to the biological system under consideration. Each step introduces assumptions, and strong experimental reasoning makes those assumptions explicit.

11. Integrating the measurement model: choosing a defensible conclusion

A useful strategy for approaching any examination dataset is to begin by identifying the quantity that was actually measured. A value may represent transmitted power, percent transmittance, absorbance, a difference in absorbance, or a normalized signal. The numerical values cannot be interpreted interchangeably. Next, identify what physical mechanism is proposed to connect the observation to the biological property. A direct assay of a purified molecular absorber differs from a reporter reaction, a two-state equilibrium measurement and a turbidity measurement. Even if all four are performed with a spectrophotometer, the underlying model is not identical.

The following illustrative observations summarize a possible investigation of two purified protein preparations. Preparation P has A₂₈₀ = 0.400 and A₃₄₀ = 0.005. Preparation Q has A₂₈₀ = 0.650 and A₃₄₀ = 0.120. Both are measured in the same buffer with the same path. A student who simply applies the same absorption coefficient at 280 nm will conclude that Q contains more protein. This may be true, but the appreciable longer-wavelength baseline suggests that some of its attenuation may have another origin. It would be premature to attribute the entire 0.250 difference to protein concentration. Inspecting a broader spectrum and examining the sample with complementary methods would clarify whether particles or other absorbers contribute.

If Q and P show comparable electrophoretic band positions, the observation provides information about their molecular sizes under the conditions of that separation. It does not prove equal concentration unless the staining and loading are quantitatively controlled. If a calibrated protein assay gives similar concentrations for P and Q, that result supports the possibility that direct ultraviolet attenuation overestimates the amount in Q. It could also reflect a limitation of the colour assay. The purpose of using different measurements is to establish whether they constrain the same model from independent directions.

Consider a second illustrative dataset: a purified pigment in 1.00 cm and 0.50 cm cells gives absorbances 1.80 and 0.95 at its maximum. The second value is slightly higher than half the first. A shorter path reading scaled to 1 cm gives 1.90. If the same pigment solution measured after a twofold dilution in a 1 cm cell gives A = 0.95, the two lower-attenuation measurements agree after scaling. The original reading may be negatively biased at high absorbance. If instead the diluted solution shifts its peak and produces A = 0.70, while the shorter path preserves the original band shape, dilution may be affecting the pigment's chemical state. Both experiments change the apparent optical depth, but only dilution changes the molecular concentration.

It is helpful to separate diagnostic evidence from confirmatory evidence. An elevated baseline can diagnose an inconsistency with the simplest model of pure molecular absorption but cannot identify the source uniquely. A verified absorbing standard can confirm photometric accuracy at the relevant wavelength and absorbance range, but it cannot confirm the chemical identity of an unknown. A blank can identify optical background, but not necessarily the response of analyte interacting with background components. A set of independent observations with appropriately different sensitivities is generally more convincing than repeated measurements of the same signal.

When choosing a wavelength, consider the desired contrast rather than simply the largest peak. For a single purified species, a large coefficient yields a large calibration slope and may improve sensitivity if the transmitted power remains measurable. For two overlapping absorbers, a wavelength where their relative responses differ greatly may provide better selectivity. In a kinetic assay, the difference in coefficient between reactant and product is the useful signal, not necessarily the magnitude of either coefficient alone. At an isosbestic wavelength the two forms can absorb strongly yet give no signal for their interconversion when the total concentration is constant. The best analytical wavelength is determined by the actual question.

Finally, when comparing conclusions, prefer the one that is directly supported by the supplied data without requiring extra untested assumptions. A changed spectrum can support a changed optical state; it may not establish a specific structural rearrangement. A consistent dilution series can support proportionality within a tested range; it does not establish universal linearity. A normal nucleic acid purity ratio can support the absence of certain gross spectral abnormalities; it cannot establish intact nucleic acid of the desired sequence. A strong calibration fit can support estimation under matched conditions; it does not guarantee accuracy in a different biological matrix. These distinctions are the central reasoning skills that UV–visible spectroscopy contributes to the study of biological methods.

12. Misconceptions and examination traps

Absorbance equals percentage absorbed

A = −log₁₀T, not 100(1−T). Percent readings look linear, but A = 1 leaves 10% transmission. Convert first.

Doubling concentration halves transmission

Under the ideal law, doubling concentration doubles A and squares T. Students confuse a linear absorbance plot with transmission.

All lost light was absorbed

A forward detector also loses scattered light. A cloudy sample with high OD may contain particles rather than a new chromophore. Inspect baseline and geometry.

A280 measures any protein equally

Protein coefficients depend on aromatic residues and cystine. Equal mass can give unequal absorbance; use the relevant coefficient or calibration.

An A260/A280 ratio proves DNA integrity

It screens spectral composition, not fragment length or sequence. A fragmented sample can have a normal ratio; use independent integrity evidence.

All UV cuvettes are glass

Ordinary glass is often unsuitable in UV. Blanking does not restore lost transmission. Choose suitable quartz or certified UV-transparent material.

A blank removes every interference

A blank handles matched background, not unknown interacting species or sample-specific particles. Ask whether it reproduces the relevant matrix.

High R² proves Beer–Lambert behaviour

Curved data can have high R². Inspect residuals and independent checks before accepting the model.

λmax is always optimal

Peak height improves sensitivity but may sacrifice selectivity. For two species or a reaction, relative coefficients or their difference matter.

Isosbestic crossing proves exactly two states

Conserved two-state models can yield a crossing, but additional species may also satisfy the same constraint. Test the whole spectrum and other evidence.

An absorption peak is the emitted colour

Observed colour depends on transmitted or reflected light, not simply the peak absorbed wavelength. Distinguish absorption from emission.

A negative difference spectrum is impossible

A subtraction can be negative where a species disappears; a reference mismatch can also produce negative readings. It does not imply negative molecular absorption.

A short path reading needs correction twice

Some microvolume instruments already normalize to 1 cm. Confirm what the reported value represents before calculating concentration.

All dilution discrepancies are instrumental

Dilution can shift equilibria, change protonation or dissociate aggregates. Compare path-length changes at fixed concentration.

Stray light increases apparent A

Unwanted detected light usually increases T and lowers apparent A for a strongly attenuating sample. Work in power space before taking logs.

A large initial-to-final difference is an initial rate

A long interval may include substrate depletion or drift. Fit the appropriate initial linear interval and use matched controls.

Equal radiant powers mean equal photon numbers

E = hc/λ, so longer wavelength means more photons per second at equal radiant power. Compare photon flux where relevant.

OD600 is a universal cell count

Scattering depends on size, concentration and geometry. A calibration for one preparation or instrument need not transfer to another.

13. Original examination practice

All questions are original teaching exercises. Numerical datasets are illustrative unless otherwise identified.

1. Part B — Which property remains fixed when a monochromatic wave crosses a stationary boundary between transparent media?

  1. Frequency
  2. Wavelength
  3. Speed
  4. Refractive index
Answer and explanation

A. Frequency. The source fixes frequency, and the fields must oscillate continuously at the interface. Speed and wavelength change according to the refractive index; the index belongs to the medium.

2. Part B — Which photon carries the greatest energy in vacuum?

  1. 520 nm
  2. 600 nm
  3. 780 nm
  4. 260 nm
Answer and explanation

D. 260 nm. E = hc/λ. Among the four wavelengths, 260 nm is shortest. Neither beam intensity nor perceived colour changes the energy of an individual photon at a specified wavelength.

3. Part B — What is the absorbance corresponding to 10% transmittance?

  1. 0.100
  2. 0.900
  3. 1.000
  4. 10.000
Answer and explanation

C. 1.000. Convert 10% to T = 0.10, then A = −log₁₀(0.10) = 1. The alternatives confuse transmittance, fraction removed and percentage with the logarithmic quantity.

4. Part B — Which units suit a molar decadic absorption coefficient when c is in mol/L and l in cm?

  1. mol L⁻¹ cm⁻¹
  2. L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹
  3. cm mol L⁻¹
  4. L g⁻¹ cm⁻¹
Answer and explanation

B. L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. εcl must be dimensionless. L g⁻¹ cm⁻¹ is instead a mass-based coefficient; the other alternatives do not cancel concentration and distance units.

5. Part B — Which molecular feature commonly accounts for strong UV absorption near 260 nm in nucleic acids?

  1. Phosphate group rotation alone
  2. Electronic transitions of nucleotide bases
  3. Long-range scattering by individual hydrogen bonds
  4. Infrared vibration of ribose alone
Answer and explanation

B. Electronic transitions of nucleotide bases. The aromatic heterocyclic bases contain electronic chromophores. The sugar-phosphate framework and hydrogen bonds contribute to structure but are not the principal explanation for the characteristic 260 nm band.

6. Part B — What does a bathochromic shift mean?

  1. A band moves to longer wavelength
  2. A band becomes taller at a fixed wavelength
  3. A band moves to higher frequency
  4. A band loses all intensity
Answer and explanation

A. A band moves to longer wavelength. Bathochromic means red shift, toward longer λ and lower ν. Hyperchromicity describes increased intensity, not a shift; movement to shorter wavelength is hypsochromic.

7. Part B — Which pair contributes most commonly to intrinsic protein absorption near 280 nm?

  1. Glycine and alanine
  2. Aspartate and glutamate
  3. Lysine and arginine alone
  4. Tryptophan and tyrosine
Answer and explanation

D. Tryptophan and tyrosine. The aromatic side chains of tryptophan and tyrosine provide major contributions at 280 nm. Cystine can contribute weakly. The listed nonaromatic residues lack equivalent strong bands in this region.

8. Part B — Which material is generally appropriate for conventional absorbance measurements near 260 nm?

  1. Ordinary window glass
  2. Opaque ceramic
  3. Fused silica (quartz)
  4. Unspecified coloured plastic
Answer and explanation

C. Fused silica (quartz). Fused silica transmits the relevant ultraviolet radiation. Ordinary glass or arbitrary plastic may absorb it strongly, and opaque material cannot provide a useful transmitted beam.

9. Part B — Which optical component most directly separates wavelengths in a typical grating monochromator?

  1. Sample cuvette
  2. Photomultiplier dynode
  3. Diffraction grating
  4. Reference solution
Answer and explanation

C. Diffraction grating. The grating disperses the incident radiation by interference. The cuvette holds the solution, the reference defines the baseline, and dynodes amplify detector electrons.

10. Part B — What is the principal purpose of a matched reagent blank?

  1. To increase the analyte concentration
  2. To account for optical background of reagents without analyte
  3. To fix every sample-specific interaction
  4. To replace the calibration standards
Answer and explanation

B. To account for optical background of reagents without analyte. A matched blank establishes background transmission or absorbance. It does not ensure selectivity against unknown interferents, replace standards or alter the intended concentration.

11. Part B — Which statement about a double-beam instrument is correct?

  1. It can compensate for common source fluctuations
  2. It proves all chemical equilibria are constant
  3. It eliminates the need for references
  4. It measures absolute concentration without calibration
Answer and explanation

A. It can compensate for common source fluctuations. The sample/reference ratio reduces common-mode source drift. It still requires comparable paths and an appropriate reference and does not determine chemical identity.

12. Part B — Which signal is characteristic of reduced NADH compared with oxidized NAD⁺?

  1. Exclusive absorption near 600 nm
  2. A unique X-ray diffraction peak at 340 nm
  3. No absorption anywhere in the ultraviolet
  4. Absorption near 340 nm
Answer and explanation

D. Absorption near 340 nm. Reduction of the nicotinamide group produces a useful absorption band near 340 nm. Both forms also have ultraviolet absorption elsewhere; the method is not diffraction.

13. Part B — What does hyperchromicity describe in a spectrum?

  1. An increase in wavelength alone
  2. A decrease in incident radiant power alone
  3. A decrease in absorption intensity
  4. An increase in absorption intensity under comparable conditions
Answer and explanation

D. An increase in absorption intensity under comparable conditions. Hyperchromicity describes increased absorption. A wavelength shift is a different spectral property, and a change in the lamp power is not necessarily a molecular change.

14. Part B — In the ideal Beer–Lambert model, which quantity is linear in path length at fixed concentration?

  1. Transmittance
  2. Percent transmittance
  3. Absorbance
  4. Fraction of photons remaining
Answer and explanation

C. Absorbance. A = εcl is linear in l. Transmission instead follows T = 10⁻ᴬ and decays exponentially with path length.

15. Part B — What is the appropriate interpretation of an isosbestic wavelength for an ideal conserved two-state system?

  1. Both forms are necessarily transparent there
  2. The two forms have equal coefficients there
  3. The reaction proceeds fastest there
  4. The concentration of each form is necessarily equal
Answer and explanation

B. The two forms have equal coefficients there. At that wavelength εX = εY, so the total absorption remains unchanged as composition changes. Neither zero absorption nor equal populations is required, and wavelength is not a reaction-rate measurement.

16. Part B — Which quantity is plotted on the vertical axis of an ordinary absorption spectrum?

  1. Absorbance
  2. Molecular weight
  3. pH regardless of wavelength
  4. Time regardless of wavelength
Answer and explanation

A. Absorbance. An absorption spectrum displays A as a function of spectral variable, often wavelength. Kinetic traces use time, whereas pH titrations may use pH.

17. Part B — Which factor most directly defines calibration sensitivity in a linear concentration assay?

  1. Slope of absorbance versus concentration
  2. Number of samples only
  3. Length of the experiment only
  4. The intercept alone
Answer and explanation

A. Slope of absorbance versus concentration. Sensitivity is the incremental response per concentration increment. A high slope may improve discrimination of concentration differences but does not automatically imply selectivity or accuracy.

18. Part B — Why can OD600 be positive for particles with negligible visible absorption?

  1. The particles necessarily emit red photons
  2. The logarithm creates light
  3. The optical path length is always zero
  4. They scatter light away from the forward detector
Answer and explanation

D. They scatter light away from the forward detector. Scattering reduces detected forward power, producing apparent absorbance. No molecular absorption or emission is required for this attenuation.

19. Part B — What is the effect of extra unattenuated stray light at high true absorbance?

  1. Apparent absorbance is always infinitely high
  2. True transmittance becomes negative
  3. Apparent absorbance is usually too low
  4. The molar mass increases
Answer and explanation

C. Apparent absorbance is usually too low. Additional detected power increases the measured transmittance, reducing −log₁₀T. The effect becomes important when the correct transmitted power is weak.

20. Part B — What does a normal A260/A280 ratio by itself establish?

  1. Exact sequence identity
  2. Only consistency with an expected spectral ratio
  3. Absence of every contaminant
  4. Complete molecular integrity
Answer and explanation

B. Only consistency with an expected spectral ratio. A ratio summarizes two optical readings. Different mixtures can share the ratio, and fragmentation or incorrect identity need independent analysis.

21. Part B — Which optical signal directly represents a time-dependent enzyme assay rate when the reporter coefficient is known?

  1. The absolute wavelength setting
  2. Slope dA/dt with the appropriate sign and conversion
  3. The first reading divided by molecular mass
  4. The blank concentration alone
Answer and explanation

B. Slope dA/dt with the appropriate sign and conversion. dA/dt = εl dc/dt for a single relevant reporter under fixed conditions. Converting to amount rate also requires reaction volume and stoichiometry.

22. Part B — What does an absorbance calibration intercept describe in a simple empirical linear model?

  1. Predicted signal at zero analyte concentration
  2. Molar mass of the analyte
  3. Maximum possible absorbance
  4. Detector wavelength resolution
Answer and explanation

A. Predicted signal at zero analyte concentration. In A = b + mc, b is the zero-concentration predicted response; it can represent a background contribution. The slope, not intercept, reflects incremental sensitivity.

23. Part B — Which condition supports independent absorbance additivity for two solutes?

  1. Their peak wavelengths must be identical
  2. Their concentrations must be equal
  3. Their transmittances must add directly
  4. Their optical properties remain unchanged on mixing
Answer and explanation

D. Their optical properties remain unchanged on mixing. A = l(εXcX + εYcY) follows when attenuation contributions are independent. Equal peaks or concentrations are unnecessary; successive transmitted fractions multiply rather than add.

24. Part B — What distinguishes an action spectrum from an absorption spectrum?

  1. It necessarily plots molar absorption coefficients
  2. It always proves receptor identity
  3. It plots a defined biological or photochemical response against wavelength
  4. It measures only emitted fluorescence
Answer and explanation

C. It plots a defined biological or photochemical response against wavelength. An action spectrum characterizes a specified outcome under defined illumination; an absorption spectrum describes optical absorption. Similarity may support but does not prove causal identity.

25. Part C — A solution has A = 0.300 in a 1 cm cell. Its concentration is tripled and measured in a 0.5 cm cell without changing ε. What is the new transmittance?

  1. Approximately 0.150
  2. Approximately 0.450
  3. Approximately 0.355
  4. Approximately 0.501
Answer and explanation

C. Approximately 0.355. New A = 0.300 × 3 × 0.5 = 0.450. Thus T = 10⁻⁰·⁴⁵ ≈ 0.355. The alternatives confuse absorbance with transmission or omit the path/concentration change.

26. Part C — An instrument reports the actual absorbance 0.150 in a 0.20 cm path for a fivefold dilution. If ε = 15,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, what was the original concentration?

  1. 50 µM
  2. 250 µM
  3. 10 µM
  4. 1000 µM
Answer and explanation

B. 250 µM. The diluted concentration is 0.150/(15,000 × 0.20) = 50 µM. Multiplying by five gives 250 µM. Using a 1 cm path or failing to correct dilution gives smaller values.

27. Part C — A 1 cm cell yields A260 = 0.240 for a tenfold diluted dsDNA sample. Use 50 µg/mL per unit of A260. What is the original mass in 80 µL?

  1. 9.6 µg
  2. 0.96 µg
  3. 120 µg
  4. 96 µg
Answer and explanation

A. 9.6 µg. The original concentration is 0.240 × 50 × 10 = 120 µg/mL. Volume 80 µL is 0.080 mL, so the yield is 9.6 µg. Concentration and total mass are distinct.

28. Part C — Two solutes have coefficients (in absorbance per µM at 1 cm): X (0.03, 0.01), Y (0.01, 0.02). A₁ = 0.35 and A₂ = 0.20. Find cX and cY.

  1. 5 µM, 10 µM
  2. 7 µM, 14 µM
  3. 15 µM, 0 µM
  4. 10 µM, 5 µM
Answer and explanation

D. 10 µM, 5 µM. Equations are 3X + Y = 35 and X + 2Y = 20 after scaling by 100. Substitution gives X = 10 and Y = 5. Both absorbances check: 0.30+0.05 and 0.10+0.10.

29. Part C — Two candidate wavelengths give coefficient vectors (10, 20) and (15, 30). Why is the pair inadequate for resolving two unknown components?

  1. Both wavelengths have zero signal
  2. The coefficients have different absolute values
  3. Both concentrations must be negative
  4. The vectors are proportional, so the equations are dependent
Answer and explanation

D. The vectors are proportional, so the equations are dependent. The second vector is 1.5 times the first; the determinant is zero. Many mixtures produce the same pair of readings. Strong absolute signals cannot compensate for a lack of independent spectral contrast.

30. Part C — A calibration gives A = 0.040 + 0.025c, c in µM. A threefold diluted unknown has A = 0.215. What is the original c?

  1. 7.0 µM
  2. 25.8 µM
  3. 21.0 µM
  4. 8.6 µM
Answer and explanation

C. 21.0 µM. Subtract the intercept: (0.215−0.040)/0.025 = 7.0 µM in the diluted sample. Multiply by three. Ignoring the intercept gives 8.6 µM diluted or 25.8 µM original.

31. Part C — At high concentration, A is 1.70 in a 1 cm cell and 0.95 in a 0.5 cm cell. A twofold dilution in a 1 cm cell gives 0.95 with unchanged spectral shape. Which inference is best?

  1. Dilution necessarily changes molecular species
  2. High-attenuation measurement may underestimate A; both low-attenuation tests agree
  3. The short cell has zero path length
  4. The concentration is exactly halved by shortening the cell
Answer and explanation

B. High-attenuation measurement may underestimate A; both low-attenuation tests agree. The shorter path and dilution both reduce optical depth and predict 1.90 scaled to the original conditions. Their agreement suggests optical nonlinearity at high A, though it does not uniquely identify the cause.

32. Part C — A sample's peak shifts after dilution, but an equal optical-depth reduction by a shorter path at unchanged concentration leaves the peak unchanged. What hypothesis gains support?

  1. A concentration-dependent molecular equilibrium
  2. A fixed wavelength-independent electronic dark offset as the only cause
  3. The path length changes the number of solutes per unit volume
  4. An identical spectrum at every concentration
Answer and explanation

A. A concentration-dependent molecular equilibrium. Dilution changes molecular concentration whereas the shorter cell does not. A shift unique to dilution is evidence for altered composition or environment, though a full control of matrix and wavelength is still needed.

33. Part C — At λ₁ pure X has A = 0.20 and pure Y A = 0.80, with the same total concentration. A mixture has A = 0.50. At λ₂ both pure forms have A = 0.40. Which result is predicted?

  1. 50% Y and A₂ = 0.40
  2. 25% Y and A₂ = 0.10
  3. 75% Y and A₂ = 0.60
  4. 50% Y and A₂ = 0.20
Answer and explanation

A. 50% Y and A₂ = 0.40. The fraction Y is (0.50−0.20)/(0.80−0.20)=0.5. At λ₂ both forms contribute the same coefficient, so conserved total concentration yields A₂ = 0.40 regardless of fraction.

34. Part C — For a two-state acid indicator, AHA = 0.10, AA⁻ = 0.90, measured A = 0.70, pKa = 6.0. Under ideal assumptions estimate pH.

  1. 5.52
  2. 6.00
  3. 7.00
  4. 6.48
Answer and explanation

D. 6.48. The deprotonated fraction is (0.70−0.10)/0.80=0.75. A⁻/HA = 0.75/0.25=3. Henderson–Hasselbalch gives 6+log₁₀3 ≈ 6.48. The lower value reverses the ratio.

35. Part C — A sample has true A = 3.00. A fixed stray fraction s = 0.001 enters sample and reference channels, Tobs = (T+s)/(1+s). Estimate observed A.

  1. 3.301
  2. 3.000
  3. 2.699
  4. 1.000
Answer and explanation

C. 2.699. Ttrue = 0.001; Tobs = 0.002/1.001 ≈ 0.001998. Aobs ≈ 2.699. Extra detected radiation lowers apparent absorbance rather than raising it.

36. Part C — Two equally weighted wavelengths produce ideal A values 0 and 2 for the same solution. A broadband detector combines transmitted powers. What apparent A results?

  1. 1.000
  2. Approximately 0.297
  3. 2.000
  4. 0.505
Answer and explanation

B. Approximately 0.297. Mean transmitted fraction = (1+0.01)/2 = 0.505, so A = −log₁₀(0.505) ≈ 0.297. Averaging the two absorbances is not appropriate before taking the logarithm.

37. Part C — Reference, sample and dark detector signals are 1250, 470 and 50 units. Assuming linear response, what is A?

  1. 0.425
  2. 0.456
  3. 0.350
  4. 0.650
Answer and explanation

B. 0.456. Corrected T = (470−50)/(1250−50) = 420/1200 = 0.35. A ≈ 0.456. Ignoring dark signal gives about 0.425, and 0.35 is transmission, not absorbance.

38. Part C — A stable reference was recorded before a lamp brightened by 20%. A later sample has true T = 0.25. What A would a sequential single-beam reading report using the old reference?

  1. 0.523
  2. 0.602
  3. 0.699
  4. 0.250
Answer and explanation

A. 0.523. Relative to the old reference, apparent T = 1.20 × 0.25 = 0.30. A = 0.523; the correct contemporary A is 0.602. Source drift is not an analyte change.

39. Part C — A reporter reaction consumes NADH; A340 falls 0.1866 per minute, l = 1 cm, ε = 6220 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Reaction volume 2 mL, 0.2 mg enzyme. Find specific activity assuming 1:1 stoichiometry.

  1. 30.0 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
  2. 0.060 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
  3. 0.150 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
  4. 0.300 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
Answer and explanation

D. 0.300 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹. Rate = 0.1866/6220 = 30 µM/min. In 0.002 L, amount rate is 0.060 µmol/min. Dividing by 0.2 mg gives 0.300. The concentration rate is not the amount rate.

40. Part C — A coupled assay consumes two NADH molecules per target reaction. The enzyme-containing slope is −0.1244 A/min and control slope −0.0622 A/min. At 1 cm and ε = 6220, what is target concentration rate?

  1. 10.0 µM/min
  2. 20.0 µM/min
  3. 5.0 µM/min
  4. 30.0 µM/min
Answer and explanation

C. 5.0 µM/min. Subtract the background magnitude: 0.0622 A/min = 10 µM/min NADH consumption. Two NADH per target event gives 5 µM/min. Omitting background or stoichiometry overestimates the target rate.

41. Part C — A folded protein has 2 Trp, 4 Tyr and one disulfide. Use the Pace coefficients. With A280 = 0.3417 and l = 1 cm, estimate its concentration.

  1. 10 µM
  2. 17.1 µM
  3. 20 µM
  4. 40 µM
Answer and explanation

C. 20 µM. ε = 2×5500 + 4×1490 + 125 = 17,085. c = 0.3417/17,085 = 2×10⁻⁵ M. Count one cystine pair, not two independent cysteines.

42. Part C — A homodimer of 40 kDa subunits has ε280 = 20,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ per subunit, with independent optical contributions. For A = 0.400 at 1 cm, what is the dimer concentration and mass concentration?

  1. 20 µM and 1.60 mg/mL
  2. 10 µM and 0.80 mg/mL
  3. 10 µM and 0.40 mg/mL
  4. 40 µM and 0.80 mg/mL
Answer and explanation

B. 10 µM and 0.80 mg/mL. The dimer coefficient is 40,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, giving 10 µM dimers. Dimer molar mass is 80,000 g/mol, so mass concentration is 0.80 g/L = 0.80 mg/mL.

43. Part C — A sample has A260 = 0.180 and A280 = 0.100; another has A260 = 0.018 and A280 = 0.010. Both ratios are 1.8. If the absolute absorbance uncertainty is identical, which is more robust?

  1. The first ratio, because its signals are larger relative to the error
  2. The second, because it is more dilute
  3. Both equally, because the ratios are equal
  4. Neither has any uncertainty
Answer and explanation

A. The first ratio, because its signals are larger relative to the error. Ratios propagate errors from numerator and denominator. The smaller denominator makes the second ratio more sensitive to the same absolute reading error; equal ratios do not imply equal reliability.

44. Part C — A protein preparation has elevated absorbance at 280 nm and also at 450 nm, where the purified protein is known to have negligible absorption. Which is the strongest interpretation?

  1. Protein concentration must have doubled
  2. All aromatic residues disappeared
  3. The sample necessarily contains exactly one contaminant
  4. Additional attenuation or an interferent may contribute; concentration alone is not established
Answer and explanation

D. Additional attenuation or an interferent may contribute; concentration alone is not established. An unexpected long-wavelength baseline suggests non-target attenuation, including scattering. It cannot uniquely identify a specific contaminant or prove the protein amount from A280 alone.

45. Part C — A plate-reader standard and unknown have the same concentration and coefficient. Their effective path lengths are 0.40 and 0.60 cm. If standard A = 0.200, what does the ideal model predict for the unknown?

  1. 0.133
  2. 0.200
  3. 0.600
  4. 0.300
Answer and explanation

D. 0.300. At fixed ε and c, A scales with l. 0.200 × 0.60/0.40 = 0.300. Equal concentration does not imply equal absorbance if the geometry differs.

46. Part C — An absorbance calibration gives a blank SD of 0.004 and slope 0.020 per µM. Under an illustrative 3σ and 10σ convention, what are the detection and quantification estimates?

  1. 0.20 and 0.60 µM
  2. 2.00 and 0.60 µM
  3. 0.60 and 2.00 µM
  4. 0.012 and 0.040 µM
Answer and explanation

C. 0.60 and 2.00 µM. Divide signal thresholds by the slope: 3×0.004/0.020=0.60 µM; 10×0.004/0.020=2.00 µM. Signal thresholds without slope conversion are in absorbance units.

47. Part C — A standard addition sample gives A = 0.30 before spiking and 0.50 after 4 µM added, at unchanged final volume. An independent control reveals an additive interferent of A = 0.10. What analyte concentration is consistent with the model?

  1. 6.0 µM
  2. 4.0 µM
  3. 10.0 µM
  4. 2.0 µM
Answer and explanation

B. 4.0 µM. Incremental slope = (0.50−0.30)/4 = 0.05 per µM. Remove the additive interference from the original signal: (0.30−0.10)/0.05 = 4 µM. Attributing the whole intercept to analyte gives 6 µM.

48. Part C — A DNA melting experiment has baseline absorbances 0.46 and 0.70 at the temperature of interest, and measured A = 0.64. In a two-state model, estimate the melted fraction.

  1. 0.75
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.91
  4. 0.50
Answer and explanation

A. 0.75. Fraction = (0.64−0.46)/(0.70−0.46) = 0.18/0.24 = 0.75. Dividing by the upper absolute absorbance does not account for the duplex baseline.

49. SET / integrated — Assertion: Equal radiant power at 500 and 250 nm means twice as many photons per second at 500 nm. Reason: Photon energy is inversely proportional to vacuum wavelength.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion. Photon rate = power/E. At twice the wavelength each photon has half the energy, so twice as many photons deliver equal power. The reason is directly causal.

50. SET / integrated — Assertion: A double-beam spectrophotometer eliminates all matrix effects. Reason: A double-beam design can compensate for common-mode lamp drift.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

D. Assertion false; reason true. Optical comparison compensates some source variations but does not remove sample-specific chemical interactions, scattering or mismatched reference composition.

51. SET / integrated — Assertion: A spectrum can show negative values after subtracting an initial spectrum from a final spectrum. Reason: A molecule may disappear during a reaction, lowering absorption at its characteristic wavelength.

  1. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  2. Assertion true; reason false
  3. Both true; reason explains assertion
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

C. Both true; reason explains assertion. The difference A(final)−A(initial) can be negative when a contributing absorber is consumed. This does not violate the nonnegative absolute absorption of a passive molecular species.

52. SET / integrated — Which combination is valid? I. Quartz is generally suitable for UV. II. A260/A280 proves DNA sequence. III. High A can be biased downward by stray light. IV. Scattering can reduce forward transmission.

  1. I and II only
  2. I, III and IV
  3. II and III only
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

B. I, III and IV. I, III and IV are valid optical statements. II is invalid because a ratio cannot establish sequence identity.

53. SET / integrated — An illustrative assay yields mean concentration 5.0 µM from four repeated readings of one cuvette. Independent sample preparations were not made. Which inference is justified?

  1. Four independent biological replicates exist
  2. Short-term measurement repeatability may be assessed; biological variation is not established
  3. Accuracy is guaranteed by agreement
  4. The mean is the true concentration without uncertainty
Answer and explanation

B. Short-term measurement repeatability may be assessed; biological variation is not established. Repeated readings of one preparation quantify only certain instrumental or technical variation. Accuracy and biological variance require independent evidence.

54. SET / integrated — An unknown's absorbance is within the calibration range, but its buffer contains an additional coloured substance not present in standards. What is the best next analytical decision?

  1. Test selectivity and matrix matching before using the calibration
  2. Assume interpolation removes matrix bias
  3. Force the calibration through zero
  4. Use the largest possible path length regardless of signal
Answer and explanation

A. Test selectivity and matrix matching before using the calibration. Interpolation is valid only for comparable response conditions. A coloured matrix can contribute additive absorption or change the analyte response; a matched blank, matrix study or separate analytical method may be needed.

14. Revision and mastery

UV–visible analysis measures the wavelength-dependent loss of transmitted radiation relative to a reference. Molecular electronic transitions generate absorption bands; concentration, path length, molecular state and unwanted attenuation all influence the observed spectrum. Interpret measurements with explicit assumptions, matched blanks and validated calibrations. Quantitative biological conclusions require appropriate chemical specificity, stoichiometry and independent controls.

Important terminology

Chromophore: group responsible for absorption; molar absorption coefficient: wavelength-dependent absorbance per concentration and path; calibration: relationship between response and known amounts; blank: reference matrix without the analyte; scattering: redirection of light; isosbestic point: wavelength of common absorbance in specified changing spectra; selectivity: ability to distinguish the target response; sensitivity: change of response per change in analyte amount.

Active recall

  1. Derive the absorbance relation from differential power loss through a homogeneous medium.
  2. Explain why doubling concentration squares ideal transmittance.
  3. Convert between percent transmittance and absorbance without confusing the two scales.
  4. Explain how conjugation can shift electronic absorption bands.
  5. Distinguish hyperchromicity from a red shift and describe a possible biological example.
  6. Calculate protein concentration from a sequence-based molar absorption coefficient.
  7. Describe why A260/A280 cannot establish sequence or molecular integrity.
  8. Explain how two independent wavelengths can resolve a two-component mixture.
  9. Compare single-beam, double-beam and diode-array optical layouts.
  10. Describe how a blank and calibration intercept affect an unknown concentration estimate.
  11. Explain why high absorbance is especially vulnerable to stray light.
  12. Design a path-length versus dilution comparison to distinguish chemical and instrumental nonlinearity.
  13. Convert an NADH absorbance slope to amount rate and specific activity.
  14. Explain how scattering can alter an apparent absorption spectrum.
  15. Describe the evidential limitations of an isosbestic point.

Mastery checklist

  • Explain photon energy, chromophores and electronic transitions in connected causal language.
  • Derive the Beer–Lambert relationship and state each assumption.
  • Interpret spectral shapes, normalized spectra and mixture coefficients.
  • Distinguish optical and chemical causes of calibration nonlinearity.
  • Calculate concentrations, dilution factors, total yields and enzyme rates with correct units.
  • Choose matched controls and distinguish concentration evidence from structural evidence.

Connections with other chapters

Chemical bonding and biomolecular structure provide the origin of electronic transitions; enzyme kinetics supplies the reaction model; sample preparation and separation address interfering species. Continue with fluorescence spectroscopy and circular dichroism in 14.B.02, where emission and differential absorption provide different molecular information. Revision strategy

First derive the logarithmic relationship without notes. Next work a dilution, a two-component mixture and a rate calculation. Finally compare competing explanations for an unexpectedly curved calibration or changing spectral shape. Review errors by identifying which assumption failed, rather than memorizing the numerical answer.
