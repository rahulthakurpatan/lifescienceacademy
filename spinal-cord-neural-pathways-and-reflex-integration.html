1. The spinal cord as a regional integrator

A moving animal needs to solve two problems simultaneously. It must transmit information between distant parts of its body and the brain, and it must organise movements rapidly enough to respond to changes in its immediate surroundings. The vertebrate spinal cord provides both long-distance communication and local computation. A hand can begin withdrawing from a damaging stimulus through spinal circuitry before the full conscious experience of pain develops. Conversely, a carefully planned movement depends on signals descending from the brain and on continuous information arriving from muscles, joints and skin. Neither example requires the spinal cord to act as a passive cable. In both, its neurons select, combine and transform signals into an organised pattern of muscle activity.

The spinal cord is a continuous part of the central nervous system, extending caudally from the medulla through the vertebral canal. The vertebral column provides mechanical protection, while meninges and cerebrospinal fluid surround the cord. The outer dura is separated from the vertebral canal by an epidural space; the arachnoid and pia lie nearer the cord, with cerebrospinal fluid in the subarachnoid space. These anatomical relationships distinguish the protected neural tissue from the bones surrounding it. A vertebral fracture and a spinal cord lesion are therefore not synonymous, although mechanical displacement can damage nervous tissue.

In adult humans the cord usually ends near the first or second lumbar vertebral level, forming the conus medullaris. The lower spinal nerve roots descend within the canal before reaching their intervertebral exit points. Together these long roots form the cauda equina. The arrangement reflects unequal growth of the vertebral column and spinal cord during development. As a result, a lumbar spinal cord segment is not necessarily opposite the lumbar vertebra bearing the corresponding number. The neurological level of a lesion must be described in terms of neural segments and pathways rather than inferred mechanically from the name of a nearby bone.

Thirty-one pairs of human spinal nerves are conventionally divided into eight cervical, twelve thoracic, five lumbar, five sacral and one coccygeal pair. There are seven cervical vertebrae but eight cervical spinal nerve pairs. The eighth cervical nerve exits between the seventh cervical and first thoracic vertebrae. Spinal segments are defined by their root attachments, not by equal lengths of neural tissue. The cervical and lumbosacral enlargements contain additional neuronal populations associated with limb innervation. The enlargements are a structural consequence of the need to organise more complicated sensory and motor interactions rather than merely an increase in the diameter of passing axons.

A transverse section reveals a central region of grey matter surrounded by white matter. Grey matter contains many neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses and glia; white matter is enriched in axons, many of which are myelinated, together with supporting cells. These are relative descriptions rather than exclusive rules. Axons occur within grey matter and not every axon in white matter is myelinated. The central canal is a narrow ventricular remnant lined by ependymal cells. It is surrounded by grey matter that forms a bridge between the two sides of the spinal cord. A separate anterior white commissure carries axons crossing the midline.

The distinction between segment and tract is fundamental. A segment supplies an anatomical relationship to a particular pair of spinal nerves. A tract is a collection of central axons with related courses and functions; it can extend across numerous segments. A single spinal segment therefore contains both local circuits and fibres carrying information from other regions. A lesion of local grey matter may interrupt particular segmental responses while sparing some long-range communication. A lesion restricted to a white matter tract may change function far below the damaged level even when local motor neurons survive. Predicting the result requires identifying which cell bodies or axons are actually affected.

The spinal cord of a mammal is bilateral, but the two halves communicate extensively. Each dorsal horn receives sensory inputs from its own side, and each ventral horn contains motor neurons whose peripheral axons generally supply muscles on that same side. Commissural interneurons can transmit excitation or inhibition across the midline. Their connections provide a way for a stimulus on one limb to change the activity of the other limb. Long ascending and descending tracts also cross at different positions in the nervous system. Laterality is therefore a property of an entire specified pathway, not an intrinsic property of the words sensory or motor.

Longitudinal and regional organisation

The geometry of the spinal cord changes along its length for two independent reasons. The number of local neurons changes according to the tasks assigned to a region, and the number of fibres in transit changes according to how many body regions remain to be served. An ascending tract tends to gain fibres as sensory input from successive levels joins it. A descending tract tends to lose fibres as branches leave to influence spinal neurons. Therefore a cervical section generally has a relatively larger proportion of white matter than a sacral section. It carries communication between the brain and much of the body, whereas a sacral section is concerned with more restricted regions. This is an expectation based on the cumulative organisation of the pathways rather than a method for calculating the exact number of axons in a histological section.

The shape of the grey matter is also informative. At a limb enlargement, many motor pools must supply muscles acting around multiple joints, while numerous sensory inputs must be processed in parallel. A thoracic segment has a different set of demands, including trunk motor activity and autonomic control. The relatively small ventral horns in many thoracic sections do not indicate that their motor neurons are intrinsically less excitable. The size of a horn mainly reflects the distribution and number of its neural elements. A section can therefore contain clues about its level, but assigning an exact segment would require additional anatomical information.

When examining a section, first identify the anterior median fissure, which is generally deeper than the posterior median sulcus. Next identify the central grey region and the surrounding white region. Trace the dorsal horns towards the posterior surface and the broader ventral horns towards the anterior surface. This orientation prevents an error that propagates through every subsequent pathway prediction. In particular, dorsal and ventral are anatomical positions, whereas ascending and descending refer to the course of axons along the longitudinal axis. A fibre in the dorsal part of the section may ascend towards the brain, but it does not ascend simply because the dorsal surface happens to be drawn at the top of a diagram.

The white matter is conventionally divided into posterior, lateral and anterior funiculi. Each funiculus contains several pathways rather than representing a single fibre bundle with one function. The lateral funiculus, for example, includes important descending motor and ascending sensory components. Its injury cannot be interpreted as necessarily a loss of only movement or only sensation. A tract diagram usually highlights a selected pathway while leaving neighbouring axons unlabelled. Students should not assume that the empty region in a schematic section contains no neural tissue. The diagram represents a selection of the anatomy, not the entire anatomy.

Local connections are distributed over more than a single transverse plane. A propriospinal neuron has an axon that connects spinal levels without travelling all the way to the brain. Some intersegmental connections are short and others extend farther. Their role can be appreciated by considering a movement of the elbow: neurons serving the shoulder and hand may also need to adjust their activity. If spinal segments behaved as isolated units, motor pools at different levels could not easily share rapidly arriving information. Intersegmental circuitry allows one afferent input to influence multiple pools while descending pathways provide broader coordination.

A long axon travelling within the cord is supported by glia and depends on metabolic exchange with the surrounding tissue. Oligodendrocytes form central myelin, in contrast to the Schwann cells that form peripheral myelin on eligible axons. A single sensory neuron can thus have one branch that belongs to the peripheral environment and another that enters the central environment. This observation makes the CNS–PNS boundary a boundary of anatomical location and supporting tissue, not necessarily a boundary between two different neurons. A change in conduction at either branch could alter the arrival time of the same afferent signal.

The cord is also vascularised and metabolically active. Neuronal firing and synaptic signalling depend on ionic gradients maintained by cellular metabolism. A selective disturbance of blood supply may affect a group of spinal structures because of their vascular territory rather than because they belong to the same physiological pathway. It is therefore important to distinguish experimentally imposed selective tract lesions from the broader effects of ischaemia. The simplified pathway predictions in this chapter assume the injury is restricted as stated. They should not be treated as diagnostic instructions for identifying the cause of an actual patient's symptoms.

Comparative and histological perspectives

An important comparative perspective is that the spinal cord retains a segmental plan even when the animal's mode of locomotion changes. In a limbless vertebrate, coordinated activity of muscles distributed along the trunk is essential for propulsion. In a quadrupedal mammal, limb motor pools must also coordinate flexion, extension and changes in support across joints. In both cases the nervous system requires communication between segments and between the two sides of the body. The detailed configuration of the circuitry depends on the organism's anatomy, but the distinction between regional sensory entry, local integration and efferent output remains useful. Human segment numbers should not be projected onto all vertebrates.

The central canal and commissures provide particularly useful reference points when interpreting spinal diagrams. The canal is a small lumen, whereas the grey commissure consists of nervous tissue surrounding it. Neither structure is a peripheral nerve. The anterior white commissure is located anterior to the grey commissure and contains crossing axons. It is important in reasoning about the anterolateral sensory pathway because interruption of the fibres during their crossing can affect information originating on both sides. This differs from injury to an already assembled ascending tract, where many fibres from a particular side of the body run together. The geometric distinction becomes a physiological distinction when a particular site is selectively damaged.

One can also distinguish gross anatomy from the cellular organisation underlying it. A grey horn is a visible region, not a single functional unit. Its neurons have different axonal targets and different patterns of input. In the dorsal horn, for instance, some neurons contribute to local processing while others project over long distances. The ventral horn contains populations whose axons leave the spinal cord as well as local cells that help control those populations. Histological lamination is one way of describing regional organisation, but a learner does not need to memorise every lamina to understand these fundamental relationships. Instead, follow the named neuronal class and its known connections whenever a diagram or experimental question provides them.

2. Grey horns, roots and spinal nerves

The dorsal horn is a receiving and processing region for sensory input. The axons of primary sensory neurons enter through the dorsal roots and may terminate on local neurons, send branches into ascending pathways, or do both. The ventral horn contains lower motor neurons that send axons through the ventral roots. In the thoracic and upper lumbar cord an intermediate lateral region contains sympathetic preganglionic neurons; parasympathetic preganglionic neurons occur in the sacral cord. These autonomic populations are distinct from the somatic alpha motor neurons that directly activate ordinary skeletal muscle fibres.

The sensory neuron cell bodies serving the spinal cord usually reside outside the central nervous system in dorsal root ganglia. Most are pseudounipolar: a single process from the cell body branches into a peripheral process and a central process. The peripheral branch conveys activity initiated at or associated with a receptor, while the central branch enters the spinal cord. Although the soma supports both branches metabolically, it is not normally a chemical synaptic relay between them. A single impulse can propagate through the branch junction along the same neuron. Confusing the ganglion with a synaptic station adds a fictitious neuron to the sensory limb of a reflex.

Near the cord, the dorsal and ventral roots are functionally distinct. The dorsal root carries afferent axons into the central nervous system and the ventral root carries efferent axons out of it. After their union, the short spinal nerve contains both components. The spinal nerve divides into dorsal and ventral rami, which are generally mixed. A dorsal ramus is not a dorsal root: it supplies structures such as the deep muscles and skin of the back through both motor and sensory fibres. Ventral rami serve the anterolateral body and limbs, and many participate in nerve plexuses. Root and ramus names must therefore be interpreted at their correct positions in the branching tree.

A dermatome is an area of skin supplied predominantly by sensory fibres associated with one spinal root. Adjacent dermatomes overlap, so an isolated root lesion may diminish sensation without producing complete anaesthesia across its entire mapped area. A myotome refers to a group of muscles or movements associated predominantly with one spinal segment. Many muscles receive motor axons from more than one segment, and peripheral nerves often combine fibres originating from multiple roots. These anatomical arrangements explain why the territory of a peripheral nerve differs from the territory of an individual spinal root. They also caution against treating simplified textbook maps as exact boundaries for every individual.

The classical separation of sensory and motor roots is associated with the work of Charles Bell and François Magendie in the early nineteenth century. Their investigations established an enduring functional principle of spinal organisation, but the historical priority and interpretation of their respective contributions are more nuanced than assigning the entire discovery to one experiment. The decisive physiological logic is that selective interference with an input route and selective interference with an output route produce different effects. A sensory root interruption prevents the relevant peripheral information reaching central circuits. A motor root interruption prevents the relevant motor command reaching its effector. Neither result can be inferred merely from the appearance of nerve tissue.

Consider a theoretical isolated preparation with a receptor, one sensory root and one motor root contributing to a simple reflex. Interrupt the sensory root while leaving the motor axon and muscle excitable. The receptor may still generate impulses, but the impulses cannot reach the integrator through the interruption. Stimulation of the surviving motor axon downstream of the interruption can still produce contraction. Now instead interrupt the motor root. Sensory information can reach the cord, and local neurons can still respond, but the muscle cannot be activated through the disconnected motor fibres. The same absent reflex in these two situations has different causes. A direct stimulation control is therefore essential if an investigator wants to distinguish loss of sensory access from failure of motor output.

Motor neuron organisation within the ventral horn also reflects the arrangement of muscles. Motor pools serving axial musculature are generally more medial, while those serving distal limb muscles occupy more lateral regions in enlargements. This broad topography is useful for understanding coordinated posture and dexterous movement, but it does not justify identifying a particular muscle solely from a crude drawing of the spinal horn. Motor neurons within a pool share a target muscle and can receive convergent inputs from sensory afferents, local interneurons and descending systems. The output of that pool depends on the combination of these inputs.

Conduction and selective root interruption

Tracing an afferent signal requires distinguishing the physical route of a nerve from the direction of an action potential. The peripheral sensory process may travel in a named limb nerve, enter a plexus and then join the spinal nerve before reaching the dorsal root. None of these transitions requires a new sensory neuron. The axon belongs to the same cell throughout. The sensory soma in the dorsal root ganglion is essential for long-term maintenance of its axon but does not have to depolarise and then release a transmitter onto another neuron for each impulse to reach the cord. The first chemical relay of the signal depends on which central target its terminal contacts.

The corresponding motor neuron has a different arrangement. Its soma and dendrites remain in the central nervous system, and its axon extends out through a ventral root and mixed nerve. At the neuromuscular junction, the motor axon communicates with a skeletal muscle fibre, which is a different excitable cell. The fact that both sensory and motor axons occupy the same peripheral nerve does not mean that impulses jump between them. Each axon conducts along its own membrane. Connective tissue sheaths organise and protect nerve fibres, but these sheaths are not synapses between fibres.

These differences explain how a peripheral nerve can transmit activity in two physiological directions simultaneously. Sensory fibres can carry impulses towards the cord while motor fibres carry impulses away from it. An action potential in one fibre follows local membrane excitation. Under artificial electrical stimulation a fibre can be excited in both directions from the stimulation site if both directions are excitable. Orthodromic propagation is in the usual functional direction and antidromic propagation is in the opposite direction. These terms become particularly useful for the electrically evoked responses discussed later. They do not mean that a motor fibre becomes sensory when an impulse is made to travel antidromically.

A useful approach to a root interruption problem is to draw two branches leading from a mixed nerve to the cord and to mark the location of the interruption. If the dorsal branch is interrupted, ordinary peripheral stimulation may still elicit a direct motor response by electrically exciting surviving motor fibres, but a sensory-evoked response dependent on the interrupted route cannot recruit the same central arc. If the ventral branch is interrupted, stimulation distal to the interruption may still excite its viable motor axons in an acute experimental preparation. Such a response should not be interpreted as evidence that a descending command can reach the muscle. The stimulus has bypassed the interruption. Over longer periods, degeneration of disconnected distal motor axons changes this prediction.

Sensory perception is also not identical to receptor excitation. A receptive ending can be activated normally yet fail to convey information beyond an interrupted axon. A spinal sensory neuron can receive input normally yet fail to relay it to a higher centre. Conversely, activation of the appropriate central circuits can generate a percept without normal activation of a peripheral receptor. In a selective pathway experiment, specify the variable actually measured: receptor potential, incoming nerve activity, central neuronal discharge, reflex electromyography, or a behavioural report. A positive measurement at one stage does not establish the integrity of every later stage.

Motor force is likewise a poor stand-alone indicator of spinal pathway integrity. A muscle may be weak because fewer motor neurons are recruited, because their firing rates are lower, because transmission to muscle is impaired, or because the contractile tissue cannot produce the expected force. An investigator who records a motor action potential can establish that a signal reached a recording location; recording muscle electrical activity adds evidence about recruitment beyond the neuromuscular junction. Measuring tension then tests the mechanical consequence. These levels of observation allow a failure to be narrowed to a smaller part of the circuit without assuming its cause from the final movement alone.

The spinal root organisation provides an instructive example of a many-to-many anatomical relationship. Several roots contribute fibres to a peripheral nerve, and one root distributes fibres into more than one named nerve. Consequently a peripheral nerve lesion can affect muscles supplied by several roots while leaving other muscles supplied by those same roots intact. Conversely a root lesion can affect movements mediated by different named nerves. To solve such a problem one needs the relevant distribution data; it is not enough to know the nerve name. For NET questions that supply a simplified root-to-muscle mapping, trace the provided connectivity rather than memorising a clinical syndrome.

Autonomic pathways add another dimension to the spinal organisation. Preganglionic sympathetic fibres leave the cord through ventral roots at thoracic and upper lumbar levels and eventually connect with peripheral autonomic ganglia. They do not directly supply skeletal muscle in the same manner as alpha motor neurons. Visceral afferents can enter the dorsal roots and contribute to local autonomic and somatic responses. The distinction between visceral and somatic pathways concerns their sources, targets and functions, whereas afferent and efferent describe direction relative to the central nervous system. Detailed autonomic ganglionic organisation belongs to the next node.

Historical interpretation and autonomic context

The historical work underlying the functional separation of spinal roots is particularly useful because it illustrates how a biological hypothesis can be tested by selective perturbation. Early investigators could identify two roots anatomically, but anatomical separation alone did not establish that the roots carried different kinds of physiological information. An alternative possibility would have been that each root conveyed a mixture of sensory and motor signals. Evidence that selectively interrupting posterior roots impaired sensory responses while selectively interrupting anterior roots impaired movement helped distinguish the functional alternatives. The separate contributions of Bell and Magendie were historically contested; Magendie's work in 1822 provided important experimental evidence. Modern knowledge of sensory ganglia, motor neuron locations and action potential propagation explains the observations more precisely than the early investigators' terminology did.

This inference relies on the distinction between sensory observation and motor performance. If an animal cannot move a limb after an experimental intervention, its failure to move in response to stimulation does not demonstrate that it cannot detect the stimulus. An independently measurable sensory response is needed. Similarly, movement after direct stimulation of a nerve does not demonstrate intact sensory integration. The logical strength of selective root experiments is their differential pattern of observations under distinct interventions, not merely the appearance or absence of movement after any intervention. These principles can be studied through conceptual experimental diagrams without treating the historical procedures as contemporary laboratory instructions.

A dorsal root can carry sensory information from internal organs as well as from skin and skeletal muscle. Conversely, a ventral root can carry preganglionic autonomic axons in addition to somatic motor axons at appropriate spinal levels. The statement that dorsal roots are sensory and ventral roots motor therefore uses motor in the broad sense of efferent output, not solely contraction of skeletal muscle. A question contrasting somatic with autonomic pathways may exploit this distinction. The peripheral target and the number of neurons in the output chain must be considered separately from the root through which the first outgoing axon travels.

Separating conduction, central continuity and muscle responsiveness

An interruption experiment is most informative when it is paired with a stimulation experiment. Imagine a spinal nerve preparation in which the dorsal root has been interrupted between its ganglion and the spinal cord. A brief touch of the skin can still generate activity in the receptor and in the peripheral portion of the primary sensory axon, but that activity can no longer reach the spinal cord by the severed root. Direct activation of the central stump, however, can still send impulses into the cord and may evoke a motor response, provided the central circuitry and ventral output remain functional. This distinction between naturally activated sensory input and experimentally supplied input is crucial: a failed reflex does not establish that every component of the reflex pathway is damaged. Conversely, a preserved motor response to stimulation of the central sensory stump does not establish that the original skin receptor or the peripheral nerve is intact. It demonstrates functional continuity only downstream of the site of stimulation.

Now imagine interrupting the ventral root while leaving the sensory root intact. Sensory input can still activate spinal neurons; the muscle supplied through that interrupted output cannot receive the normal efferent impulse. Stimulation of the distal motor stump may nevertheless produce contraction. A direct muscle stimulus can be used as a further control of mechanical responsiveness, as long as the experiment distinguishes direct muscle excitation from unintended activation of residual nerve terminals. The two lesions can therefore abolish the same visible movement for different reasons. Compare a sensory recording upstream of the spinal cord with a motor recording downstream of the cord rather than attempting to classify the interruption by movement alone. In an intact animal several roots may contribute to a muscle or a sensory territory, so the experiment is an idealised single-route model and should not be read as a prediction of complete paralysis following any one root injury.

3. Ascending pathways and sensory laterality

A sensation becomes available for conscious interpretation only if the appropriate information reaches supraspinal processing centres. Different modalities travel in partly distinct ascending systems. Two important examples are the dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway and the anterolateral system. The former is important for discriminative touch, vibration and conscious proprioception from the body. The latter carries much nociceptive and thermal information along with some less precisely localised mechanical information. Their different crossing points create predictable differences when only one side of the spinal cord is disrupted.

In the dorsal column pathway, the central branches of primary sensory neurons enter the spinal cord and ascend mainly on the same side. Fibres from the lower body travel in the fasciculus gracilis, which lies medially. Fibres from upper body levels join the fasciculus cuneatus, located lateral to gracilis in upper thoracic and cervical regions. In the caudal medulla these primary axons terminate in corresponding dorsal column nuclei. Second-order neurons cross the midline as internal arcuate fibres and form the medial lemniscus. The pathway continues to the ventral posterolateral thalamic nucleus, whose neurons project towards somatosensory cortex.

The term first-order neuron identifies position in a functional relay sequence. The dorsal root ganglion neuron is the first order in this example; it does not become second order simply because it enters the central nervous system. The next synapse is in the medulla for the principal dorsal-column route. Local collaterals of the same primary afferent may nevertheless participate in spinal reflexes. The long ascending branch and the local reflex branch serve different objectives. Consequently, a dorsal-column lesion above an intact local reflex arc can impair conscious perception of position without necessarily eliminating the local receptor-driven stretch response.

Many nociceptive and thermal primary sensory fibres instead terminate within the dorsal horn after entering the cord. Some travel a short distance in the dorsolateral region before making their first spinal synapses. Second-order projection neurons cross through the anterior white commissure and ascend predominantly on the opposite side in the anterolateral white matter. The spinothalamic route contributes to thalamic and cortical representation of somatic sensation; the broader anterolateral system also has projections supporting arousal and other aspects of nociceptive processing. The usual textbook account identifies the general arrangement rather than claiming a single uniform path for every nociceptive signal.

The separation between the two ascending systems is not perfect. Touch can be represented through more than one route, and injury to one tract does not necessarily remove every possible perception elicited by a stimulus. The words pain and nociception are also not equivalent. Nociception concerns neural encoding and processing of potentially damaging stimuli; pain is a conscious sensory and emotional experience. A spinal circuit can respond to nociceptive input even when conscious reporting of pain is unavailable. The distinction is particularly important when interpreting experiments on reflexes.

To predict a lesion pattern, mark where the primary afferent begins, where its relay occurs and where the axon crosses the midline. For a right-sided lesion of the cord, dorsal-column fibres that started on the right have generally not yet crossed. Disruption therefore preferentially impairs ipsilateral vibration and conscious position information below the lesion. Many anterolateral fibres carrying information from the left have already crossed, so right-sided damage can preferentially impair contralateral pain and temperature information below the lesion. Because of the short intersegmental course and crossing of some fibres, the sensory boundary may begin one or more segments below the lesion. This is a reasoning model, not an exact clinical map.

Some ascending pathways convey information to the cerebellum rather than serving as the principal route for conscious somatosensation. Spinocerebellar systems carry information associated with limb state and spinal activity that can support movement coordination. Their different crossing patterns illustrate why the rule that all sensory fibres cross at the same level is incorrect. A broad distinction between conscious dorsal-column proprioception and cerebellar feedback is sufficient here; detailed feedback control of muscle length and posture belongs to the later motor integration chapters.

Somatotopy and parallel sensory processing

The dorsal column system preserves information about the location and character of stimuli through a series of ordered neuronal connections. Its fibres are arranged broadly according to the regions from which they arise. An ordered representation of body regions is called somatotopy. Such ordering is not the same as a one-to-one map of each receptor onto a unique cortical neuron. At successive relays there is divergence, convergence, and processing of inputs. Nonetheless, retention of a spatial relationship helps the nervous system determine where a mechanical event occurred and compare signals from different parts of the body.

Consider a person distinguishing two closely spaced contact points on the skin. The initial information depends on receptor density, receptive fields and the properties of the stimulus. Signals must then travel through pathways that preserve enough spatial detail for central discrimination. Dorsal-column function contributes to this process, but the pathway alone does not determine the threshold for discrimination. A central lesion may reduce the ability to distinguish inputs even when peripheral receptors are structurally intact. Equally, a peripheral receptor disturbance may degrade discrimination despite an intact ascending tract. The behavioural result reflects the whole processing chain.

Vibration and conscious position information similarly involve changing activity in populations of sensory neurons. When a joint changes position, receptors in muscles, skin and joints can contribute information about that change. The brain interprets the combined input in the context of existing body state and movement. The dorsal column–medial lemniscus system is especially important for conveying these signals for conscious discrimination. The word proprioception should not be restricted to this one route: information about limb state also influences spinal and cerebellar circuits. The same receptor population can contribute to several physiological tasks through branching pathways.

The medullary crossing of the dorsal column system has an important consequence for interpreting drawings of the pathway. A lesion of the left medial lemniscus above the crossing preferentially affects information that originated on the right. A lesion of the left dorsal column below the crossing preferentially affects information originating on the left. The difference does not require the receptor to change its identity, nor does it reflect a reversal of the sensory modality. It follows entirely from the position of the interruption relative to the axons that cross. In three-neuron pathway diagrams, mark the site of the first relay and the direction of the second neuron's axon separately.

Anterolateral transmission differs because a substantial amount of processing occurs near the point of spinal entry. Thinly myelinated and unmyelinated afferents can respond to potentially damaging stimuli, thermal changes or related peripheral events. The dorsal horn contains networks that receive and modify such signals before projection neurons transmit them towards the brain. Local excitatory and inhibitory interactions influence whether a given peripheral input produces substantial ascending activity. A dorsal horn neuron is therefore not necessarily a simple relay with an output proportional to one afferent input.

Different ascending routes also have different relationships with reflexes. An ascending projection neuron can report an event that has already initiated a local motor response through another branch of the circuitry. Imagine that a nociceptive afferent contacts both a local interneuron and a projection neuron. An interruption of the latter's long ascending axon could impair transmission towards higher centres while the interneuron remains available to activate withdrawal. Conversely, suppression of the local motor circuit need not eliminate all ascending signalling. The two outputs are parallel consequences of the input, not necessarily consecutive steps in one obligatory series.

Nociceptive processing can be influenced by descending signals originating in brain circuits. This modulation changes how strongly particular dorsal horn networks respond to afferent activity. The existence of such modulation explains why the same physical stimulus can produce different neural responses depending on context. It does not imply that every difference in pain experience is due to spinal modulation; attention, emotion and higher-level interpretation also contribute. For examination reasoning, separate afferent encoding, spinal transmission and conscious perception so that the stated experimental manipulation is assigned to the appropriate level.

A second-order neuron need not synapse in the thalamus immediately after crossing. Axons can branch, and the broad anterolateral system includes pathways terminating in different brainstem and thalamic regions. A simplified spinothalamic diagram is useful when predicting dominant contralateral transmission of pain and temperature information, but it should not be extended to imply that every dimension of pain is carried by a single direct pathway. When the question explicitly identifies spinothalamic fibres, use the course of those fibres. When it refers to the broader anterolateral system, consider that the functional label encompasses more than a single tract.

A particularly informative comparison involves stimulating the left foot while recording at two points: the left dorsal column at a higher spinal level and the right anterolateral region. Mechanical vibration may strongly recruit ascending left dorsal-column components, while a suitable nociceptive stimulus may strongly recruit the right anterolateral system. These observations would not establish absolute segregation, because the recorded populations depend on the stimulus, electrode location and other pathways. The value of such an experiment is to test a predicted relationship between modality, crossing and recording side under controlled conditions, not to prove that any one pathway contains only one type of signal.

Sensory maps are vulnerable to an additional interpretive mistake: assuming the level of a long-tract lesion identifies the original location of every affected receptor. A cervical dorsal-column lesion can interrupt fibres entering at lumbar and sacral levels because those fibres travel upward through the cervical cord. In contrast, interruption of a dorsal root at a lumbar level selectively affects fibres entering at that root, even if the cord above remains structurally normal. The longitudinal course of a tract explains how a small cross-sectional interruption can affect a much larger distribution of sensory inputs.

How to interpret the anatomical crossing

Consider the difference between anatomical crossing and physiological representation in a sensory examination. The primary sensory soma does not migrate across the midline when the second-order neuron crosses. The first-order cell remains associated with the original receptor territory, and the second-order projection carries the information into the opposite side of the central pathway. This principle explains why a diagram that shows a crossing arrow must identify which neuron owns the crossing axon. Merely describing a pathway as contralateral leaves open the important question of where contralaterality arises. In a lesion problem, the position relative to that change determines the expected side of impairment.

The dorsal-column nuclei receive input from the ascending primary afferents and provide a central site at which different signals can be integrated before further transmission. They are not simply extensions of the dorsal root ganglia. The ganglia contain primary afferent somata, whereas the dorsal-column nuclei contain central neurons that receive synapses. Their names can be confusing because both are associated with sensory information. A useful test is to ask whether the sensory information crosses from one cell to another at the named structure. At the ganglion there is normally no obligatory conventional relay of that kind; at a dorsal-column nucleus there is a major synaptic relay in the principal pathway.

The anterolateral route can be represented with three principal neuronal orders for examination purposes, but its dorsal horn circuitry is heterogeneous. When a nociceptive afferent enters the cord, it may influence a projection neuron through direct and indirect connections. A projection neuron can receive several sources of information, so its activity does not necessarily reflect only one type of receptor. In a controlled experiment, selectively stimulating a particular peripheral afferent population can provide evidence about its contribution to that neuron's activity. The result does not establish that all of the neuron's activity in an intact animal originates from that population. This is a recurring principle in interpreting nervous system experiments: demonstrate a contribution before claiming exclusivity.

4. Descending pathways and motor organisation

Movement depends on the ability of brain networks to influence spinal motor pools. Descending axons from the cerebral cortex and brainstem can excite or inhibit spinal interneurons, and some can contact motor neurons directly. These influences allow a locally available reflex circuit to operate differently during standing, walking or deliberate movement. A descending tract is therefore not simply a wire for a fixed command: its effect depends on the postsynaptic neurons that are available and on the current state of the spinal network.

The corticospinal system arises from several cerebral cortical areas, including primary motor cortex and other motor and somatosensory regions. Axons descend through cerebral white matter, the internal capsule, the cerebral peduncle, the pons and the medullary pyramids. Most cross near the junction of the medulla and spinal cord, then continue in the lateral corticospinal tract. This route is particularly important for skilled voluntary movements of the extremities. Many connections are mediated by spinal interneurons, although direct corticomotoneuronal connections are prominent in fine hand control in primates. Thus a diagram containing an upper and a lower motor neuron represents a useful minimum functional chain, not a universal count of synapses.

A smaller anterior corticospinal component descends without crossing at the pyramidal decussation. Many of its fibres cross at segmental levels or influence motor circuits bilaterally, contributing particularly to axial and proximal control. This arrangement supports coordinated use of the trunk rather than strict independent control of two halves. Brainstem systems, including reticulospinal and vestibulospinal pathways, provide additional influences on spinal circuits involved in posture, equilibrium and locomotion. Their detailed coordination with cerebellar and basal ganglia functions is reserved for the later chapter on central motor integration.

The terms upper motor neuron and lower motor neuron describe functional positions in a motor hierarchy. A spinal alpha motor neuron is a lower motor neuron because its axon reaches skeletal muscle fibres. A corticospinal projection neuron is an upper motor neuron because it controls spinal output through central pathways. A lower motor neuron is not synonymous with a ventral root: the former is a cell and its axon, whereas the latter is a bundle that may include other efferents. Similarly, not every descending fibre is an upper motor neuron involved in voluntary contraction.

Because most lateral corticospinal fibres have crossed before entering the cord, interruption of the right lateral corticospinal tract tends to compromise control of right-sided muscles below the lesion. Damage to the corresponding pathway above the pyramidal crossing would instead affect the opposite side predominantly. The direction of an axon is distinct from the side of its functional destination. The correct analysis begins by locating the lesion relative to the crossing. It is also essential to distinguish the long tract from segmental motor pools: a right ventral horn injury at a particular segment directly damages the local final motor output, whereas a tract lesion can leave that motor pool and its sensory inputs intact but alter descending influence.

When descending influence is abruptly interrupted, spinal reflex activity can initially be reduced during spinal shock. Later, reflexes below a lesion may become exaggerated as network properties and descending control change. This temporal dependence means that a simplistic equation equating every upper motor neuron lesion with immediate hyperreflexia is incorrect. The resulting pattern depends on injury extent, time since interruption and surviving pathways. A single selectively damaged corticospinal tract should not automatically be equated with every consequence of a large spinal cord injury.

Motor networks and task-dependent control

The motor system must transform a goal into a pattern of activity distributed over many muscles. A neuron that influences a motor pool does not directly specify the final joint angle. Its output changes the excitability of local circuits, and these circuits operate in the presence of sensory feedback and activity in other descending pathways. Joint movement is a consequence of forces and external constraints. For instance, increasing excitation of an extensor motor pool can produce movement in an unloaded limb, but in a limb pressed against an immovable surface the same neural change may mainly increase force.

The cortical origins of corticospinal fibres are diverse. Primary motor cortex is an important source of descending projections associated with voluntary motor control, but other cortical regions also send fibres that influence spinal processing. Sensory cortical projections can influence sensory circuits as well as motor-related activity. Consequently the name corticospinal is anatomical: it identifies a projection from cortex to spinal cord. It does not imply that all fibres have identical effects or that activation of every fibre produces an overt contraction. A rigorous explanation distinguishes the origin of the projection from its physiological effect on a particular spinal target.

Most corticospinal fibres act through interneurons or other spinal circuitry. This provides an anatomical basis for divergence and convergence. One descending input can influence several local neurons, and a motor neuron may receive contributions from multiple descending and peripheral sources. Direct corticomotoneuronal contacts provide an additional route particularly relevant to some skilled distal movements. The existence of these direct contacts does not eliminate the importance of indirect pathways. Both can operate together, with their relative contributions depending on species, muscle and task.

An upper motor pathway can modify inhibition as well as excitation. Suppose an excitatory descending neuron contacts an inhibitory interneuron that projects to a motor pool. Increased firing of the descending neuron may reduce recruitment of that pool. In another circuit, descending excitation may suppress an inhibitory intermediary through additional connections, increasing motor output by disinhibition. The sign of the effect cannot be determined from the direction of travel of the descending axon. The sequence of synaptic signs and the state of each target must be identified. This principle also applies to reflex circuits and is a recurring basis of Part C questions.

The distinction between direct and indirect effects becomes important when interpreting stimulation of descending pathways. If stimulation of a corticospinal pathway produces a motor neuron discharge after a short interval, the result supports functional connectivity but does not by itself establish that a direct anatomical synapse exists. The measured interval includes central axonal travel and synaptic integration. Recording from the target neuron, analysing the timing of postsynaptic potentials and comparing the effects of selective manipulations provide stronger evidence about the pathway. Anatomical tracing can reveal connections but must be combined with physiological evidence to infer their functional effect.

A spinal motor neuron pool represents the convergence of commands relevant to a muscle. Its motor neurons can differ in size, recruitment threshold and the number and characteristics of muscle fibres they supply. This provides a basis for graded force even though each individual nerve action potential follows an all-or-none mechanism. When descending excitation increases, additional neurons may reach threshold and already active neurons may fire more frequently. The muscle response can consequently increase without increasing the amplitude of an individual action potential. The detailed properties of motor units and neuromuscular transmission are assigned to 7.D.08; here they explain why a spinal motor pool is a useful functional unit of integration.

To reason about laterality in a diagram, distinguish the side of the cortical cell body from the side of the spinal segment and the side of the peripheral target. In the major lateral corticospinal pathway, the first two sides are usually opposite because of medullary decussation. The spinal motor neuron and its peripheral muscle are usually on the same side. A left cortical projection therefore often influences right limb muscles. At a spinal level below the crossing, it is the right corticospinal tract that carries much of this influence. Students who memorize only the phrase contralateral motor control may wrongly assign a right spinal lesion to left weakness.

Motor function is not completely abolished whenever one descending pathway is interrupted. The remaining descending systems can still influence spinal circuits and support some movement. Moreover, the importance of a particular pathway differs across species and tasks. Skilled fractionated movements of digits rely heavily on corticospinal control in primates, while other pathways contribute to axial stability and more general limb movements. Predicting a disproportionate impairment of a skilled task is more defensible than assuming every movement must disappear after any corticospinal injury.

A spinal reflex is often described as independent of the brain, meaning that its essential local circuit can generate a response without an obligatory conscious cortical loop. This is not the same as being independent of descending neural influence in an intact animal. The baseline state of spinal interneurons and motor neurons changes with posture, voluntary effort, behavioural context and other central inputs. Removal of descending input can therefore both prevent voluntary control and change local reflex responses. It is the difference between possessing a complete local pathway and having that pathway operate at its ordinary gain in the intact animal.

Final common pathway and coordination

Descending motor pathways are often introduced using the concept of a final common pathway. The lower motor neuron integrates influences from several sources and provides an obligatory route for ordinary neural activation of the skeletal muscle fibres it innervates. If its axon cannot transmit impulses to the muscle, neither a descending cortical command nor an appropriate reflex input can activate those fibres through that neuron. This predicts that interruption of a particular motor axon can weaken both voluntary and reflex activity, whereas interruption of a particular descending tract may leave the local final pathway available. The motor neuron is common to multiple neural routes, not common to every muscle fibre in the entire limb.

A local increase in motor neuron firing is only one aspect of descending control. The timing of motor recruitment matters as well. A movement involving several joints requires activity in a sequence that is compatible with the intended trajectory. Descending pathways can bias the spinal circuits towards one pattern rather than another, while sensory feedback helps correct unexpected deviations. This distributed organisation means that one cortical input need not explicitly encode the complete time course of every participating muscle. It also means that recovery of a coarse movement after injury does not demonstrate restoration of the fine descending control that existed beforehand.

For a simple comparison, imagine recording from two motor pools supplying antagonistic muscles. During one task the activity of the first pool rises and that of the second falls. During a different task both pools increase their activity. The first pattern may favour movement in one direction; the second may favour stiffness of the joint. The presence of a reciprocal inhibitory circuit does not make the second pattern impossible. Converging descending excitation and modulation of inhibitory transmission can permit both pools to remain active. Therefore an observed co-contraction should not automatically be interpreted as destruction of reciprocal inhibition. The appropriate comparison is with the motor task and with the expected response to a defined stimulus.

5. From sensory stimulus to coordinated reflex

A reflex is a reproducible, relatively automatic response produced by a neural circuit when an appropriate stimulus is detected. The response is not necessarily a contraction of one muscle, and the word automatic does not imply that it is immutable. Reflex circuits operate within a changing nervous system. Their effectiveness depends on the state of the receptor, afferent conduction, central synaptic integration, efferent conduction and the ability of the effector to respond. These five components define a reflex arc, but real arcs may contain many interneurons, multiple effectors and branches to other parts of the nervous system.

The receptor converts a physical or chemical event into a change in neural activity. In many somatic reflexes the sensory ending belongs to the afferent neuron itself. The afferent enters the spinal cord, where its synaptic targets may include motor neurons and interneurons. Excitatory synapses increase the probability that the target will generate an action potential; inhibitory synapses decrease that probability by hyperpolarising the cell or reducing the effectiveness of concurrent excitation. If motor neurons reach threshold, their impulses travel to muscles and evoke contraction. Whether a limb moves depends on the forces produced by all relevant muscles, the external load and joint mechanics.

A monosynaptic reflex has one central synapse in its principal sensory-to-motor excitatory pathway. The muscle stretch reflex provides the classic example. A sudden lengthening of a skeletal muscle stretches its muscle spindles, specialised sensory structures lying within the muscle. Primary group Ia afferents increase their firing when the appropriate spindle endings are deformed. Their spinal terminals excite alpha motor neurons supplying the same muscle and often synergistic muscles. The resulting contraction tends to oppose the original length change. A tendon tap triggers the reflex by mechanically transmitting a brief stretch to the muscle; the principal receptor is the spindle, not the Golgi tendon organ simply because a tendon was tapped.

At the knee, a brief stretch of the quadriceps activates its afferent pathway and causes quadriceps contraction, producing extension when mechanical conditions permit. Simultaneously, collateral pathways recruit inhibitory interneurons that reduce the activity of motor neurons supplying antagonistic muscles. This reciprocal inhibition reduces opposition to the desired response. The direct excitatory limb is monosynaptic, whereas the reciprocal inhibitory route has an intervening interneuron and is therefore disynaptic in the simplified circuit. The full coordinated knee response should not be described as if every component passed through just one central synapse.

Reciprocal inhibition is a circuit property, not a special inhibitory neuromuscular junction in the antagonist muscle. The antagonist's motor neuron normally excites muscle through acetylcholine release. Reducing activity of that motor neuron reduces excitatory drive to its muscle. Spinal inhibitory interneurons commonly use glycine, and GABA also contributes to spinal inhibition. Glutamatergic excitation from the spindle afferent may recruit an inhibitory interneuron; it is the transmitter and receptors at the interneuron's output synapse that determine the inhibitory effect. Excitation of an inhibitory neuron can consequently produce net inhibition at a downstream target.

The stretch reflex illustrates negative feedback because the response opposes the perturbation that increased spindle firing. Negative feedback does not mean that every intervening synapse is inhibitory. In this case a largely excitatory path produces a muscle contraction that shortens the stretched muscle. The sign of the complete loop depends on the mechanical effect of the contraction. This is a different level of explanation from the sign of an individual synapse. Spindle sensitivity, gamma motor activity and longer-term postural adjustments are developed in 7.D.09, where the loop is considered in greater detail.

A withdrawal reflex recruits a broader network when potentially damaging stimulation occurs. Nociceptive afferents activate spinal interneurons, which distribute activity to motor pools associated with several joints. On the stimulated side, appropriate flexor muscles are excited while antagonistic extensors are often inhibited. This conventional arrangement explains removal of a foot from a harmful object, but the exact muscle pattern depends on the location of the stimulus and position of the limb. A protective movement must move tissue away from the source, not blindly activate an identical group of muscles for every possible stimulus.

If an animal is standing, withdrawing one leg creates a second mechanical problem: the remaining leg must support body weight. Commissural interneurons can recruit extensor motor neurons on the opposite side and inhibit opposing flexor motor pools. This crossed-extensor response coordinates support with withdrawal. The network thus produces complementary patterns of excitation and inhibition across the two sides of the body. A commissural pathway is not equivalent to a spinothalamic tract, even though both involve axons crossing the midline. One participates in local bilateral motor coordination; the other conveys ascending sensory information.

The withdrawal and crossed-extensor circuits are polysynaptic, with divergent afferent input and multiple interneuronal connections. Their longer pathways permit spatial coordination and modulation, but the additional synapses can contribute to greater latency and variable response patterns. A shorter latency is consistent with a simpler and faster circuit, yet latency alone does not establish the number of central synapses because sensory fibre speed and path length may differ. A fair comparison requires control of peripheral conduction distances, stimulus characteristics, temperature and recording methods.

Reflex gain, feedback and network state

An essential distinction in reflex physiology is that a stimulus is not itself a neural signal. A tendon tap is a mechanical event, whereas the action potentials in spindle afferents are the resulting neural code. The strength and timing of the mechanical event affect the deformation of the receptor, and the deformation affects the sensory response. Before attributing a change in reflex amplitude to a spinal synapse, an investigator must consider whether the receptor received an equivalent mechanical stimulus. Identical movement of a hammer does not necessarily produce identical muscle stretch if the limb position or baseline tension has changed.

In the stretch reflex, an increase in firing of a Ia afferent generally increases excitatory input to alpha motor neurons of the stretched muscle. At each motor neuron, however, the afferent contribution is only part of the total synaptic input. A motor neuron that is close to threshold may fire in response to a small additional excitatory input. One farther from threshold may require more coincident excitation. Two preparations with identical spindle volleys can therefore produce different motor responses even when the Ia synaptic connection is anatomically intact. The input–output relationship of the motor pool is nonlinear because firing requires threshold crossing.

This threshold dependence explains the phenomenon of facilitation without introducing the idea that an action potential becomes partially larger as a subthreshold stimulus grows. An additional excitatory input may bring more motor neurons into the range in which the Ia volley triggers spikes. The output measured across the entire population increases as the number of recruited cells increases. A descending influence that changes baseline excitability can therefore modify an apparent reflex response without changing the number of sensory axons stimulated. The relevant difference lies in the receiving network, not necessarily in the receptor.

The opposite situation occurs if a simultaneous inhibitory input increases conductance in the target motor neuron. Depending on its reversal potential and the timing of excitation, inhibition may hyperpolarise the membrane or shunt excitatory current, reducing the depolarisation generated by a given excitatory synapse. An inhibitory postsynaptic response therefore cannot be identified solely by whether a voltage recording moves in the negative direction. A conductance change can reduce the chance of firing even when the membrane potential changes little. This principle from synaptic integration becomes concrete in the reciprocal inhibitory circuitry of spinal reflexes.

Consider a minimal negative-feedback model of muscle length. Let the deviation from a desired length be a positive quantity when the muscle is longer than the reference. An increase in length increases spindle input, and increased motor recruitment generates a force tending to shorten the muscle. The loop has a corrective effect when the muscle can actually move under the prevailing load. If the joint is rigidly fixed, an increase in recruitment may increase tension without appreciably reducing length. The neural connections are the same, but the mechanical environment changes the consequence of their action. Feedback must therefore be understood across neural and mechanical components together.

A reflex's gain is the change in output relative to a specified change in input under given conditions. For a stretch reflex, an experiment might define output as an increment of muscle electrical activity and input as a measured displacement. The numerical gain depends on the chosen output and input units and on the background state. If the input increases while the motor pool approaches its maximum recruitment, the output may saturate. A single gain estimate obtained at one operating point should not be assumed to apply at every stimulus strength. A simple linear approximation is most meaningful over a limited range around a defined starting condition.

The circuit involving an antagonist has a separate functional role. When an agonist contracts, activation of the antagonist would generate a competing torque at the joint. Reducing antagonist motor activity can make the desired movement faster and reduce unnecessary opposition. But co-contraction is not always undesirable. During tasks requiring stability, the simultaneous activity of opposing muscle groups can increase joint stiffness and resist unexpected disturbances. Descending and local influences can adjust the balance between reciprocal inhibition and co-activation. The classic reflex diagram demonstrates an available pattern of coordination rather than establishing that every voluntary movement requires complete antagonist silence.

In a withdrawal circuit, the meaning of a flexor and extensor depends on the joint involved. A limb contains several joints with different movement axes, and different combinations of muscles can move the stimulated surface away from a source. A network that always produced a rigid sequence of contractions could fail if the limb began in an unusual position. Spinal circuits integrate sensory inputs from different locations, including information about the current state of the limb. The commonly drawn ipsilateral flexion and contralateral extension arrangement captures a useful protective pattern but should not be mistaken for a universal law of every withdrawal response.

The bilateral pattern can be analysed using a simple signed network. Let a sensory afferent excite an ipsilateral excitatory interneuron and an ipsilateral inhibitory interneuron. The former increases firing of a flexor motor pool; the latter reduces activity of an extensor pool. Commissural interneurons carry signals to the opposite side, where a different pattern of excitation and inhibition can increase extensor output and reduce flexor output. If the commissural pathway fails but the ipsilateral pathways remain functional, local withdrawal can still occur. The animal may nevertheless have difficulty redistributing load. This dissociation demonstrates why observing the stimulated limb alone cannot establish integrity of the complete coordinated response.

Reflexes can also outlast the initiating stimulus. In a polysynaptic circuit, excitation may continue through pathways with different delays, repeated activity and slowly decaying postsynaptic effects. A response lasting longer than the stimulus is not evidence that the original receptor must still be firing. To distinguish sustained peripheral input from central persistence, an experiment would need to record the afferent activity alongside the motor response. This is another example of why measuring only the final movement cannot uniquely identify the internal mechanism.

Spinal circuits can coordinate timing across segments as well as across sides. The withdrawal of a foot may require changes at the ankle, knee and hip, whose motor pools occupy different regions. Intersegmental interneurons distribute the signal so that muscle activity is organised rather than isolated. During locomotion these circuits interact with spinal networks generating alternating patterns of motor output. The existence of rhythmic spinal circuitry does not mean that normal walking is entirely produced without sensory feedback or brain control. Rather, it shows that the cord contains mechanisms for structuring the timing of movement in addition to responding to individual perturbations.

Reflex modulation can be illustrated by comparing a stretch imposed during a phase in which a muscle is active with the same stretch imposed when the muscle is relatively inactive. An appropriate sensory signal may reinforce support during one phase and be attenuated during another so that the limb can move freely. The difference can result from changes in presynaptic transmission, interneuronal activity and motor neuron excitability. This state dependence helps prevent a simple feedback response from opposing every intended movement. A circuit that is useful during quiet standing must be adjustable when the animal changes task.

Inhibition, irradiation and loop organisation

The phenomenon of irradiation in reflex physiology refers broadly to recruitment of more widespread responses as inputs become stronger or as central excitability increases. A small local stimulus may evoke limited muscle activity, while a stronger stimulus may recruit additional segments or contralateral muscles. The underlying explanation is that different pathways and motor pools reach effective activation at different levels of input. This does not conflict with the all-or-none nature of individual action potentials: the number of active neurons, their firing frequencies and the distribution of synaptic activity can all vary. A useful examination distinction is between the binary initiation of an individual spike and graded recruitment of a population.

The synaptic route through an inhibitory interneuron introduces a delay relative to a direct excitatory connection, other things being equal. Yet the time of an inhibitory effect cannot be calculated from the number of synapses alone. The interneuron may have a short axon and receive a strong excitatory input, while another pathway with fewer synapses may involve longer conduction distances or slower fibres. When comparing experimentally recorded latencies, the relevant distances and the identity of the activated axons must be considered. This is why circuit structure and electrophysiological measurements should reinforce each other rather than substitute for each other.

Another useful distinction is between reciprocal and recurrent inhibition. In reciprocal inhibition an input that favours one motor action recruits inhibition of an opposing motor pool. In recurrent inhibition, collateral activity from a motor neuron can recruit an inhibitory interneuron, classically a Renshaw cell, which can feed inhibition back onto motor circuitry. These two arrangements have different initiating signals and connectivity. They can coexist within the same spinal network. The fact that both involve inhibition does not make their functions interchangeable. A question describing a motor axon collateral rather than an afferent branch is therefore giving a significant clue about the circuit topology.

The reflex arc should also be distinguished from a complete closed feedback loop. An arc describes the neural and effector route by which a detected stimulus produces a response. A feedback loop additionally specifies how that response changes the variable being detected. In a stretch reflex the contraction can reduce the original muscle length deviation. In a withdrawal response the movement may remove the stimulated tissue from the source of the damaging event, reducing further receptor activation. If the limb cannot move because of external constraints, the neural arc can still produce a response even though the physical correction is limited. Reflex function depends on the interaction between the arc and the environment.

A classic demonstration of reciprocal innervation is discussed in Sherrington's 1932 Nobel lecture, where he describes spinal inhibition of extensor activity coordinated with flexor recruitment. The important scientific advance was to recognise inhibition as an active physiological process rather than merely a lack of excitation. This inference is particularly persuasive when an ongoing motor activity is suppressed in association with the recruitment of another response. An investigator still needs to control for mechanical changes: movement of one muscle may alter the length or recorded force of another without a change in its neural activity. Electrical recording and controlled mechanical conditions make it possible to distinguish active neural inhibition from a passive mechanical consequence.

6. Experimental reasoning and quantitative interpretation

A physiologist can distinguish the sensory, central and motor parts of a reflex by recording electrical events at more than one site. A peripheral nerve recording establishes whether an afferent volley has arrived, and a motor nerve or electromyographic recording establishes whether the output has been recruited. Mechanical force is a different measurement because it incorporates neuromuscular transmission, excitation–contraction coupling and external loading. If a muscle force response disappears while its electrical activity persists, the evidence points towards a problem downstream of neural recruitment rather than an absent spinal reflex signal.

The classical work of Charles Sherrington helped establish the integrative nature of spinal reflexes, especially the coordination of excitation and inhibition among antagonistic muscles. The central question was how activity in a sensory nerve could produce an ordered response involving several motor pools instead of indiscriminate contraction. Comparing the activity of agonists and antagonists revealed that successful movement may require active suppression of opposing motor pathways. The conceptual conclusion is that the central nervous system integrates competing influences rather than operating as a set of completely independent sensory-motor pairs. Historical studies involved animal preparations, so their interpretation must respect the physiological state of the preparation and the limitations of extrapolation.

Electrical stimulation of a mixed peripheral nerve can evoke both a direct motor response and a reflex response. The direct M response follows activation of motor axons and their conduction to muscle. The H reflex, named for Paul Hoffmann, is produced primarily by recruitment of large sensory afferents and their spinal excitation of motor neurons. At relatively low stimulation intensities, a reflex response may be seen before a substantial direct motor response emerges. As stimulus intensity increases, the direct response generally grows. Antidromic activity in motor axons can collide with reflex-generated orthodromic impulses in the same axons, reducing the H response at high intensity. Thus the H response is not a simple monotonic measure of stimulus intensity.

A distinction between the mechanically evoked stretch reflex and the electrically evoked H reflex is essential. Electrical stimulation bypasses the spindle transduction step. The H reflex can therefore provide evidence about afferent-to-motor transmission and motoneuron excitability without measuring the mechanical sensitivity of the spindle ending itself. It is not a pure assay of a single synapse, since presynaptic modulation, other afferents and the excitability of the motor pool can affect its size. A change in H reflex amplitude can be informative only when competing explanations are considered.

In a simplified conduction experiment, total response latency can be expressed as T = Lₐ/vₐ + Lₑ/vₑ + Tcentral + Tperipheral. Here T is the interval between stimulation and the measured response in seconds, Lₐ and Lₑ are the afferent and efferent path lengths in metres, and vₐ and vₑ are conduction velocities in metres per second. Tcentral includes central conduction, synaptic transmission and integration; Tperipheral includes receptor delay if relevant and neuromuscular transmission up to the chosen measurement endpoint. A mechanical recording also includes electromechanical delay. The formula assumes that conduction times can be approximated as constant along each specified path.

In an illustrative calculation, suppose both path lengths are 0.75 m, afferent velocity is 60 m/s and efferent velocity is 50 m/s. Afferent conduction takes 0.0125 s or 12.5 ms; efferent conduction takes 0.015 s or 15 ms. If the measured electrical response latency is 31 ms and peripheral delays total 2 ms, the residual central interval is 31 − 12.5 − 15 − 2 = 1.5 ms. This value is compatible with rapid central transmission but cannot establish the precise number of synapses. Uncertainty in either conduction distance, velocity or neuromuscular delay affects the residual estimate.

Another useful experimental design measures arrival times at two recording positions on a nerve. If the positions are separated by distance d and the difference in arrival time is Δt, conduction speed is v = d/Δt, assuming the same propagating component is identified at both sites. For a distance of 0.12 m and a delay of 2 ms, speed is 0.12/0.002 = 60 m/s. Comparing differences between sites cancels common stimulation delays. However, a compound nerve action potential contains contributions from different axons, so comparing unlike peaks may yield a misleading value.

To investigate inhibition, imagine recording from a motor pool while a conditioning input is delivered shortly before a test afferent input. The test alone evokes a reproducible response; the conditioning input alone does not evoke that response, while the paired input reduces its amplitude. These observations are compatible with recruitment of inhibition but do not prove whether the inhibition acts presynaptically on transmitter release or postsynaptically on motor neurons. Intracellular recordings, changes in membrane conductance and suitable controls are needed to refine the inference. Experimental questions often reward this distinction between evidence for an effect and evidence for its cellular site.

Presynaptic modulation can change the effectiveness of a sensory terminal without abolishing its incoming action potential. Postsynaptic inhibition changes the probability of firing in a target neuron. In a network containing many inputs, descending pathways can influence either process. For example, an identical sensory volley arriving during different phases of locomotion may generate different motor outputs because the receiving network is in a different state. The stimulus is unchanged, but the circuit's input–output relationship has changed. This is why reflexes are informative probes of nervous system function rather than invariant measures of connectivity.

Electrophysiology and experimental controls

A compound action potential is the electrical signal produced by a population of activated axons rather than a single neuron's membrane potential. Its recorded amplitude depends on the number, size, timing and orientation of the active fibres as well as on the electrode arrangement. The height of a compound response can change because axons become more synchronously active, even if the amplitude of each individual action potential is unchanged. When comparing afferent volleys across experimental conditions, use a consistent recording configuration and consider both response timing and waveform.

Electromyography records electrical activity associated with skeletal muscle fibre activation. In a reflex experiment, its onset provides a useful measure of when muscle excitation begins after a stimulus. Its amplitude and shape depend on recruitment, firing synchrony and recording conditions. The measurement is not identical to muscle force, and a small electrical signal from surface electrodes may be affected by electrode placement. A comparison between electrical latency and force latency must account for the additional time required to develop measurable mechanical tension. This distinction becomes especially important when estimating central processing time from an observed response.

An H-reflex experiment illustrates the distinction between receptor activation and direct axonal stimulation. The same external electrical stimulus can recruit sensory and motor axons depending on their excitability and position. The H response travels centrally in the sensory axon and subsequently peripherally in the motor axon; the M response travels directly towards muscle after motor axon excitation. For a recording site near the target muscle, the reflex response generally appears later because its route includes travel towards and away from the cord. The shorter M latency is therefore not evidence of a faster central synapse; there is no obligatory spinal synapse in the direct M route.

Antidromic collision occurs because an action potential leaves behind membrane that is temporarily refractory. When action potentials travelling in opposite directions approach along the same axon, neither can continue through the region made refractory by the other. In the H-reflex experiment, direct motor axon stimulation can send one impulse towards the muscle and another towards the cord. A reflex-generated impulse travelling towards the muscle may collide with the antidromic impulse. This contributes to the fall in the H response as more motor axons are directly recruited. The explanation uses basic action potential properties, not a hypothetical inhibitory connection from muscle back to the electrode.

Comparisons of H-reflex magnitude commonly consider the direct response because the latter provides information about recruitment and recording conditions. For example, if the M response changes greatly between two nominally identical stimulus conditions, the stimulation may not be recruiting the same motor axon population. A change in the H response cannot then be interpreted confidently as a purely central modulation. Even an unchanged M response is not conclusive evidence that sensory recruitment is identical, because sensory and motor axons can have different thresholds. It is an informative control, not a guarantee that all other variables are held constant.

A hypothetical experiment can demonstrate the limitations of ratios. Suppose the maximum direct response has amplitude 8 mV and the largest reflex response under a particular condition has amplitude 4 mV. The ratio of those amplitudes is 0.5, a dimensionless measure of the recorded response relative to the chosen direct maximum. It is not a measurement showing that precisely half of all alpha motor neurons fired. Differences in motor unit size, temporal summation, recording geometry and the properties of compound signals make that interpretation unjustified. If the direct maximum is stable while the reflex response changes, the ratio can nevertheless be useful for comparing conditions within the same preparation.

Suppose an investigator observes a test reflex of 3.0 mV, and after a conditioning stimulus its amplitude becomes 1.8 mV. The fractional reduction is (3.0 − 1.8)/3.0 = 0.40, or 40 percent. The numerical change establishes a reduction in the recorded output, but it does not identify its cellular origin. Conditioning could alter transmission at the afferent terminal, change the excitability of motor neurons or recruit inhibitory circuitry elsewhere. Comparing the afferent volley, direct motor response and intracellular potentials under both conditions helps distinguish these possibilities. The dataset is illustrative rather than a published experimental result.

A different design compares a mechanical stretch reflex with an electrically evoked H response. If mechanical stretch evokes a smaller response while the H response is unchanged, one possibility is that the mechanical stimulus failed to activate the spindle afferents as effectively. Another possibility is that the responses recruited different populations or were affected differently by central modulation. The result supports considering a difference upstream of direct axonal activation but does not prove one specific receptor defect. Matching limb position, stimulus timing, background muscle activity and recording conditions improves the comparison.

The conduction velocity relation introduced earlier is derived from constant-speed motion. If the same action potential travels between two points separated by d, the travel interval equals d divided by velocity. Rearranging yields v = d/Δt. The useful feature is that stimulation delay, receptor activation delay and other events occurring before the first recording cancel in the difference, provided the two recordings refer to the same propagation event. The method assumes that the measured distance approximates the axonal path between electrodes. A straight-line distance can underestimate the true path if the nerve follows a curved route.

Error in a latency estimate can be proportionally important when central delay is calculated as a small residual. Suppose total latency is 30 ms, estimated afferent conduction is 12 ms, efferent conduction is 14 ms and other delays are 3 ms. The residual is only 1 ms. If each conduction estimate is uncertain by 1 ms, the uncertainty in the central residual is comparable to or larger than the residual itself. The central interval must not be reported with unjustified precision. This example explains why direct recordings and converging anatomical evidence are needed before assigning a particular synapse count.

Classical electrophysiological experiments separated the effects of excitatory and inhibitory inputs by recording activity from identified neural targets while controlling the timing of stimuli. The underlying logic remains useful even without detailed knowledge of specialised recording equipment. If one input alone produces a depolarising response and a second input changes the response to the first, the interaction can be assessed relative to the responses to each input alone. If their combined effect differs from simple addition, one must consider conductance changes, presynaptic interactions, threshold effects and other network properties. The observation of non-additivity does not automatically locate the mechanism.

The experimental interpretation should also include controls for time-dependent changes. A reflex response may decline if stimuli are delivered repeatedly with short intervals, due to changes in synaptic transmission or motor neuron state. If all control trials are measured first and all test trials much later, a decline unrelated to the manipulation could be mistaken for its effect. Alternating or randomising trial order, allowing appropriate intervals and measuring baseline stability help separate the intended manipulation from spontaneous changes. These general principles are examinable because a seemingly plausible mechanism can be supported by an inadequately controlled experiment.

Finally, anatomical tracing and physiological recordings answer complementary questions. An anatomical pathway between two regions makes functional influence possible but does not establish when that influence is engaged or whether its effect is excitatory or inhibitory. A physiological response after stimulating one region shows functional coupling under the conditions of the experiment but may be mediated by several intermediate neurons. A strong inference about a reflex circuit combines the known course of axons, identified synaptic targets, timing of responses and results of selective pathway interruption. This convergence of evidence is more informative than any isolated observation.

Quantitative datasets and interpretation

The numerical interpretation of a conditioned response can be improved by explicitly defining which variable is compared. Let R₀ denote a baseline reflex amplitude and R₁ denote the amplitude after conditioning, both measured with the same units and recording arrangement. The fractional change is (R₁ − R₀)/R₀, provided R₀ is nonzero. A negative value indicates a smaller recorded response. For example, a reduction from 2.4 mV to 1.8 mV gives a change of −0.6/2.4 = −0.25, or a 25 percent reduction. This calculation does not indicate that an inhibitory synaptic conductance has increased by 25 percent; it describes an output variable that reflects multiple physiological processes.

A further hypothetical dataset helps illustrate experimental discrimination. In a control trial an afferent volley has an amplitude of 1.0 arbitrary unit, a direct muscle response has an amplitude of 6 mV, and the test reflex has an amplitude of 2.0 mV. In condition A the afferent volley falls to 0.6 units, the direct response remains 6 mV and the reflex falls to 1.1 mV. In condition B the afferent volley remains 1.0 unit, the direct response remains 6 mV and the reflex falls to 1.1 mV. The same change in the final reflex is associated with different accompanying observations. In condition A reduced afferent recruitment is a plausible contribution. In condition B an explanation involving altered transmission or integration after the recording site becomes more plausible. Neither comparison alone identifies the molecular mechanism.

This dataset also shows why normalising all observations to a single baseline is not sufficient to locate a defect. The reflex falls to 55 percent of its control value in both conditions. If only that percentage were reported, the two conditions would appear physiologically equivalent. The accompanying input measurements reveal that they are not equivalent at every stage of the circuit. In condition A the afferent response is 60 percent of its original value; in condition B it remains unchanged. Nonlinear relationships between input and motor output prevent direct subtraction of these percentages to assign precise contributions to each neural component. The appropriate inference is qualitative unless a validated quantitative input–output model is available.

A student analysing a graph should therefore identify the independent variable, the dependent variable, the recording position and the comparison condition before interpreting a curve. On an H and M recruitment graph, the horizontal axis represents the intensity of electrical stimulation, not time. The two curves describe the response amplitude at different stimulation intensities, not the sequence of two responses within one trial. In a time-domain electromyogram, by contrast, separate M and H responses can occur at different latencies after a single stimulus. Confusing these two representations produces a fundamental interpretation error. A recruitment curve answers how output changes as recruitment conditions vary; a time trace answers when the measured events occur.

An important control in studies of reflex inhibition is the possibility of a direct change in the effector. If a manipulation affects the muscle membrane or neuromuscular transmission, both reflex-generated and directly generated muscle responses may become smaller even if spinal transmission is intact. The direct motor response therefore provides a comparison route that bypasses central processing. If the direct response is stable, a major change in the measured downstream response is less likely, though not fully excluded. If the direct response changes, the investigator must address that possibility before attributing the reflex effect to central inhibition.

It is also necessary to distinguish a response that is absent from one that falls below the detection threshold of an instrument. A small population of recruited motor neurons may produce an electrical signal too weak or too poorly aligned with an electrode to identify reliably. Repeated trials, appropriate recording conditions and a stated criterion for detection help establish whether the apparent absence is reproducible. A biological conclusion should reflect the resolution of the measurement. In an examination dataset, the phrase no detectable response is usually a more precise description than the claim that no neuron in the network fired.

The classical Bell–Magendie investigations, the development of reciprocal inhibition as a physiological principle and the H-reflex method address different questions at different levels of analysis. The root experiments concerned functional segregation of pathways. Studies of reciprocal innervation concerned the interaction of central motor outputs. Electrophysiological reflex measurements permit the timing and strength of functional recruitment to be compared under controlled conditions. Together these approaches show how gross anatomy, network connectivity and measured neural activity contribute to an integrated explanation. None can replace the others; each constrains a different aspect of the proposed mechanism.

Reading paired recordings rather than a single peak

Consider an illustrative experiment in which an identical test stimulus evokes a motor response of 8 millivolts in a stable recording. When a conditioning input arrives shortly before the test, the response falls to 3 millivolts. The relative suppression is (8 − 3)/8 = 0.625, or 62.5 percent. The numerical result describes a change in the recorded response; it is not a direct measurement of the number of inhibited motoneurons. A compound potential depends on how many fibres are activated, how synchronous their activity is, electrode geometry and the individual action potentials. A more informative control would show whether the test sensory volley remains comparable and whether a direct motor response stays constant. If the sensory volley is unchanged and the direct motor response is stable, the suppression is consistent with altered spinal processing rather than failure of the stimulated sensory axons or of the distal motor apparatus. The comparison is still insufficient to locate an inhibitory synapse precisely: presynaptic regulation of transmitter release, postsynaptic inhibition and changes in converging excitation can all alter the motor output. The student should describe the strongest inference supported by the controls and state what additional measurement could distinguish these alternatives.

Time can supply another constraint. If a conditioning input changes the magnitude of a response without measurably changing the onset of its earliest component, the change may reflect altered recruitment rather than a longer route to the first activated motoneuron. A change in onset latency may instead reflect conduction differences, altered threshold crossing or recruitment of a route with additional synapses. Neither pattern is uniquely diagnostic. Repeating the comparison at different conditioning intervals can reveal a time-dependent influence, but the resulting curve is a functional description of the preparation, not automatically a measurement of the decay of an individual inhibitory postsynaptic potential. The comparison is valid only when stimulus strength and recording conditions are controlled.

7. Integrated predictions from pathway interruption

A selective loss of the dorsal root supplying a muscle can weaken its stretch reflex even if the descending motor pathways and muscle contractility are preserved. Direct stimulation of the motor nerve can help show that the motor pathway still works. In contrast, a ventral root lesion removes some motor output while leaving sensory activity entering through the dorsal root. A mixed spinal nerve injury can affect both components. The response of a single muscle may be partly preserved by neighbouring roots, and the expected degree of loss depends on the actual pattern of innervation.

A useful conceptual model of hemicord damage combines the crossing rules of the dorsal-column, anterolateral and lateral corticospinal systems. Consider a lesion on the left that affects these three tracts below the medullary crossing. The dorsal-column impairment is predominantly left-sided below the lesion because those fibres have not yet crossed. The lateral corticospinal impairment is predominantly left-sided because most fibres have already crossed above the lesion. The anterolateral impairment is predominantly right-sided below the lesion because many fibres from the right crossed near their entry levels. At the actual level of damage, local sensory and motor neurons may also be affected, producing a more complex picture.

The same model shows why lesion depth matters. Damage centred in the anterior white commissure can interrupt crossing pain and temperature fibres from both sides at particular segments while initially sparing the dorsal columns. This pattern differs from a lateral tract lesion, which affects fibres accumulated from many segments. Longitudinal extent, incomplete damage and anatomical variability mean that real lesions often do not follow ideal patterns perfectly. In examination questions, use the explicitly specified damaged structures and avoid adding unstated injury to the remainder of the cord.

The spinal cord is therefore both a conductor and a distributed integrator. Ascending pathways report selected aspects of the body's condition, descending pathways regulate local computation, and spinal interneurons coordinate the motor consequences of arriving signals. A circuit can generate useful output without requiring a conscious decision, yet its output remains modifiable by ongoing central activity. This synthesis connects the anatomy of spinal roots and columns to the later study of peripheral organisation and neural control of posture. The next node examines how peripheral and autonomic pathways connect these central circuits to the rest of the animal.

Local and long-tract injury: a systematic approach

A general method for solving selective lesion questions begins by identifying whether the affected structure contains a neuronal soma, a peripheral axon, a local central connection or a long central tract. Next identify its normal direction of signal travel and the physiological task for which it is important. For a long tract, mark all relevant midline crossings and place the lesion before or after each crossing. For a reflex, trace each limb independently and determine whether the sensory and motor components remain connected. This approach converts a question that appears to require memorisation of a named syndrome into an exercise in causal pathway reasoning.

A unilateral ventral horn lesion provides a clear example. If an injured segment contains motor neurons supplying a particular muscle, output through their axons is lost or reduced even when descending command fibres remain intact. A local stretch reflex requiring those same motor neurons is also weakened, because the final common output is affected. Sensory signals travelling through the dorsal root may still enter the cord. By contrast, damage restricted to the lateral corticospinal tract can impair voluntary recruitment below the lesion while leaving the lower motor neurons, sensory afferents and many local reflex connections anatomically intact. The latter pattern is a disturbance of control rather than necessarily a loss of the final motor neuron.

A unilateral dorsal horn lesion can interfere with local sensory processing and particular projection neurons. Its effects differ from those of injury to all dorsal roots, which would remove input before it reached spinal grey matter. If the question describes an interruption restricted to second-order neurons of the anterolateral pathway, dorsal-column primary afferents and some local circuits may survive. If the injury instead destroys a substantial portion of dorsal grey matter, it may disrupt several local and ascending functions. The specificity of a prediction should match the specificity of the described lesion.

Bilateral motor coordination provides another example of the value of selective perturbation. Suppose an animal can withdraw one limb from a stimulus but cannot generate the usual complementary activity in the other limb. Possible explanations include damage to commissural connections, failure of the opposite-side motor output, or a change in the central state needed to recruit that response. If direct stimulation demonstrates normal opposite-side motor and muscle function, the hypothesis of a failure in central bilateral coupling becomes stronger. A recording showing that the ipsilateral sensory volley is preserved would further support this interpretation. No single behavioural observation proves which interneuron population is involved.

The time at which observations are made is an essential experimental variable after spinal injury. Immediately after substantial interruption of descending pathways, loss of ongoing facilitation and other network changes may reduce reflexes. With time, altered excitability and reorganised synaptic influences can produce a different response. An examination statement that reflexes below a lesion are increased is therefore conditional on lesion type and stage. A comparison of immediate and later findings is not necessarily contradictory; it may reveal changing physiology rather than an error in pathway anatomy.

The anatomical lessons of the cord connect the nervous system to the respiratory and cardiovascular topics studied earlier. Respiratory motor output ultimately requires functional spinal motor neurons and peripheral pathways to muscles. Autonomic influences on circulation also involve spinal pathways at appropriate levels. These organ-level functions should not be confused with the local somatic reflex examples, because they involve different central circuits and effectors. Their common principle is that signals generated or modified centrally must reach an appropriate peripheral effector, and feedback signals must return through suitable sensory routes.

The transition to peripheral organisation follows naturally from the root architecture. The spinal cord can be regarded as the central site at which many afferent and efferent routes converge, but the peripheral nerves distribute those routes throughout the animal. Understanding their branching patterns and the distinction between somatic and autonomic output will make it possible to analyse the two-neuron autonomic chains and their ganglionic synapses without confusing them with the non-relay sensory ganglia introduced here.

9. Misconceptions

  1. Dorsal ramus equals dorsal root. Rami are mixed, unlike the dorsal sensory root. These names share the word dorsal, so a root-selection question often substitutes ramus for root. Identify whether the structure lies before or after the mixed spinal nerve divides.
  2. The ganglion is a mandatory chemical synapse. The sensory neuron conducts through its own branching process. The word ganglion may suggest a relay because autonomic ganglia contain synapses. Count the sensory axon and its soma as one neuron.
  3. All sensory fibres cross at entry. Dorsal columns cross in the medulla; many anterolateral fibres cross in the cord. Many textbook sensory arrows end contralaterally. Mark the actual crossing for the particular modality before interpreting a lesion.
  4. All motor fibres cross in the cord. Most lateral corticospinal fibres cross in the medulla. The phrase contralateral cortical control can conceal where the crossing occurs. Place the lesion above or below the pyramidal decussation.
  5. The tendon tap tests the tendon organ. The principal receptor of the stretch reflex is the muscle spindle. The mechanical stimulus is applied to a tendon, which makes the wrong receptor tempting. Ask which structure experiences the rapid length change in the muscle.
  6. All parts of the stretch reflex are monosynaptic. The antagonist pathway includes an inhibitory interneuron. The direct pathway is often drawn alone. Distinguish the excitatory branch from its accompanying inhibitory collateral.
  7. An antagonist is inhibited at its neuromuscular junction. Central inhibition reduces excitatory motor drive. Students sometimes apply the sign of the central synapse to the peripheral effector. Trace inhibition to the motor neuron and its subsequent excitatory muscle junction.
  8. Reflexes require conscious perception. Local spinal responses can be organised before conscious interpretation. Awareness and movement occur close together in everyday behaviour. Separate the local motor branch from the ascending route that supports conscious experience.
  9. Reflexes are immune to descending control. Their gain and recruitment depend on central state. The word automatic can be mistaken for fixed. Compare the minimum circuit needed for the response with factors that alter its excitability.
  10. Latency proves the number of synapses. Conduction distance and speed also contribute. Additional synapses can add delay, but other conduction terms vary. Subtract known peripheral travel times before making a limited inference.
  11. The H reflex tests spindle mechanotransduction. Electrical stimulation bypasses this receptor step. The H reflex resembles the stretch response, so the two stimuli are easily conflated. Trace the electrically activated axon rather than assuming mechanical transduction.
  12. A segment is identical to a vertebral level. Differential growth alters their spatial relationships. Spinal segments and bones use similar numbers. Identify the level of root attachment separately from the vertebral exit.
  13. A tract lesion destroys all local motor neurons. Tract and grey-matter lesions affect different structures. Both structures lie within the cord, which encourages overgeneralisation. Trace which axons or somata are specifically damaged.
  14. Pain is identical to nociception. Pain is an experience; nociception is neural processing. A withdrawal response can be observed without a conscious report. Distinguish an encoded nociceptive event from the subjective experience of pain.
  15. A spinal lesion always causes immediate hyperreflexia. Acute spinal shock can initially reduce reflexes. Chronic lesion patterns are often taught without the time course. Read whether the question describes acute or established injury.

10. Original Part B practice

  1. Which structure contains most primary spinal sensory neuron somata?

    1. Dorsal root ganglion
    2. Ventral horn
    3. Medullary nucleus
    4. Anterior white commissure
    Answer and reasoning

    Dorsal root ganglion. The pseudounipolar primary neuron has its soma outside the cord; dorsal horn neurons are central relays. Ventral horn houses somatic motor neurons; nuclei and commissures are central structures, not the primary sensory soma.

  2. Which spinal structures are normally mixed after branching?

    1. Dorsal column
    2. Dorsal and ventral rami
    3. Dorsal roots alone
    4. Ventral roots alone
    Answer and reasoning

    Dorsal and ventral rami. Both rami distribute sensory and motor fibres. Roots before the union are functionally segregated. The dorsal root is predominantly afferent and ventral root efferent; a central column is not a peripheral ramus.

  3. Where does the principal dorsal-column pathway cross?

    1. Lumbar ganglion
    2. Motor cortex
    3. Caudal medulla
    4. Spinal dorsal horn
    Answer and reasoning

    Caudal medulla. The first-order axon ascends ipsilaterally to a dorsal-column nucleus; the second-order axon crosses. The spinal crossing is characteristic of the anterolateral pathway, not the principal dorsal-column route.

  4. Which receptor initiates the usual patellar stretch reflex?

    1. Golgi tendon organ
    2. Cutaneous nociceptor
    3. Pacinian corpuscle
    4. Muscle spindle
    Answer and reasoning

    Muscle spindle. The tap stretches the quadriceps and activates spindle afferents, rather than directly testing tendon-organ feedback. The Golgi tendon organ monitors tension; cutaneous receptors do not initiate the classic muscle stretch reflex.

  5. What is the central synapse count for the direct Ia to alpha motor neuron route?

    1. One
    2. Zero
    3. Two in every branch
    4. Three
    Answer and reasoning

    One. The reciprocal inhibitory branch has an extra interneuron and is not monosynaptic. The direct central connection has one synapse. The inhibitory branch has another synapse; the NMJ is peripheral.

  6. Where do most lateral corticospinal fibres cross?

    1. Cerebral cortex
    2. Caudal medulla
    3. Dorsal root ganglion
    4. Thalamus
    Answer and reasoning

    Caudal medullary pyramidal decussation. Most fibres cross before descending in the lateral spinal column. The dorsal ganglion and thalamus do not contain the major descending crossing.

  7. What happens to the antagonist during the simplified withdrawal response?

    1. Its sensory axons are removed
    2. It always contracts more strongly
    3. Its motor pool is inhibited
    4. Its muscle receives a direct inhibitory NMJ
    Answer and reasoning

    Its motor pool is inhibited. Inhibitory interneurons reduce motor output, rather than releasing an inhibitory transmitter at the skeletal neuromuscular junction. The muscle is normally excited by its motor neuron; central inhibition reduces that excitatory output.

  8. Which region contains somatic lower motor neuron bodies?

    1. Dorsal root ganglion
    2. Dorsal column
    3. Medial lemniscus
    4. Ventral horn
    Answer and reasoning

    Ventral horn. Their axons leave through ventral roots; dorsal root ganglia house primary sensory neurons. The other sites contain sensory somata or sensory axons rather than somatic motor cell bodies.

  9. What is a funiculus?

    1. Broad white matter region
    2. Sensory ganglion
    3. One muscle fibre
    4. A single synapse
    Answer and reasoning

    A broad white-matter region containing several tracts. A funiculus is not identical to one tract; the lateral funiculus contains ascending and descending components. A funiculus is an anatomical white-matter division; it can house several tracts.

  10. What is the principal central myelin-forming cell?

    1. Ependymal cell
    2. Oligodendrocyte
    3. Schwann cell
    4. Microglial cell
    Answer and reasoning

    Oligodendrocyte. Schwann cells myelinate peripheral fibres; the difference is relevant at root entry. Schwann cells myelinate peripheral axons; microglia and ependymal cells have other functions.

  11. Which dorsal-column fasciculus serves the lower body?

    1. Lateral corticospinal tract
    2. Anterior white commissure
    3. Fasciculus gracilis
    4. Fasciculus cuneatus
    Answer and reasoning

    Fasciculus gracilis. It lies medially; cuneatus carries upper body information at suitable levels. Cuneatus receives upper body inputs at relevant levels; the other listed structures are not lower-body dorsal-column fasciculi.

  12. Which structure contains crossing anterolateral projection fibres?

    1. Dorsal root ganglion
    2. Ventral root
    3. Central canal
    4. Anterior white commissure
    Answer and reasoning

    Anterior white commissure. The first central relay is commonly in dorsal horn circuitry, before the crossing. The central canal is a lumen, whereas roots carry entering or exiting peripheral fibres.

  13. Which thalamic relay is a principal station for body somatosensation?

    1. Ventral posterolateral nucleus
    2. Ventral posteromedial nucleus
    3. Medullary pyramid
    4. Dorsal root ganglion
    Answer and reasoning

    Ventral posterolateral nucleus. Body sensory signals project via this relay towards somatosensory cortex; facial pathways differ. VPM is associated chiefly with craniofacial somatosensory relay; the pyramid carries motor fibres.

  14. What does orthodromic propagation mean?

    1. Towards the soma in every neuron
    2. Usual functional direction
    3. Opposite usual direction
    4. Across every synapse
    Answer and reasoning

    Propagation in the usual functional direction. Antidromic impulses travel in the reverse direction along the same axon. Antidromic means reverse direction, and the normal direction differs between sensory and motor axons.

  15. What is a motor pool?

    1. All sensory roots
    2. One descending tract
    3. Motor neurons supplying one muscle
    4. One motor neuron plus its muscle fibres
    Answer and reasoning

    The motor neurons associated with a target muscle. A motor pool is a neuronal population; a motor unit is one neuron and the muscle fibres it supplies. The single neuron plus its fibres defines a motor unit, not a motor pool.

  16. What is the characteristic cell shape of most dorsal root ganglion neurons?

    1. Bipolar exclusively
    2. Anucleate
    3. Epithelial
    4. Pseudounipolar
    Answer and reasoning

    Pseudounipolar. A single process branches into central and peripheral processes; the ganglion is not an obligatory chemical relay. Most spinal primary sensory neurons are pseudounipolar; bipolar neurons occur in other sensory systems.

  17. What kind of neuron connects spinal levels within the cord?

    1. Propriospinal neuron
    2. Retinal photoreceptor
    3. Muscle spindle fibre
    4. Oligodendrocyte
    Answer and reasoning

    Propriospinal neuron. It provides intersegmental communication without necessarily ascending to the brain. Only the propriospinal neuron by definition connects spinal levels within the cord.

  18. Which spinal region contains sympathetic preganglionic neurons?

    1. Cauda equina
    2. Thoracic and upper lumbar intermediate grey
    3. Dorsal-column nuclei
    4. Cerebral cortex
    Answer and reasoning

    Thoracic and upper lumbar intermediate grey matter. These neurons are autonomic, not alpha motor neurons innervating skeletal muscle. Sympathetic preganglionic somata are spinal; the listed alternative regions are not their usual location.

  19. What is the primary functional distinction between M and H responses?

    1. Both are direct motor responses
    2. Both require cortical relay
    3. Direct motor versus spinal afferent-mediated recruitment
    4. Both require spindle stretch
    Answer and reasoning

    Direct motor axon recruitment versus afferent-mediated spinal recruitment. The M response has no obligatory central relay, whereas the H response depends on spinal transmission. Electrical H-reflex stimulation bypasses the mechanical receptor; neither response requires a cortical loop.

  20. Which region of the nervous system contains the spinal cord?

    1. Peripheral nervous system
    2. Endocrine system
    3. Skeletal muscle
    4. Central nervous system
    Answer and reasoning

    Central nervous system. Spinal nerves and dorsal root ganglia belong to the peripheral nervous system. Spinal roots and nerves are peripheral structures; the cord is central.

  21. Does excitation of an inhibitory interneuron necessarily excite its downstream motor neuron?

    1. No, its output can be inhibitory
    2. Yes, excitation always spreads
    3. Yes, all interneurons are motor neurons
    4. No, because no output occurs
    Answer and reasoning

    No. The output synapse can reduce motor neuron excitability even while the interneuron itself is excited. Excitation of the interneuron does not determine the sign of the interneuron's output synapse.

  22. What is the main functional purpose of the crossed-extensor response?

    1. Spindle transduction
    2. Contralateral support during withdrawal
    3. Bilateral flexion only
    4. Conscious discrimination
    Answer and reasoning

    Contralateral support during ipsilateral withdrawal. The bilateral pattern helps compensate for loss of support by the withdrawing limb. Contralateral extension supports the body during ipsilateral withdrawal; sensory discrimination is an ascending function.

  1. A selective left dorsal-column lesion at T6 spares dorsal roots and local lumbar grey matter. Consider three statements: I. Left foot vibration discrimination can be impaired. II. The left patellar stretch response must be absent. III. Right foot nociceptive transmission through the left anterolateral pathway must be absent. Which statements follow from the specified lesion?

    1. I only
    2. I and II
    3. II and III
    4. I, II and III
    Answer and reasoning

    A. I only. Left dorsal-column fibres carry ipsilateral vibration information before medullary decussation. The local stretch arc does not require its ascending branch to reach the brain, so II is not required. The anterolateral tract is not part of the specified lesion, so III is also unsupported.

  2. Right anterolateral tract injury at T8: which side preferentially loses pain and temperature below the level?

    Answer and reasoning

    Left. Many second-order axons cross near spinal entry. The sensory boundary may lie below T8.

  3. A receptor stimulus fails to evoke a reflex. Direct stimulation distal to the root evokes a normal muscle M response. An electrode on the central side of the dorsal root detects no sensory volley, whereas a peripheral electrode detects a normal volley. Which inference is best supported?

    1. Failure of muscle excitation–contraction coupling
    2. Interruption of afferent conduction between the recording sites
    3. Loss of all descending motor drive
    4. Increased inhibition at the neuromuscular junction
    Answer and reasoning

    B. Interruption of afferent conduction between the recording sites. The peripheral sensory response and direct muscle response demonstrate survival of important input and output elements. The failure is localised by the absence of the afferent signal at the proximal electrode. The other options cannot account for the observed loss of conduction between sensory recording positions.

  4. Two nerve recording electrodes 0.15 m apart detect the same volley 3 ms apart. Calculate speed.

    Answer and reasoning

    50 m/s. Convert 3 ms to 0.003 s; divide 0.15 m by 0.003 s. The measurement assumes the same volley component.

  5. A reflex response begins 31 ms after stimulation. The afferent path is 0.72 m at 60 m/s, the efferent path 0.70 m at 50 m/s, and the remaining peripheral delay is 2 ms. A second response begins at 34 ms with the same peripheral delays. Estimate both residual central intervals and select the justified inference.

    1. 3 and 6 ms; the second central interval is longer, but synapse count is not uniquely determined
    2. 5 and 8 ms; there must be three additional synapses
    3. 3 and 6 ms; the second response has exactly three more synapses
    4. 1 and 4 ms; the first must have no central synapse
    Answer and reasoning

    A. Afferent time = 0.72/60 = 0.012 s = 12 ms; efferent time = 0.70/50 = 0.014 s = 14 ms. Peripheral terms total 28 ms. Residuals are 31 − 28 = 3 ms and 34 − 28 = 6 ms. Central conduction, synaptic delay and integration all contribute, so neither the exact number nor the difference in number of synapses follows.

  6. Use the H/M recruitment figure. In three illustrative conditions, the M amplitudes are 0.3, 3 and 8 mV, while H amplitudes are 2, 5 and 0.5 mV. The maximum direct response is 8 mV. Which interpretation best fits the data?

    1. The H response is a direct motor response because it grows at low intensity
    2. The falling H response proves that sensory axons stop conducting at high intensity
    3. Increasing direct motor recruitment can generate antidromic collision, reducing H despite a growing M response
    4. The motor axons become progressively inexcitable as the M response rises
    Answer and reasoning

    C. The H response initially grows as afferent recruitment increases, then falls as more motor axons are directly activated. Antidromic collision is a major explanation; the amplitude data do not uniquely exclude other spinal modulation. The M response is direct motor activation. A growing M response is inconsistent with the claim that progressively fewer motor axons are excitable.

  7. A left commissural interneuron pathway is interrupted while the ipsilateral flexor circuit survives. What selective defect is plausible?

    Answer and reasoning

    Reduced contralateral support response. Local withdrawal may persist even though transmission to the opposite extensor motor network is impaired.

  8. Use the illustrative table in section 8. Both condition A and condition B reduce reflex amplitude from 2.0 to 1.1 mV. Only A also reduces the afferent volley. Which conclusions are justified? I. Both reflexes decrease by 45%. II. In A, reduced sensory recruitment is plausible. III. B proves selective glycinergic postsynaptic inhibition.

    1. I and III
    2. II only
    3. I, II and III
    4. I and II
    Answer and reasoning

    D. I and II. The decrease is (2.0 − 1.1)/2.0 = 0.45 or 45%. A's smaller recorded afferent volley supports reduced recruitment as a contributor. In B a process after the sensory recording site becomes more plausible, but the data cannot discriminate presynaptic, postsynaptic and other central changes, much less specify glycine.

  9. A right medial lemniscus lesion lies above the sensory crossing. Which side is affected for dorsal-column modalities?

    Answer and reasoning

    Predominantly left. The relevant fibres crossed in the caudal medulla; the right pathway now represents much of the left body.

  10. A central spinal lesion first affects fibres in the anterior white commissure and later extends into the left lateral corticospinal tract, while dorsal columns and motor pools are spared. What combination is most consistent with the stated anatomy?

    1. Bilateral segmental pain-temperature impairment plus predominantly left-sided voluntary motor impairment below the lesion
    2. Left vibration loss plus right-sided lower motor neuron destruction
    3. Right vibration loss with bilateral abolition of every spinal reflex
    4. Bilateral loss of dorsal root ganglion cell bodies
    Answer and reasoning

    A. The anterior white commissure contains crossing anterolateral fibres, so bilateral segmental pain-temperature impairment is plausible. The left lateral corticospinal tract has already crossed in the medulla, so its involvement impairs predominantly left motor control below the lesion. Dorsal-column modalities and local motor somata are not directly affected by the stated injury.

  11. An identified motor neuron is recorded during four trials. At rest it is 6 mV below threshold. A test Ia EPSP alone raises membrane potential by 4 mV. A conditioning depolarisation is 3 mV and overlaps the test response. In a separate trial an inhibitory conductance reduces the effective test EPSP to 2 mV during the same conditioning depolarisation. Under a simplified linear summation assumption, in which combined trial is threshold crossed?

    1. Both combined trials
    2. Only the conditioning plus unreduced Ia EPSP trial
    3. Only the conditioning plus shunted EPSP trial
    4. Neither combined trial
    Answer and reasoning

    B. The ordinary combined input is 3 + 4 = 7 mV, which exceeds the 6 mV threshold gap. With shunting the sum is 3 + 2 = 5 mV, below threshold. This simplified calculation illustrates how synaptic state affects recruitment; real conductances and firing thresholds are time-dependent, so the arithmetic is an idealisation.

  12. A sensory afferent excites two interneurons. Interneuron X inhibits the ipsilateral extensor pool. Interneuron Y excites a commissural neuron that excites the contralateral extensor pool. If X is selectively silenced and all other stated connections remain active, which change is expected?

    1. The contralateral extensor drive necessarily disappears
    2. Both extensor pools must be inhibited
    3. Ipsilateral extensor inhibition is reduced while contralateral extensor excitation can persist
    4. All afferent transmission stops
    Answer and reasoning

    C. The X branch provides ipsilateral inhibition, so silencing it removes that contribution. The Y branch and commissural pathway can still recruit contralateral extensor activity. Neither branch is the afferent axon itself, so silencing X need not eliminate the sensory volley. The question requires following parallel signed connections rather than treating withdrawal as one indivisible reflex.

  13. A muscle is stretched while its joint is rigidly fixed. Can neural negative feedback increase tension without reducing length?

    Answer and reasoning

    Yes. The reflex can recruit motor units, but the imposed mechanical constraint prevents appreciable shortening.

  14. A cord section has prominent ventral horns and more white matter than a second section with smaller white columns. A lesion in the first section interrupts a dorsal-column bundle carrying information from the lower body. Which combined interpretation is most plausible?

    1. Both sections must be sacral, because leg information is present
    2. The first may be cervical enlargement; lumbar and sacral afferents can ascend through it
    3. The first must be lumbar vertebral level, because the lesion affects the leg
    4. The dorsal columns cannot contain lower-body fibres above lumbar segments
    Answer and reasoning

    B. A cervical enlargement contains substantial limb-related grey matter and long tracts serving lower segments. Lower-body dorsal-column axons ascend through higher levels before terminating in the medulla, so leg information can be affected at cervical levels. Exact segment identification requires more anatomical landmarks; the presence of lower-body information does not by itself locate a lumbar segment.

  15. A mixed nerve is stimulated distal to a recent ventral root interruption. Muscle still responds. Does this demonstrate restored descending control?

    Answer and reasoning

    No. Direct stimulation bypasses the interruption and activates viable distal motor axons.

  16. A sensory volley is unchanged, EMG is unchanged, but force falls. Which stage should be examined first?

    Answer and reasoning

    Processes linking muscle excitation to force and mechanical loading. The recorded electrical response persists, suggesting that failure of central recruitment is not sufficient to explain the force reduction.

  17. An H response falls from 5 mV to 3 mV while the direct maximum stays 10 mV. Calculate the percentage reduction in H amplitude.

    Answer and reasoning

    40%. The difference is 2 mV; 2/5 × 100 = 40. Stable M maximum is a useful but incomplete control.

  18. Two electrodes along a nerve are separated by 0.20 m. For component P, arrival times are 4 and 8 ms; for component Q, they are 6 and 16 ms. Assuming each pair identifies the same propagating component, compare speeds and select the defensible conclusion.

    1. P = 25 m/s and Q = 20 m/s; equal conduction speeds
    2. P = 50 m/s and Q = 20 m/s; P is faster, but fibre identity is not proved by speed alone
    3. P = 50 m/s and Q = 10 m/s; Q must be a particular nociceptive fibre
    4. P = 20 m/s and Q = 50 m/s; Q is faster
    Answer and reasoning

    B. P: Δt = 4 ms, so v = 0.20/0.004 = 50 m/s. Q: Δt = 10 ms, so v = 0.20/0.010 = 20 m/s. P propagates faster. Conduction velocity is informative about axon properties but does not uniquely identify receptor modality or exact fibre class without additional evidence.

  19. Two laboratories estimate central delay as a residual. Laboratory A measures total latency 32 ms and other terms 11, 16 and 3 ms. Laboratory B has the same residual but uncertainty of ±1 ms in each of the two conduction terms. Which conclusion is justified?

    1. Both have a 2 ms central residual, and B's uncertainty is important relative to that estimate
    2. Both prove exactly two central synapses
    3. B must report a negative central conduction time
    4. The residual is 5 ms because peripheral delay is excluded from subtraction
    Answer and reasoning

    A. The residual in A is 32 − 11 − 16 − 3 = 2 ms; B is stated to have the same estimate. In B, uncertainty in conduction terms can be comparable with the residual, so an exact synapse count cannot be justified. The measured total includes peripheral delay, which must be subtracted to isolate the estimated central contribution.

  20. A direct corticospinal projection is interrupted, while other descending tracts survive. Must all voluntary movement disappear?

    Answer and reasoning

    No. Motor control involves multiple pathways; skilled movements may be disproportionately affected.

  21. An animal withdraws its left leg normally but fails to recruit right support muscles. The right motor nerve produces normal contraction when stimulated. Where is a plausible failure?

    Answer and reasoning

    Central coupling to the right motor pool. The preserved peripheral response makes a complete downstream failure less likely; commissural and local central connections remain candidates.

  22. A conditioning procedure reduces mean reflex amplitude from 4 to 3 mV over 20 trials. In a control block recorded at the same time scale without conditioning, the amplitude falls from 4 to 3.1 mV. Which design and inference are appropriate?

    1. Attribute the full 25% fall to conditioning without a control
    2. Conclude that all spinal inhibitory synapses have failed
    3. Randomise or interleave trials and examine the difference relative to baseline drift
    4. Increase stimulation until the M response disappears
    Answer and reasoning

    C. The conditioned block falls by 25%, while the control block falls by 22.5%. Much of the apparent effect may be attributable to time-dependent response changes. Interleaving or randomising conditions, monitoring afferent and direct responses, and estimating trial variability can separate a specific conditioning effect from drift. The data do not establish a molecular mechanism.

12. SET and integrated practice

  1. SET: Afferent and efferent are terms defined relative to what?

    Answer and reasoning

    The central nervous system in this context. Afferents bring information towards central circuits; efferents convey output away.

  2. SET: Why are spinal cord segments not necessarily aligned with same-numbered vertebrae?

    Answer and reasoning

    Differential growth of the cord and vertebral column. Lower nerve roots descend to their exit points, producing the cauda equina.

  3. Assertion–reason: Assertion: A dorsal-column lesion may spare a local stretch reflex. Reason: Local Ia collaterals can reach spinal motor neurons independently of the long ascending branch.

    Answer and reasoning

    Both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion. The two branches participate in different functions and need not be injured together.

  4. Assertion–reason: Assertion: Every part of the knee jerk is monosynaptic. Reason: The direct Ia to agonist motor neuron connection has one central synapse.

    Answer and reasoning

    Assertion false; reason true. Reciprocal inhibition involves an additional interneuron, so the whole response is not entirely monosynaptic.

  5. Numerical: An illustrative reflex loop has afferent distance 0.6 m at 60 m/s and efferent distance 0.6 m at 40 m/s. What is the conduction contribution?

    Answer and reasoning

    25 ms. Afferent time = 10 ms, efferent time = 15 ms. Add them; synaptic and peripheral delays are excluded.

  6. Numerical: Maximum direct response is 12 mV and maximal H response is 3 mV. Find the amplitude ratio.

    Answer and reasoning

    0.25. 3/12 = 0.25, a dimensionless ratio, not a literal fraction of motor neurons.

  7. Integrated: Why does a single observed contraction not establish that a spinal reflex occurred?

    Answer and reasoning

    The contraction may follow direct motor stimulation. To identify a reflex, demonstrate involvement of a sensory input and central integration.

  8. Integrated: Compare a dorsal root interruption with a dorsal column interruption.

    Answer and reasoning

    The first prevents input from the affected root entering spinal circuits; the second mainly interrupts selected ascending fibres. Local reflex connections can remain intact after a selective dorsal-column lesion.

High-yield terminology and connections

Decussation: crossing of neural fibres across the midline. Afferent: input towards a central circuit. Efferent: output from it. Propriospinal: connecting spinal levels. Motor pool: motor neurons supplying a muscle. Reciprocal inhibition: reduction of an opposing motor output through a coordinating circuit.
