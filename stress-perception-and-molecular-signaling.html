1. From environmental disturbance to cellular information

A plant experiences a change in its surroundings before it necessarily experiences lasting injury. A drying soil reduces the availability of water to a root, a cold night alters the physical behavior of membranes, and a wound breaks cellular boundaries. These disturbances are chemically and physically different. Yet in all three situations living cells must rapidly adjust existing proteins and then, if the condition persists, alter the production of proteins needed for acclimation. A plant cannot wait for extensive injury before initiating these responses. Stress perception is the conversion of a disturbance into a detectable cellular change; signal transduction is the set of processes by which that change modifies other molecular activities. The eventual output may be protective, costly, insufficient or even harmful, depending on the intensity of the stimulus and the physiological condition of the plant.

Consider a guard cell on a dry afternoon. The first useful response may be a change in ion transport, which occurs without the need to synthesize an entirely new transport protein. A later response may include altered transcription of genes encoding protective proteins. These responses differ in timescale, energy demand and reversibility. A kinase that is already present in the guard cell can change the activity of an ion channel within a short interval. By contrast, producing a new protein requires transcription, RNA processing, translation and sometimes targeting to another compartment. Thus, an experimental measurement of transcription alone cannot reveal the complete early signaling mechanism.

A stimulus, a sensor, a messenger, a decoder and an effector describe different functional roles. A stimulus is the relevant change, such as lowered external water potential. A sensor is a molecular system whose behavior changes in response to that input. A messenger conveys information to other components; examples include free cytosolic calcium and hydrogen peroxide. A decoder is a molecule that responds selectively to the messenger and influences downstream targets. An effector directly changes a cellular activity, such as an ion channel altering solute movement. A single protein can perform more than one role. For example, a mechanically activated channel can participate in perception while allowing ions to enter the cytoplasm and thereby initiate an intracellular signal.

The distinction between perception and signaling is important because the first experimentally measurable signal is not necessarily the primary physical event. A calcium increase following dehydration could arise from mechanical changes in the plasma membrane, activation of a receptor-linked channel, release from an internal compartment or a combination of these events. Calcium is therefore a useful early readout, but observing calcium does not establish which sensor detected dehydration. Establishing the primary sensor requires experiments that distinguish direct responsiveness from participation somewhere downstream.

Plants reuse signaling components. Calcium appears in responses to touching, cold, salt and immune recognition. Hydrogen peroxide can participate in responses to both high light and pathogens. Reuse does not mean that all environmental inputs produce the same output. Cells express different sensor proteins, maintain different basal metabolic states and receive signals with different durations and spatial distributions. A root epidermal cell and a leaf guard cell can therefore respond differently to a calcium rise even when its peak concentration is similar. In addition, a signal may be interpreted together with another signal, rather than in isolation.

A signaling pathway is often drawn as a chain of arrows, but this representation is a teaching simplification. A real signaling network contains parallel routes, feedback loops, inhibitory interactions and shared targets. One branch may provide the early response while another sustains it. Loss of one branch may reduce a response without abolishing it because a second branch remains active. Conversely, an output may fail after loss of a shared component even though two different receptors are functional. These patterns matter when interpreting loss-of-function mutants.

A useful response must also end or change character. Persistent channel activation can disturb ion gradients, chronic defense activation can limit growth, and continued production of reactive oxygen species can damage proteins and membranes. Resetting a network requires pumps, phosphatases, scavenging systems, hormone metabolism and other processes that counteract the earlier activation. Termination is therefore an active component of signal regulation, not merely the absence of a stimulus. The same plant can respond strongly to the first stress exposure and differently to a second exposure because its starting physiological state has changed.

The study of stress signaling brings together the membrane transport principles of Unit 6.F, the hormone pathways of Unit 6.D, and the signal transduction logic of Unit 4.A. Its central question is not simply which proteins increase during stress, but how a particular environmental change becomes a regulated and physiologically appropriate response. The mechanisms developed below explain this conversion without assuming a universal receptor for all plant stresses.

2. Membrane, osmotic and mechanical perception

Water potential expresses the tendency of water to move between systems relative to a reference state. For a simplified living cell at negligible height differences and with matric effects ignored, water potential is the sum of solute potential and pressure potential. Dissolved solutes lower the solute potential, whereas positive turgor pressure raises the pressure potential. When the external solution becomes sufficiently concentrated, its water potential falls below that of the cell. Water tends to leave the cell through available pathways until the relevant driving force is reduced. The consequences include changes in cell volume, turgor, membrane geometry and the mechanical relationship between the plasma membrane and cell wall.

These linked physical changes explain why osmosensing is not equivalent to detecting a particular solute molecule. Mannitol can impose an osmotic challenge without providing sodium ions. Sodium chloride imposes both an osmotic challenge and an ionic challenge. The early water movement in these treatments can have similar features, whereas prolonged sodium accumulation introduces additional problems of ion toxicity and potassium homeostasis. An experiment designed to identify osmosensing must therefore compare treatments with appropriate osmotic strength, rather than merely adding equal molar quantities of different solutes.

For an ideal dilute solution, the osmotic component of water potential can be approximated by the negative of iCRT. Here i is an effective van't Hoff factor, C is solute molar concentration in moles per litre, R is the gas constant expressed in litre megapascals per mole per kelvin, and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, using R = 0.008314 L MPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, a 0.10 M ideal non-electrolyte solution contributes approximately −0.248 MPa. Under the ideal complete dissociation approximation, 0.05 M NaCl has a similar calculated osmotic contribution because i is approximately two. Real solutions deviate from ideality, and a permeant solute may not maintain the same effective osmotic difference across a membrane. This calculation provides a starting comparison, not proof that the cells experience identical perturbations.

At a biological membrane, force can be transmitted through the lipid bilayer, through attachments to cellular structures, or through changes in the mechanical environment of membrane proteins. A mechanically responsive channel undergoes a change in open probability when the relevant physical state changes. The exact relation between osmotic pressure and tension experienced by a channel depends on membrane geometry, wall constraints, and the cell's initial pressure. One must not infer that every hyperosmotic treatment simply stretches the entire plasma membrane uniformly. A cell wall constrains expansion, and shrinking or deformation changes local membrane relationships in more complex ways.

In flowering plants, the OSCA family provides an important link between osmotic and mechanical perturbations and ion conductance. In a 2014 study, Yuan and colleagues identified an Arabidopsis mutant with a diminished intracellular calcium increase after hyperosmotic stimulation. The affected gene was OSCA1. Mutant root and guard cell signaling responses were impaired, together with physiological adjustments to osmotic stress. The result connected a genetically defined protein to early calcium signaling and whole-cell responses. It did not imply that no other channels participate or that osmotic sensing in every tissue is mediated exclusively by the same OSCA protein.

The causal argument depends on comparing mutant and wild-type plants under identical treatments, establishing the genotype responsible for the phenotype, and restoring function where feasible with a functional copy of the gene. Channel activity in a suitable electrophysiological system provides different evidence from a calcium reporter measured in an intact plant. The first can test whether the protein itself supports conductance under relevant physical conditions; the second indicates how the cellular network behaves in its native context. Combining these levels is stronger than inferring direct osmosensing solely from a difference in reporter fluorescence.

Mechanosensitive channel families are not interchangeable. Plant MSL proteins, MCA proteins and OSCAs represent distinct molecular families with different localization, regulation and ion selectivity. Some mechanically activated channels conduct anions as well as or instead of cations, and a change in membrane potential may then influence calcium entry through a different channel. An examination problem that reports a mechanically induced current therefore requires attention to the reversal potential and ion composition before assigning the current to calcium. The words mechanosensitive and calcium-permeable describe separate experimentally testable properties.

Temperature also changes the physical environment of membrane proteins. Cooling typically reduces lipid motion and alters membrane organization, while heating changes membrane properties and protein stability. These changes can affect channel activity and the behavior of proteins involved in perception. Nevertheless, the absence of a single universal temperature receptor does not imply that sensing is random. Different cellular processes can provide reproducible temperature-dependent inputs that converge on signaling networks. Likewise, changes in metabolism and reactive species production can supply early information about the balance between energy acquisition and utilization during light or temperature stress.

Biotic stress illustrates a different sensing strategy. Cell surface receptors can recognize molecular patterns originating from microbes or from damaged plant tissues. The binding of a defined ligand differs conceptually from the physical activation of a membrane channel. Nevertheless, downstream calcium changes, protein phosphorylation and reactive oxygen species production may occur in both cases. This convergence prepares the plant for overlapping requirements, such as adjusting defense gene expression and restoring cellular integrity. Detailed pattern recognition and intracellular effector recognition belong to Nodes 6.H.08–10; here they serve to distinguish different input mechanisms from shared intracellular messengers.

A cell must discriminate useful signals from disturbances caused by measurement itself. Excising a leaf, changing solution flow or applying a pipette can generate mechanical signals even before a planned treatment is delivered. A mock-treated control experiences comparable manipulation without the test stimulus. Such a control is essential when an investigator reports a rapid calcium increase. Without it, the measured change may reflect handling rather than osmotic, chemical or temperature perception.

The central inference is that environmental stress has several routes into the signaling network. Osmotic state, physical deformation, changes in ion composition, receptor ligands and altered metabolism are distinguishable inputs. A primary sensor can be necessary for a particular response under a defined condition without being necessary for every response that contains the same messenger. The next step is to understand why calcium is such a widely used intermediary.

3. Calcium gradients, signatures and their termination

Calcium ions provide an especially effective link between membrane events and biochemical regulation because free calcium concentrations are maintained far from equilibrium across cellular membranes. In a resting plant cell, free calcium in the cytosol is usually in the submicromolar range, commonly around a tenth of a micromolar under suitable conditions. The extracellular space and several internal compartments can contain substantially more available calcium. Total cellular calcium is much higher than cytosolic free calcium because much calcium is stored or bound. A measurement of total tissue calcium cannot reveal the brief free cytosolic calcium signal that activates a signaling protein.

The electrical and chemical contributions to ion movement can be combined using the electrochemical potential difference. For calcium moving from outside to inside, the free energy change per mole is RT ln(Cin/Cout) + zF(Vin − Vout), where z is +2, F is Faraday's constant, R is the gas constant and concentrations approximate activities. At room temperature, if extracellular free calcium is 1 mM and cytosolic free calcium is 0.1 µM, the concentration ratio Cin/Cout is 10⁻⁴. The chemical contribution is about −22.8 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. A membrane potential of −120 mV contributes approximately −23.2 kJ mol⁻¹. The combined change is strongly negative for entry. The channel does not need to hydrolyze ATP to drive this movement; the cell had previously expended energy establishing and maintaining the gradients.

The equilibrium potential for an ion follows when the electrochemical free energy difference is zero. For calcium, ECa = (RT/2F) ln(Cout/Cin). With the illustrative concentrations above, ECa is about +118 mV. At −120 mV, the actual membrane potential is far below the calcium equilibrium potential, favoring inward current through an open calcium-permeable channel. Real cells contain more than one permeant ion and have changing membrane potentials, so the Nernst equation for calcium cannot by itself predict the reversal potential of a nonselective channel.

Opening a channel is only one component of the calcium signal. Calcium influx, release from stores, binding to proteins, sequestration and extrusion all contribute to the measured free concentration. A simplified balance is dC/dt = Jin − Jout, where C is free cytosolic calcium concentration and the two terms are effective rates expressed in concentration per unit time. This description treats buffering as incorporated into the effective fluxes; a more detailed physical description would separately account for binding and compartment volumes. If influx initially exceeds removal, calcium rises. Once influx declines and removal dominates, it falls. The observed peak occurs near the time when net change crosses zero, not necessarily when the channel is maximally open.

Calcium ATPases use energy directly to transport calcium against gradients. Calcium/proton exchangers use the driving force supplied by proton gradients to redistribute calcium. Together with reversible binding, these mechanisms contribute to the low resting free calcium concentration and terminate transient signals. If removal becomes impaired, an initial stimulus may produce a longer transient even without any increase in the original entry rate. Thus, a prolonged signal is not automatically evidence that channels open for longer. Measuring both the early rise and the falling phase helps distinguish these explanations.

A calcium signature describes the pattern of calcium change in time and space. Its useful dimensions include amplitude, duration, rise and decay rates, repetition frequency, location, and propagation. A rapid single peak and repeated oscillations can expose different sensor proteins to calcium for different lengths of time. Local calcium elevation near a membrane may activate nearby proteins while the average concentration throughout the cytosol changes less dramatically. A nuclear signal may have a different relationship to transcription than a signal restricted to the cell periphery. These differences provide a basis for signaling specificity, although the exact mapping between a particular signature and a particular biological outcome is not fully known for every stress.

It is useful to distinguish a signal's amplitude from its integrated exposure. Imagine that resting free calcium is 0.10 µM. In one illustrative experiment, a rectangular excess above baseline of 0.40 µM lasts ten seconds; in another, an excess of 0.20 µM lasts twenty seconds. Both have an area above baseline of 4 µM s. A protein requiring a high instantaneous concentration may respond to the first but not the second. A slower process that integrates exposure could respond similarly to both. Therefore neither peak height nor area alone defines a complete calcium signature.

Some calcium signals appear as spatial waves. A wave is not necessarily the movement of the same calcium ions across an entire organ. A local rise can trigger signaling in an adjacent cell, where channels open and generate a new local rise. Repetition of this process can produce an apparent traveling front. This distinction becomes important in whole-plant signaling because the distance traversed by the wave greatly exceeds the scale of individual cells. Local flux and intercellular propagation are complementary mechanisms rather than competing definitions.

Calcium signatures can be studied with fluorescent or luminescent indicators. Genetically encoded reporters allow an investigator to express an indicator in specified tissues or cellular compartments, then follow its response over time. The observed light signal depends on reporter properties as well as calcium. A reporter can saturate at high calcium concentrations, respond to pH changes or be expressed unequally across cells. A normalized fluorescence change is therefore not automatically an absolute calcium concentration. Calibrating a reporter, recording baseline behavior and using comparable acquisition settings make the interpretation more reliable.

An investigator comparing two genotypes should consider whether the resting reporter level differs before calculating the relative change. If one genotype starts at a lower fluorescence level, the same absolute increase produces a larger relative increase. Similarly, comparing maximum fluorescence values after a stimulus can be misleading if expression levels vary. Time-resolved plots, calibration when possible, independent physiological readouts and suitable controls are more informative than a single image.

A calcium signal becomes useful because a protein responds to it. Calcium ions do not directly specify which genes must be transcribed or whether a guard cell should close. The specificity emerges partly from calcium-binding proteins that change conformation when calcium binds, and from the location and target interactions of those proteins. The next section compares the principal decoder systems needed for plant stress physiology.

4. Calcium decoding by calmodulin, CPKs and CBL–CIPK networks

A rise in free calcium becomes a molecular message when it changes the state of a calcium-binding protein. Many calcium sensors contain EF-hand motifs, structural arrangements in which amino acid side chains coordinate a calcium ion within a loop associated with two helices. Binding modifies the energy balance between possible protein conformations. In a sensor protein, the resulting structural change alters its capacity to interact with a target, associate with a membrane or catalyze a reaction. Not every calcium-binding protein is a signal decoder: some proteins mainly buffer calcium or participate in structural functions. The role must be inferred from the behavior of the protein and its physiological targets.

Calmodulin, often abbreviated CaM, is a small calcium-binding protein with two globular lobes joined by a flexible region. The canonical protein contains four EF-hand calcium-binding sites, two in each lobe. The sites are not necessarily identical in affinity or kinetics. When calcium occupies appropriate sites, hydrophobic interaction surfaces become available for association with particular targets. In a classical interaction, CaM folds around a helical region of a target protein, modifying the target's conformation and activity. The biological effect depends on the target. Calmodulin can activate some enzymes, inhibit or otherwise regulate others, and influence channels or transcriptional regulators indirectly through interactions.

Plants contain canonical calmodulins as well as numerous calmodulin-like proteins. These proteins share features of calcium recognition but differ in expression, localization, binding behavior and target preferences. It is consequently misleading to assign a single universal downstream target to calmodulin in plant cells. The basic biochemical principle is more transferable: a calcium-dependent change in a sensor alters its interaction with another molecule. Calmodulin itself does not have an intrinsic protein kinase catalytic domain. If a target becomes phosphorylated following calmodulin binding, the phosphorylation must be explained by an enzyme activated or regulated by the complex, rather than by attributing kinase activity directly to calmodulin.

In a simplified one-site binding model, the fractional occupancy of a sensor is C/(Kd + C), where C is the free calcium concentration and Kd is an equilibrium dissociation constant in the same units. This equation is obtained by setting Kd equal to the product of concentrations of free sensor and free calcium divided by the concentration of sensor–calcium complex. At C = Kd, half the sites are occupied. For an illustrative sensor with Kd = 0.20 µM, increasing calcium from 0.10 to 0.60 µM changes calculated fractional occupancy from one third to three quarters. A second sensor with Kd = 1.0 µM would increase from about 9% to about 38%. Thus, the same messenger concentration can produce different relative activation of different sensors.

This calculation assumes equilibrium, independent one-to-one binding and an unchanged pool of accessible sensor. A real calmodulin molecule has multiple binding sites, can interact cooperatively and may bind targets before all sites are occupied. Its response to a rapidly varying calcium signal depends on association and dissociation rates as well as equilibrium affinity. If calcium rises and falls faster than binding can equilibrate, instantaneous occupancy may not equal the equilibrium prediction. A binding curve is therefore useful for understanding concentration dependence, but it does not establish the actual cellular decoding rule.

A calcium-dependent protein kinase is called a CPK or CDPK. Unlike calmodulin, a typical CPK combines a calcium-responsive regulatory region and a serine/threonine kinase catalytic domain in one polypeptide. At low calcium, an autoinhibitory region restricts access to the catalytic domain. Calcium binding to the calmodulin-like regulatory domain changes its arrangement and relieves inhibition, enabling the kinase to phosphorylate appropriate substrates. The kinase transfers the terminal phosphate of ATP to suitable amino acid residues. It does not transfer calcium to a target as a covalent modification.

The arrangement of sensor and kinase in a single molecule allows a calcium transient to be converted directly into a change in phosphorylation activity. Nevertheless, not every CPK responds at exactly the same calcium concentration. The family contains different proteins with different sensitivities, targets and cellular distributions. Lipid modification or interaction with other molecules can position some CPKs near the plasma membrane, where they are exposed to localized calcium changes and can phosphorylate transport or signaling proteins. Other CPKs contribute to different cellular processes. This distribution helps explain why a whole-cell calcium measurement may fail to predict activity of a particular CPK.

A representative stress-related target is a plasma membrane anion channel in a guard cell. When appropriate CPKs are active, their phosphorylation of channel regulatory sites can increase channel activity. The cell then loses anions, its membrane potential changes, and other ion fluxes can follow. However, several kinases and phosphatases can regulate the same channel. A student should therefore distinguish the observation that a CPK phosphorylates a channel from the stronger claim that the channel can function only through that CPK. Testing multiple kinases or measuring different phosphosites can reveal complementary regulatory mechanisms.

The CBL–CIPK system separates calcium binding and protein phosphorylation into two interacting proteins. CBL stands for calcineurin B-like protein. A CBL contains calcium-binding motifs and can associate with a specific group of kinases called CBL-interacting protein kinases, abbreviated CIPKs. The CBL is the calcium-responsive partner; a CIPK contains the kinase domain and transfers phosphate to substrates. Despite the historical naming resemblance to the calcineurin regulatory subunit, a CBL is not itself a protein phosphatase.

CIPKs contain a conserved regulatory interaction region often called the NAF or FISL motif. This motif helps the kinase associate with its CBL partner. Some CBL proteins are membrane-associated through lipid modification or other localization mechanisms, so interaction can recruit the kinase to a particular membrane surface. The functional result is spatial organization: a kinase with a broad biochemical capacity to phosphorylate proteins is brought near a selected set of cellular targets. Calcium-dependent recognition and subcellular targeting together contribute to signal specificity.

An important example is the Arabidopsis Salt Overly Sensitive pathway. Salt exposure produces changes in intracellular calcium, and CBL4, also named SOS3, cooperates with CIPK24, also named SOS2. The kinase regulates the plasma membrane sodium/proton antiporter SOS1. Increased antiporter activity helps the cell control cytosolic sodium by using the proton gradient to support sodium extrusion. This is an excellent example of the conversion of a calcium signal into a physiologically appropriate membrane transport adjustment. It is not necessary to memorize every CBL and CIPK combination to understand the mechanism.

The plasma membrane proton ATPase contributes to the energy supply for secondary active transport by exporting protons using ATP. SOS1 does not simply remove sodium because calcium binds the transporter. Instead, the calcium sensor and kinase regulate an existing transport system whose energetics depend on membrane gradients. If a treatment collapses the proton gradient, sodium extrusion may be impaired even when the upstream calcium signal and kinase activation remain intact. This separates the information carried by a signal from the energy required to execute its physiological output.

The SOS system also illustrates why two plants with identical early calcium responses can differ in tolerance. A wild-type plant can interpret the signal through an appropriate decoder and transport effector. A mutant lacking functional SOS1 may still generate calcium but fail to restore sodium homeostasis efficiently. A mutant affecting SOS3 or SOS2 may impair regulation of the transporter rather than eliminate the transporter itself. A high internal sodium concentration can therefore result from defects at more than one point in the chain. Measuring sodium accumulation alone cannot identify the defective molecular component.

A different CBL–CIPK combination can regulate potassium uptake or other ion transport processes. This family organization permits a common calcium messenger to coordinate different homeostatic tasks. The appropriate examination rule is to identify the particular sensor, kinase, target and membrane compartment in the question. General statements that all CBLs activate SOS1, or that all CIPKs function exclusively in salt responses, are incorrect. Detailed salinity physiology, including separation of the osmotic and ionic components of salt stress, is reserved for Node 6.H.05.

A comparison of the three decoder classes reveals complementary architectures. Calmodulin primarily changes target function through calcium-dependent binding. CPKs connect binding directly to catalytic activity in one protein. CBLs partner with CIPKs and often control localization of the kinase as well as its regulation. Their downstream outputs can overlap, and interactions with other signaling pathways are expected. A mutant removing one decoder may therefore leave calcium concentrations normal while modifying a particular output, whereas an upstream calcium entry defect can change multiple decoder pathways at once.

Experiments examining calcium-dependent phosphorylation must control calcium availability and ATP concentration separately. Removing calcium from an in vitro mixture may reduce activity because the regulatory protein is no longer activated. Removing ATP prevents ordinary phosphorylation even if calcium activates the protein normally. Including a kinase-dead version of the protein provides a control for the catalytic step. A target with a mutated phosphorylation site helps establish the relevance of the site, although changes to the site's structure may themselves affect function. Combining biochemical and genetic evidence is more convincing than using either alone.

5. Protein phosphorylation and MAP kinase cascades

Phosphorylation allows the activity of a protein to change quickly without changing the amount of protein present. A protein kinase transfers phosphate from ATP to a suitable residue in a substrate, while a phosphatase removes phosphate by hydrolysis. The addition of phosphate changes local charge and can alter protein conformation, association with partners, cellular localization, catalytic activity or stability. There is no universal rule that phosphorylation always activates proteins. Depending on the protein and site, it may increase activity, decrease activity or change another property. A pathway diagram must therefore indicate the experimentally established direction of regulation rather than assume that every phosphate is an activating mark.

One widely used kinase architecture is the mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade, usually abbreviated MAPK cascade. It contains three successive kinase levels: MAP kinase kinase kinase, MAP kinase kinase and MAP kinase. Common abbreviations are MAPKKK, MKK or MAPKK, and MPK or MAPK. An activated MAPKKK phosphorylates and regulates an appropriate MKK, which in turn phosphorylates an MPK. The MPK can then modify other proteins, including transcriptional regulators. The terminology describes the position in a cascade: a MAP kinase kinase is a kinase whose target is a MAP kinase, not a second name for the final MAP kinase.

Activation of conventional MAPKs involves phosphorylation of both a threonine and a tyrosine in an activation-loop motif of the form T-X-Y, where X represents a variable amino acid. MKKs are dual-specificity kinases with the capacity to phosphorylate the two types of residues. This contrasts with the predominantly serine/threonine substrate specificity of many other kinases in the network. The dual phosphorylation stabilizes a conformation that supports catalytic activity. Removal of one or both regulatory phosphates can reduce the activity of the MPK. The amount of the MPK protein need not change for activity to change substantially.

A cascade allows amplification because one active kinase molecule can phosphorylate multiple substrate molecules over time. Suppose, as an intentionally simplified model, one MAPKKK activates ten MKK molecules and each active MKK activates ten MPK molecules before being inactivated. Under assumptions of sufficient substrate, ATP and negligible dephosphorylation during this interval, up to one hundred MPK molecules could be activated. The example shows why a relatively small upstream event can produce a large downstream response. It is not a fixed stoichiometric rule of MAPK signaling. Real amplification depends on enzyme rates, substrate availability, phosphatases, spatial confinement and the duration of stimulation.

A two-state model helps distinguish kinase activity from phosphorylation fraction. Let f represent the fraction of a target pool that is phosphorylated. If phosphorylation occurs with an effective first-order rate constant a and dephosphorylation with constant b, then df/dt = a(1 − f) − bf. At steady state, f = a/(a + b). With a = 0.06 s⁻¹ and b = 0.02 s⁻¹, the predicted fraction is 0.75. If kinase activity remains constant but phosphatase activity doubles, b becomes 0.04 s⁻¹ and f decreases to 0.60. This illustrates why observing a lower phosphorylation fraction does not uniquely establish that the kinase was inhibited: increased phosphate removal is an alternative explanation.

The steady-state expression assumes a fixed total target pool, constant effective rates and well-mixed molecules. In a real cascade, kinase activity can itself be time dependent, individual phosphorylation sites may differ in function, and enzymes can approach saturation. Nonetheless, the simple model provides a useful conceptual foundation for understanding negative regulation and resetting of the pathway. If stimulation ends and phosphorylation becomes negligible, the model predicts an exponential decay of the phosphorylated fraction with rate constant b. The corresponding half-time is ln(2)/b. A phosphatase defect can therefore produce a sustained signal even after the original stimulus has disappeared.

MAPK signaling participates in both abiotic and biotic stress responses. Arabidopsis MPK3 and MPK6 are familiar examples of MAPKs that respond to multiple environmental and defense-related inputs. The exact upstream kinase combination and physiological output depend on the stimulus and tissue. A receptor-associated protein may participate in transmitting a microbial ligand signal to a kinase module, whereas altered cellular redox status or another type of perturbation can modify signaling during abiotic stress. It is not justified to draw a single obligatory three-kinase sequence for every plant stress.

A kinase cascade is also a possible source of specificity. Different stimuli may activate the same terminal MPK for different durations. Different tissues contain distinct substrates, interaction partners and phosphatases. Consequently, a transient MPK6 activation in one cell need not produce the same transcriptional response as a sustained activation in another. A signaling node can function as a shared element without erasing the identity of the stimulus. In examination questions, the identity of the activated kinase is only part of the evidence; timing, localization and target availability also matter.

MAPKs can modify transcription factors, thereby linking rapid signaling to changes in gene expression. Phosphorylation may alter a transcription factor's DNA-binding capacity, ability to recruit co-regulators or lifetime. Because transcription factors often control multiple target genes, the consequences can be broad. The kinase does not directly transcribe RNA. Instead, it regulates proteins that influence transcription machinery and promoter function. This distinction prevents an incorrect shortcut from a phosphorylation event to a claim of direct gene activation.

A useful experimental approach is to measure total MPK protein and phosphorylated MPK protein separately. An antibody recognizing the phosphorylated activation-loop motif can provide evidence of activation, while an antibody to the total protein can indicate whether changes in the activation signal merely reflect different protein amounts. Equal loading and appropriate specificity controls are important. A kinase assay using a suitable substrate provides additional evidence of catalytic activity. Neither immunoblotting nor a kinase assay alone identifies all physiological targets of the active protein.

For example, suppose total MPK abundance is similar in unstressed and treated tissue, but an activation-loop phospho-specific signal increases fivefold at five minutes and returns toward baseline by thirty minutes. A reasonable inference is transient regulation of the kinase rather than a fivefold increase in its synthesis. If a target transcript increases only after the kinase activation, the ordering is compatible with a causal relationship but does not establish it. The transcript might be regulated through a parallel route activated by the same stimulus. Genetic perturbation of the kinase, restoration by complementation and evidence of a relevant target interaction strengthen the argument.

The importance of controlling kinase signaling is evident from trade-offs. A transient activation can promote acclimation, whereas inappropriate persistent activation may redirect cellular resources away from growth or promote injury-associated responses. Phosphatases, protein turnover and upstream feedback help restrict the duration of the response. Several signaling branches can converge on the same output, and the cell can use a combination of phosphorylation and calcium-dependent regulation to make the output more robust. This network perspective is necessary before examining reactive oxygen species as a messenger rather than simply a product of stress.

6. Reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide as signaling intermediates

Reactive oxygen species are chemically diverse oxygen-containing molecules with enhanced reactivity relative to molecular oxygen in its ordinary ground state. Common examples in plant physiology include superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical and singlet oxygen. These species differ substantially in chemical lifetime, charge, formation mechanism and capacity to interact with cellular targets. Treating them as interchangeable obscures the mechanism of both signaling and injury. Hydrogen peroxide is relatively longer-lived than hydroxyl radical under many cellular conditions and can participate in regulated signaling over useful distances. Hydroxyl radicals react extremely rapidly near their site of formation and are more difficult to use as controlled long-range messengers.

Stress can change reactive oxygen production by altering the operation of photosynthetic and respiratory electron transfer, peroxisomal metabolism and plasma membrane enzymes. In photosynthetic tissue, excess excitation relative to metabolic consumption can increase the risk of unwanted electron transfer or energy transfer involving oxygen. In a distinct regulated mechanism, respiratory burst oxidase homologs, or RBOHs, deliberately transfer electrons from cytosolic NADPH across the plasma membrane to extracellular oxygen. The immediate product is superoxide in the apoplastic compartment. Superoxide can undergo dismutation to form hydrogen peroxide and oxygen. Thus, an apoplastic hydrogen peroxide signal can arise from regulated membrane-associated enzyme activity rather than only from accidental metabolic leakage.

RBOHs contain regulatory regions that can respond to calcium, as well as sites whose phosphorylation affects activity. Calcium-dependent protein kinases and other kinases can regulate particular RBOH isoforms. This arrangement creates a connection between early calcium signaling and controlled oxidant production. Conversely, extracellular hydrogen peroxide can influence calcium signaling. Arabidopsis HPCA1, a receptor-like kinase with extracellular cysteine residues responsive to hydrogen peroxide, provides evidence that cells can detect external oxidant changes and couple them to calcium entry. The existence of such a mechanism does not mean that HPCA1 is the receptor for all reactive oxygen species in all plant tissues.

Positive feedback occurs when the consequence of activation promotes further activation of the same process or of an upstream component. In a simplified calcium–ROS loop, calcium entry stimulates RBOH activity, extracellular oxidant production promotes further calcium signaling, and the newly elevated calcium stimulates further oxidant production. Such feedback can amplify a weak stimulus and help propagate signaling between cells. It must be balanced by negative regulation, because unrestricted amplification would consume reducing equivalents, disrupt ion homeostasis and increase oxidative injury.

Hydrogen peroxide can change protein activity through oxidation of susceptible amino acid residues, especially certain cysteine thiols. Some oxidation states can be reversed through cellular reducing systems, enabling a regulatory switch. More extensive oxidation can be irreversible or require replacement of the damaged protein. Therefore redox signaling depends on more than the presence of hydrogen peroxide: production, removal, protein accessibility and competing reactions determine which targets are modified. A concentration measured in a bulk extract is not equivalent to the concentration experienced by a particular signaling protein in a particular compartment.

The same molecule can participate in signaling at one exposure and damage at another. Imagine a short, localized increase in extracellular hydrogen peroxide sufficient to influence a membrane sensor, followed by efficient removal. The cell can respond and return toward its initial state. In contrast, prolonged production in the presence of limited antioxidant capacity may allow oxidation to spread to proteins and membrane lipids that were not intended signaling targets. The difference is not simply that one plant contains ROS and the other does not. Both contain ROS; the location, magnitude, timing and control of the exposure differ.

This balance can be summarized with a mass balance for an illustrative compartment: dH/dt = P − kH, where H is hydrogen peroxide concentration, P is an effective production rate and k is an effective first-order removal constant. If production is constant, the steady concentration is H = P/k. A basal production rate of 0.10 µM s⁻¹ and removal constant of 0.20 s⁻¹ give a steady value of 0.50 µM. If production doubles, the predicted steady value doubles if removal does not change. If removal simultaneously doubles, the same steady concentration could be maintained despite a twofold increase in production and removal flux. Thus, a normal steady concentration does not necessarily imply that oxidative metabolism is unchanged.

This idealized model assumes uniform distribution, constant rates and unsaturated removal. Actual cells possess compartment-specific production systems and antioxidant enzymes that can exhibit nonlinear kinetics. Hydrogen peroxide movement is restricted by reactions and membranes, and some aquaporins can facilitate its movement under appropriate conditions. A quantitative model is useful because it shows how distinct mechanisms can produce identical endpoint measurements. Time courses and independent measurements of production or scavenging are needed to resolve the alternatives. Detailed antioxidant pathways and oxidative injury are developed in Node 6.H.03.

Nitric oxide, abbreviated NO, is another small signaling molecule relevant to plant stress physiology. It is a reactive nitrogen species rather than a reactive oxygen species. It contains an unpaired electron and can participate in reactions with metal centers, thiols and other radicals. Its chemical properties allow it to modify proteins and interact with redox signaling networks. In plants it has been implicated in processes including stomatal regulation, defense, root responses and acclimation to environmental stress, although its exact role is context-dependent.

A major established route of nitric oxide production involves nitrite reduction. Nitrate reductase, familiar from nitrogen metabolism, can contribute to nitric oxide formation under suitable conditions. Mitochondrial processes can also contribute, particularly under conditions affecting oxygen availability and nitrite metabolism. It would be incorrect to state that higher plants possess a universally established animal-type nitric oxide synthase as their principal source. Enzymatic activities resembling nitric oxide synthase have been reported, and some algal lineages have related proteins, but the canonical animal-type enzyme has not been established as a general source in land plants.

One mechanism through which NO influences proteins is S-nitrosation, also commonly termed S-nitrosylation in the biological literature. A cysteine thiol is converted into an S-nitrosothiol. The modification can alter a protein's activity or interactions, depending on the protein and site, and can be reversible. S-nitrosoglutathione constitutes an important related pool of nitrosothiols. The enzyme S-nitrosoglutathione reductase regulates GSNO metabolism and can influence the cellular level of protein S-nitrosation indirectly. This is not equivalent to claiming that the enzyme directly removes an NO group from every modified protein.

Nitric oxide also reacts with superoxide to form peroxynitrite, a reactive nitrogen-containing oxidant. The competition among NO, superoxide dismutation and other reactions means that changing one signaling species can alter the availability of another. Under some conditions the interactions contribute to regulation; under other conditions they contribute to nitrosative or oxidative injury. An experiment that increases NO cannot be interpreted without considering the prevailing redox environment. A beneficial low-dose response does not establish that greater NO production will necessarily be beneficial.

Chemical probes are useful but have limitations. Some fluorescent NO probes respond through chemical reaction pathways involving oxidation products of nitric oxide rather than binding NO directly. Different redox conditions can therefore influence probe signals. A chemical donor may release NO at a rate dependent on the medium and can have other chemical effects. A scavenger may modify more than the intended molecule. A strong causal interpretation uses complementary methods, such as genetic perturbations, appropriate donor controls and independent physiological measurements. Similar care is needed for reactive oxygen probes, which may not distinguish each reactive species selectively.

NO, calcium, ROS and protein phosphorylation interact in networks rather than a universal fixed sequence. An ABA-treated guard cell may show changes in several of these mediators. Blocking one mediator may reduce an output because it participates in the response, but the result does not establish that every other mediator lies strictly downstream of it. Reciprocal feedback and parallel regulation are common. The next section examines ABA because its receptor–phosphatase–kinase module provides a well-established molecular example of how a hormonal signal regulates these broader networks.
