1. From environmental disturbance to cellular information

A plant experiences a change in its surroundings before it necessarily experiences lasting injury. A drying soil reduces the availability of water to a root, a cold night alters the physical behavior of membranes, and a wound breaks cellular boundaries. These disturbances are chemically and physically different. Yet in all three situations living cells must rapidly adjust existing proteins and then, if the condition persists, alter the production of proteins needed for acclimation. A plant cannot wait for extensive injury before initiating these responses. Stress perception is the conversion of a disturbance into a detectable cellular change; signal transduction is the set of processes by which that change modifies other molecular activities. The eventual output may be protective, costly, insufficient or even harmful, depending on the intensity of the stimulus and the physiological condition of the plant.

Consider a guard cell on a dry afternoon. The first useful response may be a change in ion transport, which occurs without the need to synthesize an entirely new transport protein. A later response may include altered transcription of genes encoding protective proteins. These responses differ in timescale, energy demand and reversibility. A kinase that is already present in the guard cell can change the activity of an ion channel within a short interval. By contrast, producing a new protein requires transcription, RNA processing, translation and sometimes targeting to another compartment. Thus, an experimental measurement of transcription alone cannot reveal the complete early signaling mechanism.

A stimulus, a sensor, a messenger, a decoder and an effector describe different functional roles. A stimulus is the relevant change, such as lowered external water potential. A sensor is a molecular system whose behavior changes in response to that input. A messenger conveys information to other components; examples include free cytosolic calcium and hydrogen peroxide. A decoder is a molecule that responds selectively to the messenger and influences downstream targets. An effector directly changes a cellular activity, such as an ion channel altering solute movement. A single protein can perform more than one role. For example, a mechanically activated channel can participate in perception while allowing ions to enter the cytoplasm and thereby initiate an intracellular signal.

The distinction between perception and signaling is important because the first experimentally measurable signal is not necessarily the primary physical event. A calcium increase following dehydration could arise from mechanical changes in the plasma membrane, activation of a receptor-linked channel, release from an internal compartment or a combination of these events. Calcium is therefore a useful early readout, but observing calcium does not establish which sensor detected dehydration. Establishing the primary sensor requires experiments that distinguish direct responsiveness from participation somewhere downstream.

Plants reuse signaling components. Calcium appears in responses to touching, cold, salt and immune recognition. Hydrogen peroxide can participate in responses to both high light and pathogens. Reuse does not mean that all environmental inputs produce the same output. Cells express different sensor proteins, maintain different basal metabolic states and receive signals with different durations and spatial distributions. A root epidermal cell and a leaf guard cell can therefore respond differently to a calcium rise even when its peak concentration is similar. In addition, a signal may be interpreted together with another signal, rather than in isolation.

A signaling pathway is often drawn as a chain of arrows, but this representation is a teaching simplification. A real signaling network contains parallel routes, feedback loops, inhibitory interactions and shared targets. One branch may provide the early response while another sustains it. Loss of one branch may reduce a response without abolishing it because a second branch remains active. Conversely, an output may fail after loss of a shared component even though two different receptors are functional. These patterns matter when interpreting loss-of-function mutants.

A useful response must also end or change character. Persistent channel activation can disturb ion gradients, chronic defense activation can limit growth, and continued production of reactive oxygen species can damage proteins and membranes. Resetting a network requires pumps, phosphatases, scavenging systems, hormone metabolism and other processes that counteract the earlier activation. Termination is therefore an active component of signal regulation, not merely the absence of a stimulus. The same plant can respond strongly to the first stress exposure and differently to a second exposure because its starting physiological state has changed.

The study of stress signaling brings together the membrane transport principles of Unit 6.F, the hormone pathways of Unit 6.D, and the signal transduction logic of Unit 4.A. Its central question is not simply which proteins increase during stress, but how a particular environmental change becomes a regulated and physiologically appropriate response. The mechanisms developed below explain this conversion without assuming a universal receptor for all plant stresses.

2. Membrane, osmotic and mechanical perception

Water potential expresses the tendency of water to move between systems relative to a reference state. For a simplified living cell at negligible height differences and with matric effects ignored, water potential is the sum of solute potential and pressure potential. Dissolved solutes lower the solute potential, whereas positive turgor pressure raises the pressure potential. When the external solution becomes sufficiently concentrated, its water potential falls below that of the cell. Water tends to leave the cell through available pathways until the relevant driving force is reduced. The consequences include changes in cell volume, turgor, membrane geometry and the mechanical relationship between the plasma membrane and cell wall.

These linked physical changes explain why osmosensing is not equivalent to detecting a particular solute molecule. Mannitol can impose an osmotic challenge without providing sodium ions. Sodium chloride imposes both an osmotic challenge and an ionic challenge. The early water movement in these treatments can have similar features, whereas prolonged sodium accumulation introduces additional problems of ion toxicity and potassium homeostasis. An experiment designed to identify osmosensing must therefore compare treatments with appropriate osmotic strength, rather than merely adding equal molar quantities of different solutes.

For an ideal dilute solution, the osmotic component of water potential can be approximated by the negative of iCRT. Here i is an effective van't Hoff factor, C is solute molar concentration in moles per litre, R is the gas constant expressed in litre megapascals per mole per kelvin, and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, using R = 0.008314 L MPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, a 0.10 M ideal non-electrolyte solution contributes approximately −0.248 MPa. Under the ideal complete dissociation approximation, 0.05 M NaCl has a similar calculated osmotic contribution because i is approximately two. Real solutions deviate from ideality, and a permeant solute may not maintain the same effective osmotic difference across a membrane. This calculation provides a starting comparison, not proof that the cells experience identical perturbations.

At a biological membrane, force can be transmitted through the lipid bilayer, through attachments to cellular structures, or through changes in the mechanical environment of membrane proteins. A mechanically responsive channel undergoes a change in open probability when the relevant physical state changes. The exact relation between osmotic pressure and tension experienced by a channel depends on membrane geometry, wall constraints, and the cell's initial pressure. One must not infer that every hyperosmotic treatment simply stretches the entire plasma membrane uniformly. A cell wall constrains expansion, and shrinking or deformation changes local membrane relationships in more complex ways.

In flowering plants, the OSCA family provides an important link between osmotic and mechanical perturbations and ion conductance. In a 2014 study, Yuan and colleagues identified an Arabidopsis mutant with a diminished intracellular calcium increase after hyperosmotic stimulation. The affected gene was OSCA1. Mutant root and guard cell signaling responses were impaired, together with physiological adjustments to osmotic stress. The result connected a genetically defined protein to early calcium signaling and whole-cell responses. It did not imply that no other channels participate or that osmotic sensing in every tissue is mediated exclusively by the same OSCA protein.

The causal argument depends on comparing mutant and wild-type plants under identical treatments, establishing the genotype responsible for the phenotype, and restoring function where feasible with a functional copy of the gene. Channel activity in a suitable electrophysiological system provides different evidence from a calcium reporter measured in an intact plant. The first can test whether the protein itself supports conductance under relevant physical conditions; the second indicates how the cellular network behaves in its native context. Combining these levels is stronger than inferring direct osmosensing solely from a difference in reporter fluorescence.

Mechanosensitive channel families are not interchangeable. Plant MSL proteins, MCA proteins and OSCAs represent distinct molecular families with different localization, regulation and ion selectivity. Some mechanically activated channels conduct anions as well as or instead of cations, and a change in membrane potential may then influence calcium entry through a different channel. An examination problem that reports a mechanically induced current therefore requires attention to the reversal potential and ion composition before assigning the current to calcium. The words mechanosensitive and calcium-permeable describe separate experimentally testable properties.

Temperature also changes the physical environment of membrane proteins. Cooling typically reduces lipid motion and alters membrane organization, while heating changes membrane properties and protein stability. These changes can affect channel activity and the behavior of proteins involved in perception. Nevertheless, the absence of a single universal temperature receptor does not imply that sensing is random. Different cellular processes can provide reproducible temperature-dependent inputs that converge on signaling networks. Likewise, changes in metabolism and reactive species production can supply early information about the balance between energy acquisition and utilization during light or temperature stress.

Biotic stress illustrates a different sensing strategy. Cell surface receptors can recognize molecular patterns originating from microbes or from damaged plant tissues. The binding of a defined ligand differs conceptually from the physical activation of a membrane channel. Nevertheless, downstream calcium changes, protein phosphorylation and reactive oxygen species production may occur in both cases. This convergence prepares the plant for overlapping requirements, such as adjusting defense gene expression and restoring cellular integrity. Detailed pattern recognition and intracellular effector recognition belong to Nodes 6.H.08–10; here they serve to distinguish different input mechanisms from shared intracellular messengers.

A cell must discriminate useful signals from disturbances caused by measurement itself. Excising a leaf, changing solution flow or applying a pipette can generate mechanical signals even before a planned treatment is delivered. A mock-treated control experiences comparable manipulation without the test stimulus. Such a control is essential when an investigator reports a rapid calcium increase. Without it, the measured change may reflect handling rather than osmotic, chemical or temperature perception.

The central inference is that environmental stress has several routes into the signaling network. Osmotic state, physical deformation, changes in ion composition, receptor ligands and altered metabolism are distinguishable inputs. A primary sensor can be necessary for a particular response under a defined condition without being necessary for every response that contains the same messenger. The next step is to understand why calcium is such a widely used intermediary.

3. Calcium gradients, signatures and their termination

Calcium ions provide an especially effective link between membrane events and biochemical regulation because free calcium concentrations are maintained far from equilibrium across cellular membranes. In a resting plant cell, free calcium in the cytosol is usually in the submicromolar range, commonly around a tenth of a micromolar under suitable conditions. The extracellular space and several internal compartments can contain substantially more available calcium. Total cellular calcium is much higher than cytosolic free calcium because much calcium is stored or bound. A measurement of total tissue calcium cannot reveal the brief free cytosolic calcium signal that activates a signaling protein.

The electrical and chemical contributions to ion movement can be combined using the electrochemical potential difference. For calcium moving from outside to inside, the free energy change per mole is RT ln(Cin/Cout) + zF(Vin − Vout), where z is +2, F is Faraday's constant, R is the gas constant and concentrations approximate activities. At room temperature, if extracellular free calcium is 1 mM and cytosolic free calcium is 0.1 µM, the concentration ratio Cin/Cout is 10⁻⁴. The chemical contribution is about −22.8 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. A membrane potential of −120 mV contributes approximately −23.2 kJ mol⁻¹. The combined change is strongly negative for entry. The channel does not need to hydrolyze ATP to drive this movement; the cell had previously expended energy establishing and maintaining the gradients.

The equilibrium potential for an ion follows when the electrochemical free energy difference is zero. For calcium, ECa = (RT/2F) ln(Cout/Cin). With the illustrative concentrations above, ECa is about +118 mV. At −120 mV, the actual membrane potential is far below the calcium equilibrium potential, favoring inward current through an open calcium-permeable channel. Real cells contain more than one permeant ion and have changing membrane potentials, so the Nernst equation for calcium cannot by itself predict the reversal potential of a nonselective channel.

Opening a channel is only one component of the calcium signal. Calcium influx, release from stores, binding to proteins, sequestration and extrusion all contribute to the measured free concentration. A simplified balance is dC/dt = Jin − Jout, where C is free cytosolic calcium concentration and the two terms are effective rates expressed in concentration per unit time. This description treats buffering as incorporated into the effective fluxes; a more detailed physical description would separately account for binding and compartment volumes. If influx initially exceeds removal, calcium rises. Once influx declines and removal dominates, it falls. The observed peak occurs near the time when net change crosses zero, not necessarily when the channel is maximally open.

Calcium ATPases use energy directly to transport calcium against gradients. Calcium/proton exchangers use the driving force supplied by proton gradients to redistribute calcium. Together with reversible binding, these mechanisms contribute to the low resting free calcium concentration and terminate transient signals. If removal becomes impaired, an initial stimulus may produce a longer transient even without any increase in the original entry rate. Thus, a prolonged signal is not automatically evidence that channels open for longer. Measuring both the early rise and the falling phase helps distinguish these explanations.

A calcium signature describes the pattern of calcium change in time and space. Its useful dimensions include amplitude, duration, rise and decay rates, repetition frequency, location, and propagation. A rapid single peak and repeated oscillations can expose different sensor proteins to calcium for different lengths of time. Local calcium elevation near a membrane may activate nearby proteins while the average concentration throughout the cytosol changes less dramatically. A nuclear signal may have a different relationship to transcription than a signal restricted to the cell periphery. These differences provide a basis for signaling specificity, although the exact mapping between a particular signature and a particular biological outcome is not fully known for every stress.

It is useful to distinguish a signal's amplitude from its integrated exposure. Imagine that resting free calcium is 0.10 µM. In one illustrative experiment, a rectangular excess above baseline of 0.40 µM lasts ten seconds; in another, an excess of 0.20 µM lasts twenty seconds. Both have an area above baseline of 4 µM s. A protein requiring a high instantaneous concentration may respond to the first but not the second. A slower process that integrates exposure could respond similarly to both. Therefore neither peak height nor area alone defines a complete calcium signature.

Some calcium signals appear as spatial waves. A wave is not necessarily the movement of the same calcium ions across an entire organ. A local rise can trigger signaling in an adjacent cell, where channels open and generate a new local rise. Repetition of this process can produce an apparent traveling front. This distinction becomes important in whole-plant signaling because the distance traversed by the wave greatly exceeds the scale of individual cells. Local flux and intercellular propagation are complementary mechanisms rather than competing definitions.

Calcium signatures can be studied with fluorescent or luminescent indicators. Genetically encoded reporters allow an investigator to express an indicator in specified tissues or cellular compartments, then follow its response over time. The observed light signal depends on reporter properties as well as calcium. A reporter can saturate at high calcium concentrations, respond to pH changes or be expressed unequally across cells. A normalized fluorescence change is therefore not automatically an absolute calcium concentration. Calibrating a reporter, recording baseline behavior and using comparable acquisition settings make the interpretation more reliable.

An investigator comparing two genotypes should consider whether the resting reporter level differs before calculating the relative change. If one genotype starts at a lower fluorescence level, the same absolute increase produces a larger relative increase. Similarly, comparing maximum fluorescence values after a stimulus can be misleading if expression levels vary. Time-resolved plots, calibration when possible, independent physiological readouts and suitable controls are more informative than a single image.

A calcium signal becomes useful because a protein responds to it. Calcium ions do not directly specify which genes must be transcribed or whether a guard cell should close. The specificity emerges partly from calcium-binding proteins that change conformation when calcium binds, and from the location and target interactions of those proteins. The next section compares the principal decoder systems needed for plant stress physiology.

4. Calcium decoding by calmodulin, CPKs and CBL–CIPK networks

A rise in free calcium becomes a molecular message when it changes the state of a calcium-binding protein. Many calcium sensors contain EF-hand motifs, structural arrangements in which amino acid side chains coordinate a calcium ion within a loop associated with two helices. Binding modifies the energy balance between possible protein conformations. In a sensor protein, the resulting structural change alters its capacity to interact with a target, associate with a membrane or catalyze a reaction. Not every calcium-binding protein is a signal decoder: some proteins mainly buffer calcium or participate in structural functions. The role must be inferred from the behavior of the protein and its physiological targets.

Calmodulin, often abbreviated CaM, is a small calcium-binding protein with two globular lobes joined by a flexible region. The canonical protein contains four EF-hand calcium-binding sites, two in each lobe. The sites are not necessarily identical in affinity or kinetics. When calcium occupies appropriate sites, hydrophobic interaction surfaces become available for association with particular targets. In a classical interaction, CaM folds around a helical region of a target protein, modifying the target's conformation and activity. The biological effect depends on the target. Calmodulin can activate some enzymes, inhibit or otherwise regulate others, and influence channels or transcriptional regulators indirectly through interactions.

Plants contain canonical calmodulins as well as numerous calmodulin-like proteins. These proteins share features of calcium recognition but differ in expression, localization, binding behavior and target preferences. It is consequently misleading to assign a single universal downstream target to calmodulin in plant cells. The basic biochemical principle is more transferable: a calcium-dependent change in a sensor alters its interaction with another molecule. Calmodulin itself does not have an intrinsic protein kinase catalytic domain. If a target becomes phosphorylated following calmodulin binding, the phosphorylation must be explained by an enzyme activated or regulated by the complex, rather than by attributing kinase activity directly to calmodulin.

In a simplified one-site binding model, the fractional occupancy of a sensor is C/(Kd + C), where C is the free calcium concentration and Kd is an equilibrium dissociation constant in the same units. This equation is obtained by setting Kd equal to the product of concentrations of free sensor and free calcium divided by the concentration of sensor–calcium complex. At C = Kd, half the sites are occupied. For an illustrative sensor with Kd = 0.20 µM, increasing calcium from 0.10 to 0.60 µM changes calculated fractional occupancy from one third to three quarters. A second sensor with Kd = 1.0 µM would increase from about 9% to about 38%. Thus, the same messenger concentration can produce different relative activation of different sensors.

This calculation assumes equilibrium, independent one-to-one binding and an unchanged pool of accessible sensor. A real calmodulin molecule has multiple binding sites, can interact cooperatively and may bind targets before all sites are occupied. Its response to a rapidly varying calcium signal depends on association and dissociation rates as well as equilibrium affinity. If calcium rises and falls faster than binding can equilibrate, instantaneous occupancy may not equal the equilibrium prediction. A binding curve is therefore useful for understanding concentration dependence, but it does not establish the actual cellular decoding rule.

A calcium-dependent protein kinase is called a CPK or CDPK. Unlike calmodulin, a typical CPK combines a calcium-responsive regulatory region and a serine/threonine kinase catalytic domain in one polypeptide. At low calcium, an autoinhibitory region restricts access to the catalytic domain. Calcium binding to the calmodulin-like regulatory domain changes its arrangement and relieves inhibition, enabling the kinase to phosphorylate appropriate substrates. The kinase transfers the terminal phosphate of ATP to suitable amino acid residues. It does not transfer calcium to a target as a covalent modification.

The arrangement of sensor and kinase in a single molecule allows a calcium transient to be converted directly into a change in phosphorylation activity. Nevertheless, not every CPK responds at exactly the same calcium concentration. The family contains different proteins with different sensitivities, targets and cellular distributions. Lipid modification or interaction with other molecules can position some CPKs near the plasma membrane, where they are exposed to localized calcium changes and can phosphorylate transport or signaling proteins. Other CPKs contribute to different cellular processes. This distribution helps explain why a whole-cell calcium measurement may fail to predict activity of a particular CPK.

A representative stress-related target is a plasma membrane anion channel in a guard cell. When appropriate CPKs are active, their phosphorylation of channel regulatory sites can increase channel activity. The cell then loses anions, its membrane potential changes, and other ion fluxes can follow. However, several kinases and phosphatases can regulate the same channel. A student should therefore distinguish the observation that a CPK phosphorylates a channel from the stronger claim that the channel can function only through that CPK. Testing multiple kinases or measuring different phosphosites can reveal complementary regulatory mechanisms.

The CBL–CIPK system separates calcium binding and protein phosphorylation into two interacting proteins. CBL stands for calcineurin B-like protein. A CBL contains calcium-binding motifs and can associate with a specific group of kinases called CBL-interacting protein kinases, abbreviated CIPKs. The CBL is the calcium-responsive partner; a CIPK contains the kinase domain and transfers phosphate to substrates. Despite the historical naming resemblance to the calcineurin regulatory subunit, a CBL is not itself a protein phosphatase.

CIPKs contain a conserved regulatory interaction region often called the NAF or FISL motif. This motif helps the kinase associate with its CBL partner. Some CBL proteins are membrane-associated through lipid modification or other localization mechanisms, so interaction can recruit the kinase to a particular membrane surface. The functional result is spatial organization: a kinase with a broad biochemical capacity to phosphorylate proteins is brought near a selected set of cellular targets. Calcium-dependent recognition and subcellular targeting together contribute to signal specificity.

An important example is the Arabidopsis Salt Overly Sensitive pathway. Salt exposure produces changes in intracellular calcium, and CBL4, also named SOS3, cooperates with CIPK24, also named SOS2. The kinase regulates the plasma membrane sodium/proton antiporter SOS1. Increased antiporter activity helps the cell control cytosolic sodium by using the proton gradient to support sodium extrusion. This is an excellent example of the conversion of a calcium signal into a physiologically appropriate membrane transport adjustment. It is not necessary to memorize every CBL and CIPK combination to understand the mechanism.

The plasma membrane proton ATPase contributes to the energy supply for secondary active transport by exporting protons using ATP. SOS1 does not simply remove sodium because calcium binds the transporter. Instead, the calcium sensor and kinase regulate an existing transport system whose energetics depend on membrane gradients. If a treatment collapses the proton gradient, sodium extrusion may be impaired even when the upstream calcium signal and kinase activation remain intact. This separates the information carried by a signal from the energy required to execute its physiological output.

The SOS system also illustrates why two plants with identical early calcium responses can differ in tolerance. A wild-type plant can interpret the signal through an appropriate decoder and transport effector. A mutant lacking functional SOS1 may still generate calcium but fail to restore sodium homeostasis efficiently. A mutant affecting SOS3 or SOS2 may impair regulation of the transporter rather than eliminate the transporter itself. A high internal sodium concentration can therefore result from defects at more than one point in the chain. Measuring sodium accumulation alone cannot identify the defective molecular component.

A different CBL–CIPK combination can regulate potassium uptake or other ion transport processes. This family organization permits a common calcium messenger to coordinate different homeostatic tasks. The appropriate examination rule is to identify the particular sensor, kinase, target and membrane compartment in the question. General statements that all CBLs activate SOS1, or that all CIPKs function exclusively in salt responses, are incorrect. Detailed salinity physiology, including separation of the osmotic and ionic components of salt stress, is reserved for Node 6.H.05.

A comparison of the three decoder classes reveals complementary architectures. Calmodulin primarily changes target function through calcium-dependent binding. CPKs connect binding directly to catalytic activity in one protein. CBLs partner with CIPKs and often control localization of the kinase as well as its regulation. Their downstream outputs can overlap, and interactions with other signaling pathways are expected. A mutant removing one decoder may therefore leave calcium concentrations normal while modifying a particular output, whereas an upstream calcium entry defect can change multiple decoder pathways at once.

Experiments examining calcium-dependent phosphorylation must control calcium availability and ATP concentration separately. Removing calcium from an in vitro mixture may reduce activity because the regulatory protein is no longer activated. Removing ATP prevents ordinary phosphorylation even if calcium activates the protein normally. Including a kinase-dead version of the protein provides a control for the catalytic step. A target with a mutated phosphorylation site helps establish the relevance of the site, although changes to the site's structure may themselves affect function. Combining biochemical and genetic evidence is more convincing than using either alone.

5. Protein phosphorylation and MAP kinase cascades

Phosphorylation allows the activity of a protein to change quickly without changing the amount of protein present. A protein kinase transfers phosphate from ATP to a suitable residue in a substrate, while a phosphatase removes phosphate by hydrolysis. The addition of phosphate changes local charge and can alter protein conformation, association with partners, cellular localization, catalytic activity or stability. There is no universal rule that phosphorylation always activates proteins. Depending on the protein and site, it may increase activity, decrease activity or change another property. A pathway diagram must therefore indicate the experimentally established direction of regulation rather than assume that every phosphate is an activating mark.

One widely used kinase architecture is the mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade, usually abbreviated MAPK cascade. It contains three successive kinase levels: MAP kinase kinase kinase, MAP kinase kinase and MAP kinase. Common abbreviations are MAPKKK, MKK or MAPKK, and MPK or MAPK. An activated MAPKKK phosphorylates and regulates an appropriate MKK, which in turn phosphorylates an MPK. The MPK can then modify other proteins, including transcriptional regulators. The terminology describes the position in a cascade: a MAP kinase kinase is a kinase whose target is a MAP kinase, not a second name for the final MAP kinase.

Activation of conventional MAPKs involves phosphorylation of both a threonine and a tyrosine in an activation-loop motif of the form T-X-Y, where X represents a variable amino acid. MKKs are dual-specificity kinases with the capacity to phosphorylate the two types of residues. This contrasts with the predominantly serine/threonine substrate specificity of many other kinases in the network. The dual phosphorylation stabilizes a conformation that supports catalytic activity. Removal of one or both regulatory phosphates can reduce the activity of the MPK. The amount of the MPK protein need not change for activity to change substantially.

A cascade allows amplification because one active kinase molecule can phosphorylate multiple substrate molecules over time. Suppose, as an intentionally simplified model, one MAPKKK activates ten MKK molecules and each active MKK activates ten MPK molecules before being inactivated. Under assumptions of sufficient substrate, ATP and negligible dephosphorylation during this interval, up to one hundred MPK molecules could be activated. The example shows why a relatively small upstream event can produce a large downstream response. It is not a fixed stoichiometric rule of MAPK signaling. Real amplification depends on enzyme rates, substrate availability, phosphatases, spatial confinement and the duration of stimulation.

A two-state model helps distinguish kinase activity from phosphorylation fraction. Let f represent the fraction of a target pool that is phosphorylated. If phosphorylation occurs with an effective first-order rate constant a and dephosphorylation with constant b, then df/dt = a(1 − f) − bf. At steady state, f = a/(a + b). With a = 0.06 s⁻¹ and b = 0.02 s⁻¹, the predicted fraction is 0.75. If kinase activity remains constant but phosphatase activity doubles, b becomes 0.04 s⁻¹ and f decreases to 0.60. This illustrates why observing a lower phosphorylation fraction does not uniquely establish that the kinase was inhibited: increased phosphate removal is an alternative explanation.

The steady-state expression assumes a fixed total target pool, constant effective rates and well-mixed molecules. In a real cascade, kinase activity can itself be time dependent, individual phosphorylation sites may differ in function, and enzymes can approach saturation. Nonetheless, the simple model provides a useful conceptual foundation for understanding negative regulation and resetting of the pathway. If stimulation ends and phosphorylation becomes negligible, the model predicts an exponential decay of the phosphorylated fraction with rate constant b. The corresponding half-time is ln(2)/b. A phosphatase defect can therefore produce a sustained signal even after the original stimulus has disappeared.

MAPK signaling participates in both abiotic and biotic stress responses. Arabidopsis MPK3 and MPK6 are familiar examples of MAPKs that respond to multiple environmental and defense-related inputs. The exact upstream kinase combination and physiological output depend on the stimulus and tissue. A receptor-associated protein may participate in transmitting a microbial ligand signal to a kinase module, whereas altered cellular redox status or another type of perturbation can modify signaling during abiotic stress. It is not justified to draw a single obligatory three-kinase sequence for every plant stress.

A kinase cascade is also a possible source of specificity. Different stimuli may activate the same terminal MPK for different durations. Different tissues contain distinct substrates, interaction partners and phosphatases. Consequently, a transient MPK6 activation in one cell need not produce the same transcriptional response as a sustained activation in another. A signaling node can function as a shared element without erasing the identity of the stimulus. In examination questions, the identity of the activated kinase is only part of the evidence; timing, localization and target availability also matter.

MAPKs can modify transcription factors, thereby linking rapid signaling to changes in gene expression. Phosphorylation may alter a transcription factor's DNA-binding capacity, ability to recruit co-regulators or lifetime. Because transcription factors often control multiple target genes, the consequences can be broad. The kinase does not directly transcribe RNA. Instead, it regulates proteins that influence transcription machinery and promoter function. This distinction prevents an incorrect shortcut from a phosphorylation event to a claim of direct gene activation.

A useful experimental approach is to measure total MPK protein and phosphorylated MPK protein separately. An antibody recognizing the phosphorylated activation-loop motif can provide evidence of activation, while an antibody to the total protein can indicate whether changes in the activation signal merely reflect different protein amounts. Equal loading and appropriate specificity controls are important. A kinase assay using a suitable substrate provides additional evidence of catalytic activity. Neither immunoblotting nor a kinase assay alone identifies all physiological targets of the active protein.

For example, suppose total MPK abundance is similar in unstressed and treated tissue, but an activation-loop phospho-specific signal increases fivefold at five minutes and returns toward baseline by thirty minutes. A reasonable inference is transient regulation of the kinase rather than a fivefold increase in its synthesis. If a target transcript increases only after the kinase activation, the ordering is compatible with a causal relationship but does not establish it. The transcript might be regulated through a parallel route activated by the same stimulus. Genetic perturbation of the kinase, restoration by complementation and evidence of a relevant target interaction strengthen the argument.

The importance of controlling kinase signaling is evident from trade-offs. A transient activation can promote acclimation, whereas inappropriate persistent activation may redirect cellular resources away from growth or promote injury-associated responses. Phosphatases, protein turnover and upstream feedback help restrict the duration of the response. Several signaling branches can converge on the same output, and the cell can use a combination of phosphorylation and calcium-dependent regulation to make the output more robust. This network perspective is necessary before examining reactive oxygen species as a messenger rather than simply a product of stress.

6. Reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide as signaling intermediates

Reactive oxygen species are chemically diverse oxygen-containing molecules with enhanced reactivity relative to molecular oxygen in its ordinary ground state. Common examples in plant physiology include superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical and singlet oxygen. These species differ substantially in chemical lifetime, charge, formation mechanism and capacity to interact with cellular targets. Treating them as interchangeable obscures the mechanism of both signaling and injury. Hydrogen peroxide is relatively longer-lived than hydroxyl radical under many cellular conditions and can participate in regulated signaling over useful distances. Hydroxyl radicals react extremely rapidly near their site of formation and are more difficult to use as controlled long-range messengers.

Stress can change reactive oxygen production by altering the operation of photosynthetic and respiratory electron transfer, peroxisomal metabolism and plasma membrane enzymes. In photosynthetic tissue, excess excitation relative to metabolic consumption can increase the risk of unwanted electron transfer or energy transfer involving oxygen. In a distinct regulated mechanism, respiratory burst oxidase homologs, or RBOHs, deliberately transfer electrons from cytosolic NADPH across the plasma membrane to extracellular oxygen. The immediate product is superoxide in the apoplastic compartment. Superoxide can undergo dismutation to form hydrogen peroxide and oxygen. Thus, an apoplastic hydrogen peroxide signal can arise from regulated membrane-associated enzyme activity rather than only from accidental metabolic leakage.

RBOHs contain regulatory regions that can respond to calcium, as well as sites whose phosphorylation affects activity. Calcium-dependent protein kinases and other kinases can regulate particular RBOH isoforms. This arrangement creates a connection between early calcium signaling and controlled oxidant production. Conversely, extracellular hydrogen peroxide can influence calcium signaling. Arabidopsis HPCA1, a receptor-like kinase with extracellular cysteine residues responsive to hydrogen peroxide, provides evidence that cells can detect external oxidant changes and couple them to calcium entry. The existence of such a mechanism does not mean that HPCA1 is the receptor for all reactive oxygen species in all plant tissues.

Positive feedback occurs when the consequence of activation promotes further activation of the same process or of an upstream component. In a simplified calcium–ROS loop, calcium entry stimulates RBOH activity, extracellular oxidant production promotes further calcium signaling, and the newly elevated calcium stimulates further oxidant production. Such feedback can amplify a weak stimulus and help propagate signaling between cells. It must be balanced by negative regulation, because unrestricted amplification would consume reducing equivalents, disrupt ion homeostasis and increase oxidative injury.

Hydrogen peroxide can change protein activity through oxidation of susceptible amino acid residues, especially certain cysteine thiols. Some oxidation states can be reversed through cellular reducing systems, enabling a regulatory switch. More extensive oxidation can be irreversible or require replacement of the damaged protein. Therefore redox signaling depends on more than the presence of hydrogen peroxide: production, removal, protein accessibility and competing reactions determine which targets are modified. A concentration measured in a bulk extract is not equivalent to the concentration experienced by a particular signaling protein in a particular compartment.

The same molecule can participate in signaling at one exposure and damage at another. Imagine a short, localized increase in extracellular hydrogen peroxide sufficient to influence a membrane sensor, followed by efficient removal. The cell can respond and return toward its initial state. In contrast, prolonged production in the presence of limited antioxidant capacity may allow oxidation to spread to proteins and membrane lipids that were not intended signaling targets. The difference is not simply that one plant contains ROS and the other does not. Both contain ROS; the location, magnitude, timing and control of the exposure differ.

This balance can be summarized with a mass balance for an illustrative compartment: dH/dt = P − kH, where H is hydrogen peroxide concentration, P is an effective production rate and k is an effective first-order removal constant. If production is constant, the steady concentration is H = P/k. A basal production rate of 0.10 µM s⁻¹ and removal constant of 0.20 s⁻¹ give a steady value of 0.50 µM. If production doubles, the predicted steady value doubles if removal does not change. If removal simultaneously doubles, the same steady concentration could be maintained despite a twofold increase in production and removal flux. Thus, a normal steady concentration does not necessarily imply that oxidative metabolism is unchanged.

This idealized model assumes uniform distribution, constant rates and unsaturated removal. Actual cells possess compartment-specific production systems and antioxidant enzymes that can exhibit nonlinear kinetics. Hydrogen peroxide movement is restricted by reactions and membranes, and some aquaporins can facilitate its movement under appropriate conditions. A quantitative model is useful because it shows how distinct mechanisms can produce identical endpoint measurements. Time courses and independent measurements of production or scavenging are needed to resolve the alternatives. Detailed antioxidant pathways and oxidative injury are developed in Node 6.H.03.

Nitric oxide, abbreviated NO, is another small signaling molecule relevant to plant stress physiology. It is a reactive nitrogen species rather than a reactive oxygen species. It contains an unpaired electron and can participate in reactions with metal centers, thiols and other radicals. Its chemical properties allow it to modify proteins and interact with redox signaling networks. In plants it has been implicated in processes including stomatal regulation, defense, root responses and acclimation to environmental stress, although its exact role is context-dependent.

A major established route of nitric oxide production involves nitrite reduction. Nitrate reductase, familiar from nitrogen metabolism, can contribute to nitric oxide formation under suitable conditions. Mitochondrial processes can also contribute, particularly under conditions affecting oxygen availability and nitrite metabolism. It would be incorrect to state that higher plants possess a universally established animal-type nitric oxide synthase as their principal source. Enzymatic activities resembling nitric oxide synthase have been reported, and some algal lineages have related proteins, but the canonical animal-type enzyme has not been established as a general source in land plants.

One mechanism through which NO influences proteins is S-nitrosation, also commonly termed S-nitrosylation in the biological literature. A cysteine thiol is converted into an S-nitrosothiol. The modification can alter a protein's activity or interactions, depending on the protein and site, and can be reversible. S-nitrosoglutathione constitutes an important related pool of nitrosothiols. The enzyme S-nitrosoglutathione reductase regulates GSNO metabolism and can influence the cellular level of protein S-nitrosation indirectly. This is not equivalent to claiming that the enzyme directly removes an NO group from every modified protein.

Nitric oxide also reacts with superoxide to form peroxynitrite, a reactive nitrogen-containing oxidant. The competition among NO, superoxide dismutation and other reactions means that changing one signaling species can alter the availability of another. Under some conditions the interactions contribute to regulation; under other conditions they contribute to nitrosative or oxidative injury. An experiment that increases NO cannot be interpreted without considering the prevailing redox environment. A beneficial low-dose response does not establish that greater NO production will necessarily be beneficial.

Chemical probes are useful but have limitations. Some fluorescent NO probes respond through chemical reaction pathways involving oxidation products of nitric oxide rather than binding NO directly. Different redox conditions can therefore influence probe signals. A chemical donor may release NO at a rate dependent on the medium and can have other chemical effects. A scavenger may modify more than the intended molecule. A strong causal interpretation uses complementary methods, such as genetic perturbations, appropriate donor controls and independent physiological measurements. Similar care is needed for reactive oxygen probes, which may not distinguish each reactive species selectively.

NO, calcium, ROS and protein phosphorylation interact in networks rather than a universal fixed sequence. An ABA-treated guard cell may show changes in several of these mediators. Blocking one mediator may reduce an output because it participates in the response, but the result does not establish that every other mediator lies strictly downstream of it. Reciprocal feedback and parallel regulation are common. The next section examines ABA because its receptor–phosphatase–kinase module provides a well-established molecular example of how a hormonal signal regulates these broader networks.

7. ABA-dependent responses and their molecular switch

The hormone abscisic acid, abbreviated ABA, provides a bridge between local environmental perturbation and coordinated physiological regulation. During water deficit, changes in ABA biosynthesis, turnover, conjugation and transport can increase the active hormone available to responding cells. These processes are not identical to ABA perception. Biosynthesis determines how much hormone is produced, whereas perception determines whether the available hormone is translated into a molecular response. A defect in hormone production and a defect in perception may produce similar visible symptoms but have different implications for experimental rescue.

ABA is derived from carotenoid precursors through a series of reactions that includes cleavage by 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenases, commonly called NCEDs. Subsequent reactions generate active ABA. The hormone pool is regulated by catabolism, conjugation and release from conjugated forms, as well as movement between cells. Detailed biosynthesis belongs to Node 6.D.07; the critical principle here is that the increase in ABA available to a cell can be controlled at several steps. Measuring ABA abundance after stress does not by itself establish which of these steps changed.

The central receptor mechanism became particularly clear through studies in 2009 that identified the PYR/PYL/RCAR family of soluble ABA receptors. Before considering the hormone-bound receptor, consider the cell in a state of relatively low ABA. Group A protein phosphatases of the PP2C family restrain the activity of key SnRK2 protein kinases. These phosphatases remove regulatory phosphate groups and can interact directly with the kinases. Among the familiar Arabidopsis phosphatases are ABI1, ABI2 and HAB1. The names arose from different genetic and biochemical studies, but their relevant shared role is negative regulation of ABA signaling.

A rise in ABA allows the hormone to bind to a suitable PYR/PYL receptor. The receptor changes conformation and associates with a PP2C, inhibiting its catalytic activity. Because the phosphatase is itself inhibitory to SnRK2, the consequence is release of a brake on kinase activity. This is a double-negative regulatory arrangement: a hormone-dependent receptor inhibits an inhibitor. The net result is an increase in signaling through appropriate SnRK2s. Full activation of a kinase also depends on its phosphorylation state and upstream regulation; merely removing phosphatase inhibition is not necessarily sufficient under every condition.

SnRK2 refers to SNF1-related protein kinase 2. Although the name reflects evolutionary relationships among kinase families, SnRK2 should not be confused with SnRK1, a distinct kinase system involved in energy-related regulation. Within the SnRK2 family, several members are strongly activated by ABA, whereas other members are activated mainly by osmotic stress and are relatively less responsive or unresponsive to ABA. This diversity is an important reason why osmotic signaling cannot be reduced to a single ABA-dependent route. For the core ABA pathway, SnRK2.2, SnRK2.3 and SnRK2.6 are particularly useful representative kinases in Arabidopsis.

SnRK2.6 is also known as OPEN STOMATA 1, or OST1. Its name reflects a phenotype that helped reveal its role in guard cell responses. A wild-type guard cell can use ABA signaling to adjust channel activity and reduce water loss through the stomatal pore. A plant with an impaired OST1 pathway can have a reduced capacity for this response even if it accumulates ABA during dehydration. Thus, the hormone concentration and the response competence of its target cell represent distinct variables.

The ABA receptor pathway provides a powerful example of biochemical reconstruction. In an appropriate in vitro system, a PP2C can be tested for its phosphatase activity in the presence and absence of a receptor and ABA. If ABA alone does not inhibit the phosphatase but ABA together with the receptor does, the result supports a receptor-dependent effect. The original 2009 work by Park and colleagues used chemical genetic and protein interaction evidence to establish this regulatory relationship. Other studies independently supported the receptor–phosphatase–kinase model. These experiments connected hormonal recognition to a specific inhibitory interaction rather than treating ABA simply as an unexplained inducer of stress responses.

A receptor mutant may be relatively insensitive to applied ABA even when hormone concentrations in the tissue are high. A biosynthetic mutant may show reduced endogenous hormone production but respond to supplied ABA if uptake, perception and downstream regulation remain functional. A mutant affecting a downstream kinase may fail to respond to the same hormone addition despite functional receptors. These different rescue patterns provide an accessible approach to distinguishing pathway levels. They are most informative when direct hormone measurements, matched treatment conditions and genetic complementation are included.

The behavior of phosphatase mutations requires particular care. A loss-of-function mutation in a negative regulator may enhance a response or increase sensitivity, particularly when other phosphatases do not compensate. A dominant mutation that makes the phosphatase less sensitive to receptor inhibition can instead produce ABA insensitivity because it continues restraining downstream signaling even when ABA is present. Two alleles of a gene encoding an inhibitor therefore need not produce the same phenotype. An examination question that simply calls a plant a phosphatase mutant is incomplete unless the nature of the mutation is specified.

The ABA pathway generates rapid and slower outputs. In guard cells, phosphorylation of transport proteins can alter ion movement. In the nucleus, SnRK2s phosphorylate transcriptional regulators including members of the AREB/ABF family. These regulators influence expression of genes involved in protective responses. Both outputs originate from related regulatory events, but their timescales differ. A short pulse of ABA can change conductance before the abundance of a stress-inducible protein has changed appreciably. During sustained stress, transcriptional changes can become especially important for maintenance of acclimation.

To understand the guard cell output, recall that stomatal aperture depends on the turgor of the two guard cells and the mechanical arrangement of their walls. When anion channels such as SLAC1 are activated, anions can leave the guard cell through the plasma membrane. Their outward movement contributes to depolarization. Depolarization favors activation of appropriate outward potassium channels, supporting potassium loss. The combined loss of osmotically active solutes lowers the capacity of the cell to retain water, and water leaves under appropriate potential gradients. Turgor falls and the pore narrows. The details of a given cell's voltage and ion flux depend on the available channels and prevailing electrochemical gradients.

Calcium-dependent kinases can also activate components of the guard cell transport system. ABA can promote ROS production through regulated RBOH activity, which can in turn contribute to calcium entry. OST1 and calcium-dependent pathways can therefore converge on the regulation of ion channels. A simplified diagram may depict one pathway as an ABA-dependent kinase branch and another as a calcium-dependent branch. This distinction is useful for reasoning, but does not imply that the branches are fully independent in an intact guard cell. They share inputs, regulatory components and targets.

The physiological value of closing stomata is a reduction in transpirational water loss when water supply becomes limiting. The cost is that carbon dioxide entry is also restricted, which can reduce photosynthetic carbon gain. Depending on conditions, decreased evaporative cooling can also increase leaf temperature. The optimal response is consequently not always maximal closure. It must balance water conservation, carbon fixation and thermal regulation. A plant with constitutively active ABA signaling may survive a severe water deficit better in a short assay yet have reduced biomass or yield under other conditions.

When water becomes available again, ABA levels and signaling activity may decline, ion transport may shift and stomata may reopen. This recovery depends on the actual state of the plant: if damage has accumulated in membranes or transport systems, removing the original stress may not restore function immediately. Negative regulators and hormone turnover contribute to resetting the system, but repair of injury represents a separate physiological requirement. It is important to distinguish a reversible signaling state from irreversible damage when judging a plant's stress tolerance.

8. Transcriptional reprogramming: ABRE, DRE, NAC and WRKY

A stress response that relies only on existing proteins has limited capacity for long-term adjustment. Sustained changes in temperature, water availability or pathogen exposure can require altered abundance of enzymes, transporters, protective proteins and regulatory factors. Transcriptional reprogramming is the coordinated adjustment of gene expression to the new physiological condition. A plant does not simply turn every stress-related gene on. Some genes increase expression, others decrease, and some remain unchanged. These patterns reflect the combined activity of transcription factors, chromatin state, signaling proteins and the developmental competence of the tissue.

The promoter of a gene includes DNA sequences that help regulate transcription. A cis-regulatory element is a sequence that influences expression of a linked gene on the same DNA molecule. A trans-acting factor is a diffusible regulatory molecule, commonly a transcription factor, that can recognize particular regulatory elements and influence transcription. The distinction is easiest to understand in a reporter experiment. A mutation in the promoter of a reporter gene changes how that particular DNA construct responds. A mutation in a transcription factor can affect many genes with suitable binding sites because the factor is available to act on multiple DNA molecules.

A DNA-binding transcription factor usually has at least two conceptually distinct functions. One region participates in recognition of particular DNA sequences, and another region or interaction surface influences transcription through association with other proteins. A transcription factor that binds a promoter need not activate transcription under every condition. It may require phosphorylation, an appropriate co-regulator, or a chromatin environment that permits access. Conversely, a regulatory factor can influence a gene indirectly by altering the activity or abundance of another transcription factor. Promoter occupancy and altered transcript abundance are therefore related but distinct types of evidence.

One of the major ABA-responsive promoter elements is the ABRE, or ABA-responsive element. Typical ABREs contain an ACGT core within a broader sequence context. A group of basic leucine zipper transcription factors known as AREBs or ABFs recognizes relevant ABA-responsive promoter sequences. The basic region contributes to DNA recognition, while the leucine zipper supports dimer formation. Phosphorylation by ABA-responsive SnRK2s can enhance the regulatory activity of these transcription factors. Thus, the ABA receptor module can ultimately alter transcription through a specific protein modification and DNA recognition system.

The presence of an ABRE-like sequence is not a guarantee of ABA responsiveness. Short DNA sequences occur frequently by chance, and biological activity depends on local sequence context, arrangement of multiple promoter elements, chromatin accessibility and the available transcription factors. Some ABA-responsive promoters require cooperation between an ABRE and additional elements. A useful experimental test compares the response of a reporter driven by an intact promoter with the response of an otherwise comparable reporter containing a mutation in the proposed element. If the mutation selectively reduces ABA-induced expression, it supports a functional contribution of that element in the tested construct.

Plants also possess routes by which stress-responsive genes can be induced without depending entirely on an increase in ABA signaling. The dehydration-responsive element, abbreviated DRE, and the closely related C-repeat or CRT element are well-known examples. Their characteristic core includes A/GCCGAC. DRE-binding proteins belong to the AP2/ERF family of transcription factors, in which a conserved DNA-binding domain recognizes appropriate regulatory sequences. Two groups particularly useful for understanding abiotic stress are DREB1/CBF and DREB2. Their activities connect different environmental inputs to overlapping sets of stress-responsive genes.

In a classic study published in 1998, Liu and colleagues isolated Arabidopsis DREB transcription factors that could recognize the dehydration-responsive element. The expression of representative DREB1/CBF genes increased after low-temperature treatment, whereas representative DREB2 genes responded strongly to dehydration. Overexpression of DREB1A activated target gene expression even in the absence of the normal stress and was associated with altered growth. These findings linked promoter recognition to stress-responsive transcription and illustrated that an artificially activated protective program can have developmental costs. They did not establish that every member of either family responds to exactly one stimulus in all species.

DREB1/CBF-dependent regulation is especially helpful for reasoning about cold acclimation. A low-temperature signal can increase the expression or activity of CBF regulators, leading to altered transcription of downstream cold-responsive genes. DREB2-associated regulation is particularly relevant to dehydration and heat responses. In some cases, regulation of protein stability or activity determines whether an induced DREB transcript actually produces an active transcriptional response. Therefore the presence of DREB2 mRNA after stress cannot by itself establish the activity of DREB2 protein. Translational regulation and protein turnover remain possible intervening steps.

The term ABA-independent must be used carefully. It describes a response that can be initiated or maintained without the particular ABA-dependent route being considered. It does not mean that every gene in the pathway is completely insulated from ABA under every condition. Promoters may contain both ABRE and DRE elements, and upstream signaling networks can intersect. A DREB family member may itself be influenced by other hormonal pathways depending on its identity and tissue. The appropriate conclusion is that distinguishable regulatory routes exist, not that the routes never interact.

NAC transcription factors form another large plant regulatory family. The abbreviation derives from three founding members, NAM, ATAF and CUC. A typical NAC protein has a relatively conserved amino-terminal DNA-binding region and a more variable carboxyl-terminal regulatory region. Differences in this regulatory region, expression pattern and interaction partners allow family members to perform different roles. Some NACs are associated with dehydration, salinity, senescence or defense, while others participate in developmental programs. It is scientifically inaccurate to describe all NAC proteins as positive regulators of drought tolerance.

Arabidopsis ANAC019, ANAC055 and ANAC072, the last also known as RD26, provide representative examples of stress-related NAC regulators. Tran and colleagues studied factors capable of binding a regulatory sequence associated with the ERD1 gene, whose expression responds to dehydration. Their experiments linked NAC DNA recognition to a group of stress-responsive genes and examined the phenotypes of plants overexpressing selected NACs. The evidence illustrates that a transcription factor can contribute to a network of downstream targets, while also showing why promoter regulation often requires cooperation with additional factors.

NACs help reveal the difference between a genetic association and a direct regulatory target. If overexpression of a NAC changes the abundance of fifty transcripts, not all fifty genes need contain functional NAC-binding sites. Some changes can arise through secondary transcription factors or altered physiological conditions. To establish a direct target, evidence of promoter association and a functional regulatory effect must be combined. A DNA-binding assay can show that a factor recognizes a sequence, while a promoter reporter or expression experiment can reveal whether that recognition changes transcription in a particular cellular context.

WRKY proteins are another prominent family of plant transcription factors, named for a characteristic WRKY amino acid sequence in their DNA-binding domain. They often recognize W-box regulatory elements with a core resembling TTGAC followed by C or T. WRKY proteins contribute to defense regulation and may also participate in abiotic stress responses and hormonal cross-talk. Some are activated or otherwise regulated by MAPKs. Others alter expression of signaling components, including genes connected to ABA responses. The family includes both positive and negative regulators, so membership in the WRKY family does not define the direction of regulation.

The comparison among AREB/ABF, DREB, NAC and WRKY regulators reveals a general principle. DNA recognition gives a factor a possible set of target sequences, but signaling determines when and where it can influence those targets. A promoter can integrate several inputs by containing multiple regulatory elements. If a dehydration-responsive gene contains functional ABRE and DRE sites, its transcription may be influenced by both ABA-dependent and other stress-responsive pathways. Their contributions can be additive, cooperative or antagonistic. The exact relationship must be tested rather than inferred from the presence of two short motifs.

To illustrate combinatorial control, imagine a hypothetical reporter assay. The intact promoter gives a normalized expression of 1 without stress and 10 after dehydration. Mutating the ABRE reduces stressed expression to 6, mutating the DRE reduces it to 5, and mutating both reduces it to 1. These results suggest that both sites contribute under the tested conditions. They do not establish that the contributions are independent, because the effects depend on the baseline and may involve interactions. If mutating the ABRE also changes the baseline, comparing raw stressed values alone is insufficient; fold changes and absolute normalized expression should both be examined.

A further question is whether changing transcription produces a physiologically meaningful output. A stress-responsive transcript might encode an enzyme involved in osmotic adjustment, a protein that stabilizes macromolecules or a component of an antioxidant system. Other transcripts encode regulators that alter additional gene expression. A rise in mRNA is evidence of a molecular response, not proof that the protein has accumulated, that it is active, or that the plant is more tolerant. Protein measurement, functional assays and whole-plant phenotypes provide complementary levels of evidence. This distinction prevents the common error of identifying a gene as beneficial merely because it is induced during stress.

9. Local perception and rapid systemic communication

A plant is a multicellular organism with organs that encounter different aspects of the environment. Roots directly experience soil conditions, while leaves experience irradiance, temperature and atmospheric evaporative demand. If a root encounters a sudden increase in external solute concentration, distant leaves can adjust before they experience the same local chemical exposure. If one leaf is wounded, other leaves can initiate protective responses. This coordination requires information to move beyond the initially stimulated cells. The general process is systemic signaling, in contrast to a local response restricted to the site of perturbation.

Several modes of long-distance communication operate in plants. Hydraulic signals involve changes in the pressure or water relations of connected tissues. Electrical signals involve changes in membrane potential that propagate through excitable or conducting tissues. Chemical signals can be transported in vascular pathways or generated sequentially in responding cells. Calcium and reactive oxygen species waves represent coordinated spatial patterns of signaling activity. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive: a hydraulic change may influence membrane conductance, a membrane potential change may influence channel activity, and calcium may regulate ROS production.

A hydraulic signal should not be confused with the movement of a particular chemical messenger. Water relations can change rapidly in connected tissues when transpiration or root water supply changes. A leaf can therefore detect a change in its own hydraulic state before a newly synthesized root hormone reaches it. Conversely, chemical signals such as ABA can participate in sustained coordination. Roots, vascular-associated cells and other shoot tissues can contribute to ABA regulation, and local ABA production may be important in a responding leaf. It is misleading to assume that all drought-induced stomatal closure requires ABA synthesized exclusively in roots and transported unchanged to leaves.

Electrical signals arise because ions cross membranes through channels and transport proteins. A local depolarization can influence neighboring cells and interact with other signaling processes. Plants do not need neurons to produce electrical signals, because membrane potentials and voltage-sensitive transport mechanisms occur in many living cells. However, it is equally misleading to identify a plant electrical wave with an animal nerve impulse in every detail. Plant electrical responses include different temporal and physiological patterns, and the underlying mechanisms depend on tissue and stimulus.

In a study published in 2014, Choi and colleagues used calcium imaging to examine responses to local salt stress in Arabidopsis roots. They observed a rapid spatially propagating calcium response associated with root-to-shoot communication. Genetic evidence implicated the vacuolar channel TPC1 in aspects of propagation. The relevant conclusion is that a local salt perturbation can initiate a calcium-dependent systemic process, not that TPC1 is the sole calcium entry channel or that its exact ion-conduction role in every stage of the wave is fully established. Genetic dependence on a channel does not by itself prove which ionic current carries the propagated signal.

Wounding provides another instructive example. Toyota and colleagues in 2018 used genetically encoded calcium imaging to investigate long-distance defense signaling after tissue damage. Glutamate released in association with damage can act as a signal, and particular glutamate receptor-like channels, including GLR3.3 and GLR3.6, participate in rapid electrical and calcium responses. This shows that an ordinary cellular metabolite can serve as information when its spatial availability changes. Glutamate inside an intact cell has metabolic roles; its appearance in a relevant extracellular environment after tissue damage can have a signaling role.

The glutamate receptor-like proteins of plants share evolutionary and functional features with ligand-regulated channels, but plant signaling does not depend on a nervous system. The physiological question is how damaged cells initiate information transfer to intact cells. If a receptor-like channel responds to an amino acid and contributes to a calcium increase, this can link the presence of a damage-associated compound to subsequent responses. The evidence must be interpreted in the context of the actual tissue and the receptor family member; not every amino acid or every GLR produces the same whole-plant outcome.

ROS waves involve regenerative production of reactive oxygen species. A cell receiving a signal may activate an RBOH enzyme and generate new extracellular superoxide and hydrogen peroxide. The next cell can respond to that oxidant environment and initiate its own production. This relay provides a way to transmit information farther than one reactive molecule could diffuse before being removed. RBOHD has been implicated in rapid systemic signaling in several contexts. The key conceptual distinction is between transport of a stable molecule from one organ to another and repeated local generation of a short-lived signaling species along a route.

HPCA1-related signaling provides a possible link between extracellular oxidant perception and calcium propagation. Studies have shown that loss of HPCA1 can impair systemic ROS and calcium responses to particular stimuli while electrical responses may persist. Such evidence is valuable because it separates processes that can be coupled in a wild-type plant. If two waves always appeared simultaneously in normal tissue, one might incorrectly conclude that they are a single indivisible event. Mutants that uncouple them reveal that different processes can cooperate without being identical.

Systemic communication can generate systemic acquired acclimation, in which a local abiotic challenge changes the capacity of distant tissues to withstand a later challenge. It is important to distinguish the rapid signal from the eventual protective state. A distal calcium increase is evidence that information reached a tissue, but a subsequent challenge experiment is needed to show that its stress resistance changed. Similarly, systemic acquired resistance refers to a particular form of immune-related systemic protection, discussed later in 6.H.12. Both terms refer to altered physiological capacity, not simply to the movement of an early messenger.

Consider a simple experimental system in which a local leaf receives a high-light treatment while another leaf remains shaded. Calcium reporter activity and defense-related transcription are measured in both leaves. A systemic response is supported if the shaded leaf changes despite being protected from direct treatment. However, the investigator must exclude unintended exposure, changes in the shared air environment, and general temperature changes caused by the treatment. Local stress should be applied in a way that separates the stressed tissue from the remote readout without accidentally introducing a different stress that spreads through the plant.

The speed of propagation can help evaluate mechanistic models. If a reporter front moves 30 mm in 60 seconds, its average apparent speed is 0.5 mm s⁻¹, equivalent to 3 cm min⁻¹. This does not identify the molecular carrier on its own. The measured front could reflect a relay, an electrical event coupled to calcium or a combination of processes. To compare speeds across treatments, the same definition of front arrival, imaging interval and distance measurement should be used. Curved vascular routes can also make straight-line distance an underestimate of the actual path length.

The importance of systemic signaling is particularly clear in situations where a local disturbance predicts a threat to distant tissue. A root stress may indicate that the whole plant will soon encounter limited water supply. A wound may predict additional herbivory. Rapid communication can initiate adjustments before the distant tissue experiences direct injury. Yet excessive systemic defense activation also has costs. The plant must regulate both signal spread and the response of receiving tissues. This requires integration with the hormonal and transcriptional networks already described.

10. Experimental reasoning: connecting molecules to causal mechanisms

The strength of a proposed signaling mechanism depends on how well the observations distinguish it from other plausible explanations. The same physiological phenotype can arise from a defect in perception, messenger production, messenger decoding, effector activation or cellular energy supply. For example, failure of a root to maintain ion homeostasis after salt exposure could reflect a defect in calcium entry, a failure of a calcium-dependent kinase, a nonfunctional transporter or inadequate ATP supply to the proton pump. These explanations lead to different predictions about early signaling and the response to experimental interventions.

A useful experiment measures more than one level of the pathway. If a stress-treated mutant fails to induce a protective transcript, measure an early messenger, the activation state of an intermediate kinase and the final transcriptional output. A normal early calcium transient in a mutant with defective transcription suggests that the defect may lie downstream of calcium or in a parallel branch. A reduced calcium transient and reduced transcription are compatible with a defect in early signaling, but the interpretation is not unique. The mutant might alter cellular health or the ability to maintain ion gradients. Complementation and controls for basal physiology are therefore important.

Time-resolved observations help establish a plausible order of events. Suppose an illustrative wild-type root exhibits a calcium increase within seconds, increased kinase phosphorylation after two minutes, and increased abundance of a target transcript after twenty minutes. The observed order is consistent with a route from calcium to the kinase and then to transcription. Nevertheless, temporal precedence alone does not establish that calcium activates the kinase. A single sensor could trigger both events through separate routes. Selectively perturbing calcium entry and measuring kinase activation can distinguish these models more effectively than simply collecting additional time points.

It is also necessary to distinguish necessity from sufficiency. If removal of a particular calcium sensor eliminates the induction of a gene under a defined treatment, the sensor is necessary for that response in the tested genetic background. This does not mean that activating the sensor alone is sufficient to induce the gene in an unstressed plant. A second input, such as an active transcription factor or a hormone-dependent phosphorylation event, may be required. Conversely, an artificially activated kinase may be sufficient to induce a response even though normal plants can generate the same response through redundant pathways.

Genetic complementation is the restoration of a mutant phenotype by providing a functional version of the affected gene in an appropriate context. It supports a causal connection between the altered gene and the measured phenotype. A rescue by an unrelated chemical is a different kind of experiment. If an ABA-deficient mutant responds to applied ABA, the result is compatible with a defect in endogenous hormone production. It does not identify the specific mutated biosynthetic enzyme without additional evidence. In contrast, restoration by a properly expressed wild-type gene helps identify the gene involved, while the chemical rescue helps locate the defect functionally.

Epistasis analysis compares combinations of mutations to determine how gene products contribute to a biological response. In a simple linear pathway, a downstream loss-of-function mutation can mask activation of an upstream component. Suppose a constitutively active kinase increases the transcription of a stress-responsive gene even without treatment. If deletion of the target transcription factor prevents that increase, the result supports the requirement for that transcription factor downstream of or in cooperation with the kinase. However, this experiment alone does not prove direct phosphorylation of the transcription factor. The kinase could act through an intermediate protein.

A constitutively active form of a signaling protein may bypass the need for its usual upstream activator. In a hypothetical linear pathway from sensor S to kinase K to effector E, an engineered active K might restore output in an S-deficient background if the remaining machinery is intact. Failure of active K to rescue an E-deficient background would support the requirement for E. Real pathways are often branched, so partial rescue is common. A missing upstream component may normally regulate several outputs, only one of which can be restored by activating the downstream kinase. The correct interpretation is limited to the particular measured output.

Experimental manipulation of calcium with chelators can help determine whether a response requires available calcium, but localization matters. A chelator that remains extracellular mainly affects available extracellular calcium and may also influence cell wall properties. An intracellularly delivered chelator can alter cytosolic calcium dynamics but may have broader effects on cell viability. In either case, reduced signaling is not necessarily proof that a particular named channel was involved. Channel identity requires evidence from more selective genetic or electrophysiological approaches. A chelator experiment is therefore best interpreted as evidence about messenger availability rather than a complete molecular mechanism.

Likewise, pharmacological inhibitors of protein kinases or ROS production can alter several proteins or metabolic processes. If an inhibitor reduces both a calcium signal and a physiological response, it may have acted upstream, but it may also have damaged the energy supply that supports ion gradients. Vehicle controls, concentration ranges compatible with viability, and independent genetic evidence help reduce ambiguity. A single inhibitor should not be treated as perfectly specific merely because it is conventionally associated with a particular enzyme family.

When examining reporter data, distinguish between reporter abundance and reporter activity. A promoter-driven fluorescent protein indicates the expression history of the reporter construct, not necessarily the instantaneous transcription rate of the endogenous gene. Fluorescent protein maturation and degradation can delay or extend the recorded signal. A calcium-sensitive reporter is designed to change its optical properties more directly in response to calcium, but it also has finite response kinetics. Comparing these two types of reporters without considering their different timescales can lead to an incorrect proposed order of events.

An immunoblot detecting a phosphorylated protein has analogous limitations. A stronger band may indicate more phosphorylation, more total protein or differences in sample loading and detection. Normalizing the phospho-specific signal to total target protein, within the linear range of the assay, helps estimate relative phosphorylation. For example, consider hypothetical values in arbitrary units. A wild-type sample has a phospho signal of 20 and a total signal of 100 before stress, giving a ratio of 0.20. After stress, the signals are 60 and 120, giving a ratio of 0.50. The raw phospho band triples, but the normalized ratio increases 2.5-fold. This distinction becomes important when a treatment changes protein abundance.

Quantitative PCR can compare transcript abundance using a reference gene and a suitable amplification model. In the common simplified method assuming approximately equal and near-perfect amplification efficiencies, relative expression is calculated as 2 raised to the negative delta-delta Ct. Ct is the cycle at which the fluorescence signal crosses a defined threshold. Delta Ct is target Ct minus reference Ct; delta-delta Ct is the treated delta Ct minus the control delta Ct. If control target and reference Ct values are 25 and 20, the control delta Ct is 5. If treated values are 22 and 20, the treated delta Ct is 2. The delta-delta Ct is −3, giving an eightfold relative increase. This is a model-based relative estimate, not an absolute number of transcript molecules.

The calculation assumes that the reference gene is suitably stable under the conditions compared and that amplification efficiencies are compatible with the method. A reference gene that itself responds strongly to dehydration can produce a misleading apparent change in the target. Therefore, the quality of the reference is part of the experimental design rather than a trivial mathematical detail. Replicate biological samples are needed because variation among individual plants may exceed variation between repeated measurements of the same RNA preparation.

Dose-response experiments can establish whether a signaling output changes systematically with the strength of an input. A simple saturating response can be described as R = Rmax S/(K + S), where S is stimulus concentration, K is the concentration yielding half-maximal response in this model, and Rmax is the maximal modeled response. This equation need not imply that the underlying mechanism is single-site binding. A whole-cell response may reflect multiple sequential reactions that collectively produce a saturating curve. A shift in K can indicate altered apparent sensitivity, while a change in Rmax can indicate altered response capacity. These interpretations are conditional on the model being an adequate description of the observed data.

For example, if two genotypes have equal maximal stomatal responses to ABA but one requires a greater ABA concentration to achieve a half-maximal response, that genotype has reduced apparent sensitivity in the tested assay. This does not necessarily imply lower receptor affinity. Differences in hormone uptake, metabolism, receptor abundance, phosphatase activity or downstream amplification can all shift the physiological dose-response curve. Demonstrating a change in molecular binding affinity requires a separate binding measurement. Physiological sensitivity and receptor affinity are connected but not identical concepts.

The kinetics of recovery offer further information. After removal of a stimulus, a simplified signal X may decay exponentially toward baseline, with excess signal Xexcess(t) = Xexcess(0) exp(−kt). Here k has units of inverse time. If excess signal decreases from 0.8 to 0.2 arbitrary units in 20 seconds, it has undergone two halvings; its half-time is 10 seconds, and the effective decay constant is approximately 0.0693 s⁻¹. A mutant with a longer recovery half-time may have reduced removal activity, but the same result could arise from continued low-level signal production after stimulus removal. The model cannot distinguish these possibilities without independent evidence.

11. Integrating signaling networks with plant function

The purpose of a stress signaling network is to coordinate useful changes in cell function rather than simply maximize the amount of signaling. A drying plant benefits from rapid adjustment of gas exchange, but it also needs to maintain metabolism, membrane integrity and the ability to resume growth. Different outputs must therefore occur in an appropriate order and with an appropriate magnitude. A response that protects one process can impose a cost on another. The signaling system must interpret the disturbance in relation to the overall physiological state.

One recurring feature is the distinction between rapid protective responses and slower changes in capacity. Ion channel regulation can modify water loss within a relatively short interval, using proteins that are already present. Changes in gene expression can increase the amount of a protective protein or alter the abundance of a transporter over a longer interval. Neither type of response replaces the other. Rapid regulation provides time for more sustained adjustments, while sustained changes can modify the sensitivity or capacity of future rapid responses.

Negative feedback can protect the plant from an excessive response. An activated transcription factor might induce the expression of a phosphatase that restrains the original kinase pathway. An increase in ROS can induce mechanisms that increase antioxidant capacity, which in turn limits continued ROS signaling. Hormone-induced expression of catabolic or negative regulatory components can similarly restrict the duration of a response. The molecular details differ among pathways, but the general consequence is the same: the output eventually modifies the processes that generate or maintain it.

Positive feedback has a different role. It can enable a local event to amplify or spread, as in interacting calcium and ROS signals. A signal may also increase transcription of components that make cells more responsive to subsequent stimulation. Such feedback can produce strong responses to relatively small initial disturbances. It becomes risky if activation continues after the disturbance has ended. Effective physiological regulation therefore requires that amplification be constrained in space and time by removal processes, negative regulation and the availability of substrates.

A threshold can arise without a single molecular switch. If several signaling pathways converge on a promoter and the promoter responds strongly only when multiple inputs are present, weak activation of one branch may not be sufficient. Alternatively, an inhibitory protein may keep an effector inactive until upstream activity is strong enough to overcome inhibition. These arrangements reduce inappropriate responses to small fluctuations. The precise threshold can depend on developmental stage, light conditions, previous stress and the relative abundance of signaling components. A stress threshold measured for a particular experimental condition should not be treated as a permanent universal value for the species.

Hormonal cross-talk allows stress responses to be balanced with development and defense. ABA is particularly important for water relations, but its effects are integrated with other hormones. Jasmonates participate prominently in responses to wounding and herbivory, while salicylic acid has important roles in particular immune responses. Ethylene participates in diverse stress and developmental responses. These hormones can influence shared transcription factors or signaling proteins, and their effects may be cooperative or antagonistic depending on the context. It is incorrect to assume that any two hormones always oppose each other merely because antagonism is observed in a particular experiment.

A combined-stress experiment demonstrates why cross-talk matters. A plant experiencing drought alone may reduce stomatal aperture to conserve water. A plant under heat stress alone may benefit from evaporative cooling if water is available. When both stresses occur simultaneously, the two requirements may conflict. It is not valid to predict the combined response simply by adding the responses measured under each stress in isolation. The combined condition may change hormone levels, ROS production, carbon balance and tissue water status in a way that neither single treatment reproduces.

A factorial design compares control, drought alone, heat alone and combined drought plus heat under otherwise matched conditions. Suppose a hypothetical plant retains 100 units of biomass under control conditions, 80 under drought, 90 under heat and 55 under combined stress. On an additive absolute-loss model, the single treatments predict 70 units in combination, because losses of 20 and 10 are added. The observed 55 units are 15 units below that expectation, suggesting a greater combined effect than predicted by this particular additive model. If the expected model were multiplicative retention instead, the expected value would be 72 units. The conclusion about departure from expectation must always specify the scale and model used.

Stress signaling can also alter the state from which a later response begins. After a mild treatment and a recovery period, a plant may respond faster or more strongly to a second challenge. Such a phenomenon is often called priming when the prior experience enhances subsequent responsiveness. It may reflect changes in protein abundance, metabolic state, signaling competence or gene regulation. A different outcome is desensitization, in which repeated stimulation produces a smaller response. Neither outcome should be assumed from the existence of a previous stress alone. A repeated-treatment experiment with appropriate untreated and recovered controls is needed.

Persistent responses within an individual plant are not equivalent to inherited evolutionary adaptation. A regulatory state may persist after a stimulus has disappeared, but showing transmission across generations requires separate evidence and controls for parental environment, seed provisioning and genetic differences. This distinction is developed further in Node 6.H.12. For the current chapter, the key point is that signaling history can change physiological sensitivity without necessarily changing the DNA sequence or establishing transgenerational inheritance.

The same principles guide efforts to improve crop stress tolerance. A genetic alteration that increases expression of a protective transcription factor may improve survival under a particular laboratory stress but reduce growth in a well-watered environment. Tissue-specific or stress-inducible regulation can sometimes reduce this trade-off by restricting activation to conditions or organs where it is useful. However, a promising molecular phenotype must ultimately be evaluated in relation to plant performance across realistic conditions. More signaling, more antioxidant expression or more stomatal closure is not necessarily equivalent to improved productivity.

A complete mechanistic explanation links each level of organization. The environmental input changes the physical or chemical state of a cell. Sensing components convert this change into signals such as calcium, ROS or altered phosphorylation. Decoder proteins interpret these signals according to their own binding properties, location and target availability. Hormonal pathways adjust the sensitivity and coordination of the network. Transcriptional regulators modify gene expression, and effectors alter ion transport, metabolism or protective capacity. Feedback constrains the system and systemic communication allows distant tissues to participate. This framework connects the molecular processes in this chapter to the specific stresses examined in the following nodes.

The next node focuses on oxidative stress and cellular protection. Its central concern is how reactive species are generated, how they can damage cells, and how antioxidant systems maintain redox balance. The distinction already established here between controlled redox signaling and oxidative injury provides the necessary conceptual bridge. In later nodes, the same signaling principles will be used to understand dehydration, salinity, temperature stress, immune recognition and herbivory without treating each environmental stress as a completely unrelated molecular problem.

Which experimental control is especially important when testing touch-induced calcium responses?

  1. Changing reporter expression only in treated plants
  2. Comparing different species without controlling developmental stage
  3. Mock handling with comparable manipulation but without the intended touch stimulus
  4. Eliminating every untreated sample
Answer and explanation

C. Mock handling with comparable manipulation but without the intended touch stimulus. Handling itself can generate mechanical signals. Matching manipulations and using equivalent reporters supports attribution of the response to the intended stimulus.

49. SET / additional reasoning

Which outcome is a trade-off of strong constitutive drought signaling?

  1. Loss of all hormonal communication
  2. Complete elimination of oxidative chemistry
  3. Reduced carbon gain or growth under conditions where water conservation is unnecessary
  4. Guaranteed higher yield in all environments
Answer and explanation

C. Reduced carbon gain or growth under conditions where water conservation is unnecessary. Stomatal closure and constitutive protective programs can impose costs. Survival and yield are not interchangeable measures.

50. SET / additional reasoning

Which finding most directly demonstrates systemic acquired acclimation rather than merely systemic signaling?

  2. A calcium reporter changes at the local treatment site

  4. A local stress gene is present in the genome
Answer and explanation

A. A remote tissue withstands a later challenge better than an appropriately matched unprimed tissue. The definition requires altered capacity of remote tissue to cope with a challenge. Signals are evidence of communication, not by themselves evidence of protection.
