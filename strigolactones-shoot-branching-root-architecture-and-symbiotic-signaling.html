1. Why a plant regulates its own branches

A plant does not use muscles to move toward a better source of light or a more fertile patch of soil. Instead it changes the direction and amount of growth at many sites simultaneously. A shoot may activate a dormant bud and increase the number of leaves exposed to sunlight, or it may retain that bud in a slowly growing state while its main stem extends. Roots also modify their architecture, and they release substances into the narrow region of soil influenced by their activities, called the rhizosphere. Strigolactones connect these decisions. They are a chemically diverse family of carotenoid-derived signaling compounds whose activities include regulation of shoot branching and communication with certain other organisms in soil. The same term encompasses compounds with different biological activities; a molecule that is an excellent cue for a soil organism need not be the principal endogenous signal controlling a particular bud.

An axillary bud occupies the angle between a leaf and the stem. It commonly contains a young meristem and structures that can develop into a lateral shoot. The establishment of that meristem and the subsequent release of its bud into sustained growth are separate developmental events. An intact plant can therefore possess many potential branches but express relatively few of them. In cereals, branches arising near the base of the main stem are often called tillers. In both cases, the number of active shoots affects how carbohydrates and mineral nutrients are distributed. This explains why regulating bud activity is a physiological problem as well as a developmental problem. A mutation causing additional visible branches may alter dormancy or sustained elongation without increasing the number of axillary meristems initially formed.

The classical description of apical dominance concerns suppression of lateral bud growth by the growing shoot apex. Removal of the apex often releases some buds. Auxin made near the apex participates in the phenomenon, but its action cannot be understood simply as diffusion into every bud. Cytokinin and strigolactone pathways, competition for sugars and the capacity of a growing branch to establish auxin export into the main stem help determine the outcome. The existence of these additional influences becomes evident when plants with comparable apices exhibit strikingly different branching patterns because a gene needed for strigolactone production or response is defective.

The physiological task is not to minimize branching under all circumstances. A branch can supply photosynthate and eventually bear flowers or seeds. It also demands mineral nutrients, water and carbon before it becomes a productive source. Under a limited nutrient supply, additional growing shoots may compete with existing organs. An architectural response can therefore improve the allocation of resources in one setting and reduce reproductive yield in another. The regulation is conditional rather than an inflexible instruction to suppress all branches. To explain this conditionality, we must distinguish how much signal is produced, where it travels, how sensitive a target cell is and what other signals are present.

One must also distinguish branching from overall shoot growth. A plant with a short primary shoot and many short lateral shoots may contain less shoot biomass than a plant with only a few long shoots. A count of branches cannot be interpreted as a measure of total growth without information about the length and mass of those branches. In rice, dwarf and highly tillered phenotypes sometimes occur together in strigolactone pathway mutants. These are related features of the mutant's physiology, not interchangeable descriptions. If the stem elongates poorly, the number of visible tillers may still increase because the restraints on outgrowth of pre-existing basal buds have changed. Measuring internode length separately from tiller number reveals that the hormone can regulate multiple components of plant form.

A useful conceptual comparison is between a nutrient and a regulatory signal. Phosphate supplied by a root provides chemical material needed to make new nucleotides and other molecules. A strigolactone, by contrast, is not required in amounts proportional to the mass of new shoot tissue. Relatively small amounts can change the behavior of responsive cells by engaging a molecular recognition mechanism. Its effect can therefore exceed what one would predict from the amount of material it contributes. The distinction becomes especially clear during a rescue experiment: supplying a small amount of a signaling analogue can restore a pattern of growth without replacing the bulk carbon or mineral nutrients required to construct that pattern.

The chemical signal must be interpreted in relation to space. A branch at a basal node is not necessarily exposed to the same concentration, light environment or carbohydrate supply as one higher on the stem. In a single plant, some lateral buds can be growing while others remain restrained. Even genetically identical buds may respond differently because they occupy different positions. Therefore, a measurement of strigolactone in an entire shoot does not necessarily represent the signal experienced by any particular bud. The distinction between tissue-average hormone abundance and local response is a general principle of plant physiology.

A hormone-dependent phenotype can emerge over several days even when the initiating molecular events occur much faster. Perception may trigger protein turnover within a relatively short interval, followed by changes in gene expression, metabolism, cellular expansion and communication with adjacent tissues. The visible shoot phenotype is the accumulated consequence of those events. Timing is consequently central to experimental logic. If one measures only a mature plant, it may be difficult to distinguish a primary effect on bud activation from a secondary effect caused by altered resource allocation after several other buds have grown. Time series help establish the sequence of changes without requiring an assumption that every correlated event is causal.

2. Discovery: from parasitic seeds to plant architecture

The early history of strigolactones is unusual because researchers first encountered their activity through another organism. Seeds of certain root-parasitic plants remain in soil until suitable environmental conditions and chemical cues associated with nearby roots promote germination. Germination near a host increases the chance that the emerging parasite can establish a connection to a root before exhausting its reserves. Chemicals associated with plant root exudates consequently became the object of physiological investigation. Strigol was identified as a potent germination stimulant, giving its name to the broader strigolactone family. Root exudate bioactivity was a clue to chemical communication, but it did not by itself establish a function inside the producing plant.

A second biological observation connected the compounds to beneficial interactions. Akiyama and colleagues demonstrated in 2005 that purified natural strigolactones and the synthetic analogue GR24 stimulated branching of fungal hyphae emerging from spores of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus Gigaspora margarita. Branching increases the extent of exploratory fungal growth and can help a fungus encounter a root. The assay was particularly informative because a chemical effect on the fungus could be measured before physical colonization of the root. Increased branching should not be equated with completed symbiosis, however. Contact, recognition, entry into the root, formation of exchange structures and maintenance of the association are later processes with additional requirements.

Meanwhile, genetic studies of highly branched pea, Arabidopsis, petunia and rice plants identified loci needed to restrict shoot growth. Grafting experiments in suitable species had suggested that at least some defects involved the supply of a mobile branch-inhibiting influence. In 2008, studies by Gomez-Roldan and colleagues and independently by Umehara and colleagues connected the branching phenotype with strigolactone deficiency or signaling. The ability of applied strigolactones to suppress excessive branching of certain biosynthetic mutants was critical. It joined a biochemical observation to a causal physiological test. The conclusion was not that every highly branched plant lacks strigolactone; rather, a defined group of genes controls a signaling pathway whose loss can release branches.

Three lines of evidence thus converge on the modern view: extracellular chemical bioassays, mutant physiology and chemical complementation. The compounds were not recognized as hormones merely because they were detectable in root exudates. The demonstration that they or their derivatives regulate growth of the producing plant provided that additional justification. A successful rescue experiment suggests that the missing biosynthetic step lies before the applied active signal and that the downstream response machinery is sufficiently intact under the experimental conditions. It cannot identify the exact naturally transported ligand without further chemical and spatial evidence.

Before the identity of the signal was established, a major difficulty was to distinguish defects in production from defects in response. Imagine a highly branched pea plant with a mutation in one of two genes. If a normal rootstock or stem section can restore branch inhibition in the mutant scion, the result is consistent with provision of a missing influence. If the mutant scion remains highly branched on a suitable normal stock, it might be unable to respond. Such experiments provided an important framework for later chemical work. But graft failure, differences in the vascular connection and location of signal synthesis complicate interpretation, so the inference is strongest when grafting is combined with genetic and chemical tests.

The 2008 studies did not begin from a hypothesis that a particular root chemical must be a hormone. A connection was established between previously studied branching genes and the chemistry of the pathway. An important strength of this approach is that it brings together observations that were initially independent: the appearance of branching mutants, altered composition of exudates and the restoration of a physiological response after treatment. A pure compound with an effect on wild-type plants can reveal pharmacological activity, but the rescue of a biosynthetic mutant additionally ties the effect to an endogenous pathway.

A biochemical pathway is established through a different kind of reasoning. Isolating an enzyme and measuring the chemical product formed from a defined substrate can reveal a reaction that a branching phenotype cannot resolve. The identification of carlactone in the 2012 work provided a missing connection between the known carotenoid cleavage enzymes and the chemistry of SL-like compounds. Earlier genetic evidence had established that the genes were necessary for normal branch control; biochemical evidence explained why. Subsequent characterization of P450 enzymes and signaling proteins added different parts of the mechanism. A textbook sequence that presents synthesis first and perception second is convenient for learning, but the order of historical discoveries was driven by the experimental questions that investigators could answer at the time.

This history demonstrates a recurring feature of biology: the organism used in an assay need not be the organism that normally produces the measured signal. Parasitic seed germination assays and fungal hyphal branching assays are sensitive ways to detect biological activity. They can guide the fractionation of root exudates into chemically different constituents. Yet activity in either assay is a property of the test organism and the compound together. A fraction that induces many germinations is not necessarily the fraction most important for a plant's own bud regulation. Modern interpretation retains the value of these assays while separating them from direct measurements of hormone perception in the plant.

3. Chemical identity and carotenoid origin

Carotenoids are isoprenoid compounds with extended carbon frameworks. Some have familiar functions in light capture and protection of photosynthetic membranes, but carotenoid metabolism also supplies precursors to signals. Cleavage of a large carotenoid can generate smaller molecules called apocarotenoids. Strigolactone biosynthesis uses this strategy. It is distinct from the tryptophan-derived pathway to indole-3-acetic acid and from the sterol-derived pathway to brassinosteroids. Abscisic acid also has a carotenoid origin in higher plants, but it is generated through different substrates and enzymes. Shared ancestry in carotenoid metabolism does not imply shared receptors or equivalent physiological functions.

Canonical strigolactones possess a three-ring region conventionally termed the ABC region and a butenolide D ring linked through an enol ether. The configuration of the rings and substituents differs among individual compounds. Strigol and orobanchol are representative names for naturally occurring structures. Noncanonical strigolactones lack the complete classical ABC arrangement but retain characteristic structural elements associated with activity, including the D-ring linkage in many members. Structural classes should not be confused with categories of plant responses. Different ligands and stereoisomers may be perceived unequally by plant receptors, parasitic seed receptors or fungal perception systems. A chemical name alone therefore does not predict quantitative potency in every assay.

The geometry of a molecule can be biologically important even when its elemental formula is unchanged. Enzymes and receptors have three-dimensional binding environments; reversing the spatial configuration of a ligand may change its fit or reaction. GR24 is a widely used synthetic strigolactone analogue. Experimental descriptions should identify which stereoisomer or mixture was applied, because some GR24 stereoisomers also influence KAI2-dependent pathways. A response to a mixture cannot automatically be assigned exclusively to the endogenous strigolactone receptor D14. For a basic rescue test, an analogue may be a practical substitute for a natural ligand; for a claim about the identity of the endogenous signal, more discriminating evidence is required.

A useful way to think about the D ring is as one of the recurring features that enable the family to interact with recognition proteins. A receptor, however, encounters the entire molecule and not merely a label identifying one ring. Substitutions elsewhere in the molecule can alter its binding, reaction rate, stability and access to tissues. The natural stereochemical arrangement of the D ring is an important determinant of activity, but it is not sufficient to declare that any molecule possessing such an arrangement will be equally effective. Examination problems may describe two compounds that stimulate parasitic seeds similarly but differ in their effects on shoot branching. This observation is compatible with the known diversity of recognition mechanisms and ligand properties.

The structural distinction between canonical and noncanonical forms also helps resolve an apparent paradox. A plant may release abundant canonical SLs while relying heavily on a different derivative for aspects of endogenous shoot signaling. Conversely, a mutant specifically impaired in the formation of one canonical compound might still produce a carlactone-derived metabolite that maintains much of its shoot response. The absence of one molecule from an extract does not show that all SL-related signaling has ceased. Chemical analysis should ask which compounds have been measured and how those compounds relate to the physiological endpoint of interest.

Physicochemical properties introduce further experimental complications. If a ligand is poorly stable in the collection solution, the amount remaining when it is measured underestimates the amount originally released. Adsorption to experimental surfaces and differences in extraction efficiency can likewise alter the apparent concentration. The chemical species applied to a plant may also undergo conversion before it reaches the receptor. These possibilities help explain why biochemical binding measurements and whole-plant response curves may differ. The response of an intact organism incorporates absorption, distribution, transformation and receptor sensitivity, whereas a purified receptor assay isolates a narrower part of the system.

Unlike a freely diffusible gas such as ethylene, strigolactones are organic solutes whose distribution is affected by their chemistry and the cell barriers that separate tissues. Unlike peptide hormones, their conserved biosynthetic steps do not involve translation of a preprohormone that is cleaved into a mature signaling peptide. Nevertheless, the enzymes, transport proteins and receptors that produce and interpret them are encoded by genes. Genetic mutations therefore influence the abundance and action of a nonprotein hormone through changes in proteins required for its physiology. This distinction helps prevent a common confusion when naming biosynthetic loci: MAX4 is a gene encoding an enzyme, not a gene encoding the strigolactone molecule itself.

4. Biosynthesis: D27, CCD7, CCD8 and MAX1

The first shared steps occur in plastids, where carotenoid substrates are accessible. All-trans-beta-carotene has a geometry that is not the preferred substrate for the next cleavage reaction in this pathway. DWARF27, abbreviated D27, catalyzes the formation of 9-cis-beta-carotene by isomerization. An isomerase changes the spatial arrangement of a molecule rather than simply removing a carbon fragment. This matters because substrate geometry determines the subsequent enzymatic transformations. A loss of D27 therefore decreases the supply of an appropriate substrate to CCD7 even if a plant retains substantial pools of other carotenoids.

CAROTENOID CLEAVAGE DIOXYGENASE 7, or CCD7, cleaves 9-cis-beta-carotene to produce a shorter 9-cis-beta-apo-10-prime-carotenal and beta-ionone. The aldehyde is then used by CCD8, another carotenoid cleavage dioxygenase. CCD8 performs oxidative transformations and a rearrangement that generate carlactone. Carlactone is a central biosynthetic intermediate, not a synonym for every mature strigolactone. Alder and colleagues established this sequence in 2012 by connecting the products of the three enzymes through biochemical experiments. The order of enzymes is important in reasoning about metabolic rescue: a supply of carlactone bypasses a block before carlactone formation, but it does not automatically bypass a block in a necessary downstream conversion.

The gene nomenclature reflects the independent discovery of the pathway in several model species. Arabidopsis MAX3 encodes CCD7 and MAX4 encodes CCD8. In rice the corresponding familiar genes include D17 and D10. MAX1, unlike MAX3 or MAX4, encodes a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase of the CYP711A family. After carlactone is formed, downstream oxidation generates carlactonoic acid and related derivatives; additional enzymes and different MAX1 homologues contribute to the varied compounds observed among plants. In an examination, it is useful to recognize the conserved trunk of the pathway and avoid assuming that every plant converts carlactone into the same final molecule by an identical sequence.

A pathway diagram is a representation of chemical dependencies rather than a literal statement that all reactions occur in one cellular compartment. The early carotenoid conversions occur in plastids, whereas the subsequent metabolism of carlactone involves enzymes outside the plastid, including membrane-associated P450 enzymes. Substrate access and the abundance of each enzyme can influence the flux through the pathway. A reduction in the pool of one product may result from reduced synthesis, increased further conversion or enhanced release, so a single concentration measurement is not a direct measurement of the reaction rate. Comparing precursors, products and tissue-specific phenotypes offers stronger evidence about the blocked step.

At the level of carbon metabolism, plastidial isoprenoid production supplies the precursors from which carotenoids are assembled. The full metabolic pathway from basic carbon intermediates to carotenoids is not required to interpret the branch-regulating SL pathway, but a conceptual connection is useful. When a carotenoid is transformed into an apocarotenoid, the original molecule is not simply transferred intact to a receptor. Chemical processing generates a different molecular scaffold. The 40-carbon beta-carotene substrate and the smaller carlactone product are thus members of a precursor-product relationship rather than two interchangeable signaling forms.

The distinction between D27 and the two cleavage dioxygenases is especially useful in questions about reaction types. D27 catalyzes isomerization of a double-bond configuration. CCD7 catalyzes cleavage of a carotenoid, forming an aldehyde and a smaller product. CCD8 then carries out a more extensive transformation involving oxygen incorporation and rearrangement. Although both CCD enzymes belong to a related enzyme family, they do not perform redundant steps. Loss of CCD8 cannot ordinarily be compensated by simply increasing CCD7, because accumulating the substrate of a missing reaction does not recreate its catalyst. The appropriate intermediate or downstream product must be supplied, or the missing enzymatic activity restored.

P450 monooxygenases introduce oxygen into organic substrates using a reaction system that ordinarily depends on molecular oxygen and reducing equivalents. The role of MAX1 is therefore not equivalent to receptor-mediated detection of an already active hormone. MAX1 acts on carlactone-related chemistry, and its loss may change the profile of products rather than merely eliminate all related compounds. In Arabidopsis, oxidation of carlactone to carlactonoic acid is a characterized MAX1-dependent step. Further metabolism produces derivatives such as methyl carlactonoate and other compounds. In rice, distinct MAX1 homologues contribute to the production of canonical strigolactones. The details of the terminal pathway should be interpreted in the context of the species rather than imposed as a universal sequence.

An illustrative metabolic block can be used to reason about pathway order. Consider mutant X, unable to make 9-cis-beta-carotene through the ordinary D27 reaction, and mutant Y, unable to carry out CCD8-dependent conversion of the apo-carotenal. If an experimentally supplied apo-carotenal is able to reach the relevant cellular compartment and is stable, mutant X may regain downstream production, whereas mutant Y still lacks the enzyme that processes that intermediate. If carlactone is supplied instead, both blocks have theoretically been bypassed. A negative outcome is not definitive because the supplied compound may not cross a membrane or be available in the appropriate form. It is essential to state the assumptions behind predictions about metabolic complementation.

The abundance of a precursor can sometimes increase in a mutant, but precursor accumulation is not an inevitable consequence of a blocked reaction. Alternative metabolic routes may consume the precursor, its synthesis may be downregulated, or it may be unstable. If a student is told that a mutant has very little carlactone and that supplying carlactone restores branching, the data support an upstream biosynthetic defect provided downstream perception is intact. They do not uniquely identify D27, CCD7 or CCD8. Distinguishing those possibilities requires additional information such as enzyme activity, precursor abundance or the genotype itself.

One must also distinguish necessity and sufficiency. Expression of a functional CCD8 enzyme in a suitable biosynthetic mutant may restore a missing reaction when the upstream substrate and downstream machinery are present. It does not follow that expression of CCD8 alone in any arbitrary cell is sufficient to produce the entire family of SL compounds. Other pathway components, substrates and intracellular environments matter. The lesson applies to plant metabolic engineering: identifying a key enzyme is an important step, but the product flux is constrained by the complete network of reactions.

5. Homeostasis, chemical turnover and transport

The concentration of a signaling molecule represents a balance of inputs and outputs. Cells may synthesize it locally, receive it from other cells, modify it chemically, release it into their surroundings or degrade it. Strigolactone pathways do not have a universal storage system equivalent to the well-known glucose ester of ABA. Carlactone-derived compounds can be converted into other metabolites, and the biological activity of the derivatives differs. Hydrolysis and chemical instability, including cleavage of susceptible linkages, also affect ligand availability. D14 itself has hydrolytic activity, linking perception and ligand turnover, although the detailed chemical sequence that produces the signaling-competent receptor state is not fully settled. It is important not to invent a single storage conjugate or catabolic route for the entire family.

Root exudation means transfer of chemicals from a root into its surrounding medium. It should be distinguished from translocation between plant organs. The petunia ABC transporter PDR1 is an experimentally characterized example of a strigolactone exporter. Mutations altering this transporter can change the quantity of strigolactone recovered outside roots and affect colonization and branching phenotypes. Kretzschmar and colleagues reported this connection in 2012. Because PDR1 is a membrane transporter, its position in the cell and tissue helps determine the direction in which it moves substrates. The existence of this transporter is evidence that selective movement contributes to strigolactone physiology; it is not evidence that every species uses PDR1 in every tissue.

Experiments using grafts can reveal whether one plant segment supplies a mobile influence to another segment. They cannot, without chemical identification, distinguish the movement of a mature strigolactone from that of a biosynthetic precursor or another downstream mobile signal. Local synthesis in shoots can also contribute to control of branching. The precise forms and routes of long-distance movement are not universally established. An examination claim that all strigolactones travel exclusively in xylem from roots to shoots would therefore be too strong. Equally, the uncertainty concerning the mobile molecule does not negate the evidence that systemic regulation occurs.

To appreciate how sampling influences the conclusion, imagine that two plants synthesize equal quantities of a compound in the root. One exports a larger fraction into the rhizosphere. Its internal concentration could be lower while the amount recovered from the external medium is higher. If only the root extract were measured, this plant could mistakenly be classified as biosynthetically deficient. If only the external solution were measured, it might be mistaken for a plant with enhanced synthesis. Measurements of both compartments, ideally normalized to root biomass and collection time, help distinguish a transport defect from a biosynthetic defect.

A minimal balance equation helps organize the processes controlling hormone availability. Let M be the amount of an identified SL compound in a defined root compartment, and let v_syn, v_in, v_out, v_conv and v_deg denote rates of synthesis, import, export, conversion and degradation respectively. Then the instantaneous change in amount is dM/dt = v_syn + v_in − v_out − v_conv − v_deg. Each rate must have dimensions of amount per unit time, for example nanograms per hour. This balance is not a detailed kinetic model, but it makes clear why a change in measured hormone abundance cannot be attributed automatically to an altered synthesis rate.

In a compartment whose amount remains constant, the total input equals the total output, but that does not mean every flux is zero. For instance, assume a root synthesizes 12 nanograms per hour of one compound, imports none and exports 7 nanograms per hour. If its pool is steady, conversion and degradation together account for the remaining 5 nanograms per hour. A transporter mutation that initially lowers export to 3 nanograms per hour while all other rates remain unchanged would increase the internal amount at 4 nanograms per hour. Over time, however, feedback and altered chemical turnover may change the other rates. A simple immediate prediction is not necessarily a steady-state prediction after acclimation.

Concentration adds another variable. If the compartment has volume V, the average concentration is M/V. A growing root can increase its volume while synthesizing the hormone, and the concentration could remain constant despite an increase in the total amount. Conversely, a reduction in tissue water content can increase the measured concentration without increasing hormone synthesis. When comparing organs under different nutrient or stress conditions, the normalization method should reflect the intended inference. Fresh mass, dry mass and tissue volume describe different aspects of the biological sample.

Feedback connects hormone signaling to its own production. If downstream signaling is defective, the plant may increase expression of some biosynthetic genes, because the physiological signal that ordinarily contributes to feedback regulation is absent. Elevated SL content or biosynthetic gene expression in a signaling mutant is therefore compatible with a weak hormone response. It should not automatically be interpreted as a gain of signaling. The same logic appears in other hormone systems: the amount of ligand and the competence of the response pathway are different axes of regulation.

Transport is often inferred from movement of a labeled molecule, but a label does not necessarily identify the chemical form that reaches a distant organ. A labeled precursor can be transformed into a labeled product. A labeled hormone can be degraded and its labeled fragments reused. If an experiment merely detects radioactivity or an isotopic signal in a shoot after application to a root, movement of label has been shown, not necessarily movement of the intact molecule. Chemical separation and identification of the labeled species are important when testing the identity of a transported form. This distinction is particularly pertinent for a family whose biosynthetic intermediates and mature derivatives are chemically related.

6. D14–MAX2–SMXL: signaling by removal of a repressor

A hormone becomes informative to a target cell only when a suitable receptor and downstream response mechanism are available. DWARF14, or D14, belongs to the alpha/beta-hydrolase fold family. It can interact with and hydrolyze strigolactone ligands. Hormone-dependent changes in D14 allow it to participate in a signaling complex involving the F-box protein MAX2, known as D3 in rice. The F-box protein acts as a substrate-recognition component of an SCF-type ubiquitin ligase, whose other components provide the scaffold and connect the substrate to the ubiquitin-transfer machinery. This links small-molecule perception to regulation of protein abundance.

The immediate regulatory target in rice is a repressor named D53. In Arabidopsis, related proteins SMXL6, SMXL7 and SMXL8 are important downstream repressors in the canonical shoot branching response. When productive D14-dependent recognition occurs, these substrates can be ubiquitinated and removed by the 26S proteasome. Their disappearance changes the ability of the cell to express and execute downstream responses. This is a signaling strategy based on removing a brake. Because SL suppresses bud outgrowth, removing a repressor of SL signaling can ultimately inhibit growth of a branch. The signs of these two levels are different: D53 represses a signaling pathway, whereas the output of that pathway can repress a developmental process.

A simple loss-of-function prediction follows. In a biosynthetic mutant, little effective ligand is available, so the repressor may remain abundant. Applying a suitable active ligand can permit the intact receptor and ligase to remove the repressor. In a receptor-deficient mutant, the same application may be ineffective because the necessary perception step is absent. In a MAX2/D3-deficient mutant, ligand recognition alone is insufficient for the normal ubiquitin-dependent response. A stabilized, degradation-resistant D53 protein can also maintain repression even when upstream signaling proteins are present. Two plants with similar branching phenotypes can therefore be defective at entirely different positions in the pathway.

The pathway is supported by molecular genetic experiments in rice reported in 2013 by Jiang and colleagues and Zhou and colleagues. These studies connected hormone-dependent turnover of D53 with D14 and the D3-containing SCF complex. Protein abundance measurements provide an intermediate phenotype between ligand application and the eventual number of tillers. If a repressor disappears rapidly after treatment, this is consistent with a proximal signaling event; changes in shoot length or tiller number require more time and reflect many subsequent processes. Demonstrating that repressor loss depends on the appropriate receptor and ligase is more informative than observing only the final appearance of a plant.

MAX2 also participates in signaling mediated by the related KAI2 receptor. In Arabidopsis, that pathway preferentially involves other SMAX1-family targets. It is consequently unsafe to interpret every MAX2-dependent phenotype as evidence of a specific D14-mediated strigolactone effect. Comparing receptor-specific mutants, defined ligands and the appropriate downstream substrate clarifies the pathway. At NET level, the key principle is specificity emerging from the combination of receptor, substrate and cellular context, even when some signaling machinery is shared.

The ubiquitin-proteasome mechanism can be divided into recognition and execution. In the first part, the ligand-dependent receptor state and the F-box substrate recognition apparatus make a particular regulatory protein available for ubiquitination. In the second, the proteasome recognizes an appropriate degradation signal and breaks down the protein. The receptor is not itself the proteasome, and MAX2 is not a protease that directly cleaves SMXL. The distinction explains why inhibiting the proteasome can prevent a hormone-induced reduction in substrate abundance even when ligand binding is normal.

The term repressor can refer to several molecular roles. In this pathway D53 and related SMXL proteins suppress particular transcriptional or regulatory outputs, often through interactions with other regulatory factors. Their removal is important because downstream processes are then less constrained. One should not infer that D53 binds directly to every promoter of every gene influenced by strigolactones. Some transcriptional effects are indirect, and the eventual branching phenotype depends on the interactions among multiple tissues. The canonical core pathway can be established without specifying a single direct transcriptional target that explains all phenotypes.

In a hypothetical protein-abundance experiment, compare D53 abundance following analogue treatment in a normal rice plant and a d3 mutant. A rapid decline in the normal plant but persistence in the mutant supports the involvement of D3 in stimulated turnover. If messenger RNA levels remain unchanged during this interval, a change in transcription of the D53 gene is less likely to explain the rapid protein decline. A protein synthesis inhibitor, interpreted carefully, may help distinguish ongoing synthesis from turnover, but an effect of a general inhibitor cannot by itself identify the responsible degradation machinery. A combination of substrate ubiquitination, protein abundance, genetic dependence and appropriate controls gives stronger evidence.

A degradation-resistant D53 allele is instructive because it demonstrates that a mutation can make a pathway insensitive without preventing ligand synthesis or receptor binding. The mutant protein remains functionally repressive even under conditions that normally favor its removal. If the allele acts dominantly in a particular genotype, the presence of some normal D53 does not necessarily restore the response because the persistent mutant protein can still exert repression. This differs from a simple recessive loss of a biosynthetic enzyme, where a functional allele may provide enough activity to restore the pathway. The distinction between a stabilized negative regulator and loss of a positive regulator is common in plant signaling genetics, including the comparison with GA-dependent DELLA turnover.

The signaling pathways of gibberellin, auxin, jasmonate and strigolactone share the broad principle that removal of a regulatory protein can cause major changes in physiology. Their molecular components, however, are not interchangeable. Gibberellin uses GID1-dependent recognition of DELLA repressors; the nuclear auxin pathway uses TIR1/AFB-associated recognition of Aux/IAA proteins; jasmonate perception involves COI1 and JAZ. Strigolactone uses D14, MAX2 and selected SMXL proteins. A question describing an F-box protein alone may not distinguish these pathways. The identity of the ligand, receptor and substrate provides the discriminating evidence.

It is also important to distinguish the fact that D14 has a hydrolytic catalytic site from the question of which receptor-associated chemical state triggers productive signaling. Recognition, ligand cleavage and interactions with MAX2 and the repressor are connected events. However, the exact role of transient hydrolysis products and the sequence of molecular states have been investigated with different models. For the intended examination level, the robust conclusion is that D14-dependent perception leads to MAX2-dependent repressor turnover. An answer that asserts one disputed intermediate is the universal active hormone without qualification goes beyond that established core.

7. Shoot branching and interaction with auxin and cytokinin

An axillary bud receives signals from its own tissues, from the subtending leaf and from the main stem. Auxin produced by young shoot tissues moves basipetally in the stem. It can influence the biosynthesis of strigolactones and cytokinin and thereby change the environment in which a bud grows. Cytokinin frequently promotes bud activation, while strigolactone tends to restrain outgrowth in many model systems. These influences are not a simple arithmetic subtraction between two measured concentrations. The tissue's developmental competence, local nutrient and carbohydrate supply, receptor activity and timing determine whether a particular bud initiates and sustains growth.

One useful framework involves the TCP transcription factor BRANCHED1 (BRC1) in Arabidopsis and related regulators such as TB1 in grasses. Their activity is associated with restraint of axillary bud development. Hormone and environmental signals can converge on these local transcriptional regulators. However, BRC1 is not the sole mechanism through which strigolactones control branches. If a mutation removes BRC1, other processes can still influence branch competition and growth. Distinguishing a major regulatory node from the entire pathway is essential when interpreting double mutants or partial rescue.

Another framework involves auxin transport canalization. A bud that begins to grow must establish an effective auxin-export pathway into the stem to sustain its growth. The existing main stem transport network and competition among growing shoots affect the ease with which such an export pathway is established. Strigolactone can influence the abundance or dynamics of PIN auxin efflux carriers in appropriate tissues. This can change the transport environment experienced by buds. The canalization framework and bud-local regulation are not necessarily mutually exclusive. They emphasize different levels of organization: the capacity for hormone flux between organs and the state of a particular bud.

Decapitation removes a major auxin-producing region, but it also changes the distribution of carbohydrates and other signals. An early burst of bud growth after removal of the apex need not prove that an auxin concentration change was the first initiating event in that tissue. Sugar availability can change rapidly and contribute to activation. Exogenous auxin supplied to the cut stem may restore some components of inhibition, depending on the plant and experimental conditions. A complete explanation therefore separates the original intervention, the changing signals and the measured endpoint. Final branch number integrates initiation, sustained extension and possible later arrest of several buds.

Resource competition is not merely an alternative explanation to hormone signaling; it is an important part of the system in which the signals operate. A growing bud requires sugars as respiratory substrate and structural carbon. It also requires nitrogen for protein and nucleic acid synthesis and phosphate for many cellular processes. Even when a hormone signal favors outgrowth, a severe lack of resources may prevent substantial extension. Conversely, a bud supplied with abundant resources can respond differently to the same hormone concentration. The term developmental competence encompasses the ability of a particular tissue to respond under its current physiological conditions.

The auxin export problem can be illustrated by considering two buds that connect to a common stem. If the first establishes sustained growth and an effective transport connection, it can modify conditions experienced by the second. One cannot simply treat each bud as an isolated cell receiving an identical chemical dose. The transport system links the buds and the main stem. This provides a possible explanation for coordinated changes in branching that are difficult to explain with a model in which every bud independently responds to the amount of SL in its own tissue. It also helps explain why interventions that change polar auxin transport can have effects dependent on the number and arrangement of competing shoots.

Cytokinin provides a complementary example of a regulatory influence on bud activity. In many experiments, local cytokinin application favors the activation of restrained buds. Auxin in the stem can influence cytokinin production and metabolism, so removing the shoot apex may relieve part of this regulation. SL signaling may oppose some cytokinin-promoted outcomes. However, an observation that cytokinin application stimulates a bud in an SL-treated plant does not establish that cytokinin physically destroys SL or prevents D14 binding. Physiological antagonism means that the outputs oppose one another under the conditions tested; molecular antagonism requires evidence of a defined interaction at a particular step.

A useful experimental design separates local hormone treatment from treatment of the main stem. If a compound applied directly to a bud alters its growth, that observation is compatible with a bud-local action, although movement out of the treated region remains possible. If application to a distant stem region also influences growth, systemic or transport-mediated effects become plausible. Tracer experiments and tissue-specific genetic complementation can narrow the alternatives. In a tissue-specific complementation experiment, restoration of a functional gene in one tissue tests whether that tissue can provide enough activity to support the measured phenotype. It does not necessarily prove that the gene is normally expressed only in that tissue.

8. Root architecture and phosphate status

Root architecture includes the primary root, lateral roots, root hairs and the spatial distribution of their growth. Changes in one component do not imply equivalent changes in the others. A plant may increase its root hair length without producing a longer primary root. It may alter lateral root density merely because the length of the primary axis changes. These distinctions matter for quantitative analysis. Counting laterals per plant is not the same as counting laterals per centimetre of primary root, and initiation of a lateral root primordium is not the same as its eventual emergence from the parent root.

Strigolactones influence root developmental responses in a manner that depends on species, nutrient supply, developmental stage, concentration and interactions with auxin and other signals. Under phosphate limitation, enhanced synthesis and release of certain strigolactones have been observed in diverse plants. This is consistent with a role in obtaining phosphate through beneficial fungal associations and in modifying the allocation of growth between shoots and roots. It is not a universal prediction that every root exposed to phosphate deficiency becomes longer or develops more lateral roots. Phosphate starvation can itself strongly alter root development through signaling mechanisms that need not be exclusively strigolactone-dependent.

Phosphate is needed for phosphorylated intermediates, nucleic acids, phospholipids and energy metabolism. Its availability to roots depends not only on its total amount in soil but also on diffusion, binding to soil particles and the chemical environment. A plant may consequently benefit from increasing the soil volume explored by fungal hyphae. By increasing exudation of compatible cues when phosphate is scarce, a root can modify its external chemical environment. The plant is not directly transporting phosphate by releasing a strigolactone; instead it can influence the probability and development of interactions that later facilitate nutrient acquisition.

In a controlled comparison of plants grown with adequate or limited phosphate, root mass and developmental stage should be considered. An increase in exudate concentration can reflect a change in root size, collection volume or degradation in the medium. A stronger test quantifies compounds released per unit root mass per unit time. To infer the function of the SL pathway in a phosphate response, an appropriate design also includes biosynthetic or perception mutants and, when relevant, ligand rescue. If all genotypes exhibit a root response to low phosphate, the response is not necessarily independent of SL; compensation or parallel pathways could obscure a contribution. The data establish only the difference measured under the particular conditions.

Quantifying root architecture requires attention to both development and geometry. Suppose a nutrient treatment produces twelve emerged lateral roots on a primary axis twelve centimetres long, whereas a second treatment produces ten lateral roots on a five-centimetre axis. The first plant has more lateral roots in absolute terms, but its density is one per centimetre, compared with two per centimetre in the second plant. Which plant has increased lateral-root formation? The answer depends on what is meant by formation. Density is a useful descriptive measure, but the two plants might differ in root elongation, developmental age and the fraction of initiated primordia that have emerged. Counting primordia and emerged roots at comparable developmental positions can provide complementary information.

Root hairs are extensions of individual epidermal cells and are not equivalent to lateral roots, which are multicellular organs initiated from internal tissues. Root hair elongation increases contact between the root surface and its environment, whereas lateral branching changes the distribution of root axes. A hormone that influences both traits need not use the same downstream mechanism in the two cases. When a question provides an observation about root hairs, one should not extrapolate directly to the number of lateral roots. Under phosphate limitation the contribution of these traits depends on how the root system explores its surroundings and which nutrients are limiting.

Phosphate deficiency and general starvation should also be distinguished. To investigate a phosphate-specific response, nutrient solutions can be constructed that differ in phosphate while maintaining other essential nutrients as consistently as possible. Differences in ionic composition and pH can influence root physiology independently, so appropriate controls matter. A plant exhibiting increased SL release during phosphate limitation is not necessarily responding only to phosphate concentration outside the root. Internal phosphorus status, allocation of resources and systemic signaling may contribute. Measurements of tissue phosphorus content and growth help interpret the relationship between the external treatment and the plant's physiological state.

Consider an experiment in which low phosphate increases the amount of a strigolactone measured in wild-type root exudate, but does not substantially change the primary root length. This result is not contradictory. Exudation can be regulated independently of a particular morphological endpoint. If a different species shows a change in root length without a detectable rise in the same exudate compound, the species may produce other derivatives or use parallel responses. The purpose of comparative physiology is to identify the shared principles without ignoring the diversity of outcomes. In this case the shared principle is integration of nutrient conditions with hormone production, distribution and responses, not a fixed numerical change in every root trait.

9. Arbuscular mycorrhiza and communication in soil

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi associate with the roots of many land plants. A fungal hypha is a filamentous structure that grows through soil and may extend beyond the region efficiently reached by a root's own absorbing surfaces. Before colonization, root exudates can promote fungal metabolic activity and branching. These responses can improve exploratory contact with a root. Inside a compatible root, fungal structures known as arbuscules develop in cortical cells, providing a large interface for exchange across specialized membranes. Plants can receive mineral nutrients, notably phosphate, and supply carbon resources to their fungal partners. The plant-fungus interaction thus involves both benefits and metabolic costs.

A strigolactone signal released into soil is not equivalent to the hormone response inside the plant. In the plant, D14 and its downstream machinery are well characterized; the perception mechanism responsible for the fungal response is a distinct problem. A fungal hyphal branching assay can establish a biological response to a compound but does not identify the fungal receptor or show that the fungus uses the plant D14–MAX2 pathway. The same chemical can influence different organisms through different recognition mechanisms.

An important limitation comes from the use of Arabidopsis as a model. It has been extremely valuable for research on the genetics of plant branching and strigolactone signaling, but it is generally nonmycorrhizal. A researcher wishing to study arbuscular colonization should use an appropriate host such as petunia, rice or a suitable legume rather than assuming that every flowering plant can establish this association. Conversely, the lack of a normal arbuscular mycorrhizal relationship in Arabidopsis does not imply that it lacks an endogenous strigolactone pathway. Endogenous architecture regulation and symbiotic competence are distinct properties.

Mycorrhiza must also be distinguished from rhizobial nitrogen fixation. Both are examples of intimate plant interactions with soil organisms and may involve overlapping signaling machinery in compatible plants, but their partners and primary nutrient transactions differ. Arbuscular mycorrhiza involves fungi; rhizobial nodulation involves bacteria capable of biological nitrogen fixation in compatible hosts. Strigolactone-induced hyphal branching is not nitrogenase activity and does not transform atmospheric nitrogen directly into ammonium. This distinction connects the hormone chapter to nitrogen metabolism without confusing the mechanisms.

The arbuscule is particularly important for understanding why a fungal branching bioassay and a nutrient acquisition experiment answer different questions. Before contact, a germinating fungal spore has limited information about the location of a host. Changes in hyphal branching can increase opportunities for contact. After entry into a compatible root, the association must form a physiological exchange interface. At an arbuscule the fungal membrane and the plant-derived peri-arbuscular membrane are distinct structures. Exchange requires transport across the relevant membranes, not unrestricted mixing of fungal and plant cytoplasm. The ability of a chemical to stimulate the earlier exploration stage cannot substitute for the later exchange machinery.

The advantage of extending fungal hyphae through soil is partly spatial. Roots can deplete readily accessible phosphate from the immediate surroundings, leaving a region in which diffusion may limit replenishment. Fine hyphae can explore regions beyond this depletion zone. This is especially useful for nutrients whose mobility in soil is limited. The fungal partner also requires carbon and lipids supplied by the plant, so the net benefit of the association depends on the nutrient gained relative to the carbon and other resources allocated to it. When phosphate is abundant, the value of maintaining the interaction can change; regulation of symbiotic signaling is consequently part of resource economics.

A convincing nutrient-transfer experiment distinguishes total plant phosphorus from phosphorus acquired by a specified route. An increase in biomass in the presence of fungi could result from several interactions, including altered root growth. If a compartment accessible to fungal hyphae but not to roots contains a detectable phosphorus tracer, subsequent detection of that tracer in a colonized plant supports transfer through the fungal pathway. Appropriate barriers and uncolonized controls are necessary to exclude simple leakage or access by roots. This conceptual experiment demonstrates the difference between observing an association and demonstrating a physiological transfer function.

In comparisons of colonized and uncolonized plants, the initial nutrient conditions and fungal inoculum matter. A plant with high phosphate nutrition might show less colonization than a phosphate-limited plant, but that difference alone cannot establish that SL is the only regulator. The plant can control several stages of symbiosis and the fungus may also respond directly to its nutritional environment. Combining exudate chemistry, hyphal response assays, colonization measurements and tissue phosphorus measurements produces a more complete account. The chain of inference begins with nutrient status, proceeds through signal production and perception and ends with a measured physiological benefit or cost.

10. Parasitic plants and agricultural tradeoffs

The same extracellular cue that promotes a beneficial fungal response can be exploited by root parasites such as Striga or broomrapes. A conditioned parasitic seed can perceive suitable germination stimulants released by nearby plants. If a compatible host root is sufficiently close, the young parasite may establish a feeding connection called a haustorium. The hormone in the host is not itself a nutrient supplied to the parasite. Its presence is information about the local environment. Germination and successful attachment remain separate events, and the stimulant profiles preferred by different parasites can vary.

Reducing release of particular germination stimulants might decrease parasite recruitment in a specific crop and soil context, but altering the entire strigolactone pathway can also alter shoot architecture and beneficial fungal interactions. This is a classic example of pleiotropy: a genetic change in one pathway can affect several traits. A desirable intervention would ideally distinguish the chemical species and spatial compartments contributing to different outcomes rather than treating all strigolactones as a single interchangeable substance. In experimental breeding, branch number alone is not sufficient to predict seed yield, and reduced parasite germination alone is not sufficient to establish improved crop performance.

A conceptual strategy known as suicidal germination attempts to induce germination of parasitic seeds in the absence of a suitable host, causing the emerging parasites to exhaust their reserves. The biological principle is useful for examinations because it separates a germination trigger from the resources needed for subsequent development. Its effectiveness depends on parasite species, seed conditioning, timing, environmental persistence and absence of a host, so it is not a universal field remedy. The central physiological lesson is that chemical communication can have different consequences depending on which organism detects the cue and whether that organism can complete its life cycle.

The life history of a root parasite explains why the germination response is valuable to it. Small seeds have limited stored reserves. Germination in an unsuitable place can produce a seedling unable to reach a nourishing host. A response to plant-derived molecules can bias germination toward environments in which a root may be accessible. The cue is imperfect because nonhost plants can also release active compounds. Consequently, a germination stimulant is not identical to a guarantee of host compatibility. The parasite still needs to accomplish recognition, attachment and the development of a functional connection after germination.

In many parasitic Orobanchaceae, members of the expanded KAI2-related receptor family contribute to the perception of host-derived strigolactones. These are often called HTL or divergent KAI2 proteins in discussions of particular parasitic species. This is not the same physiological system as D14-mediated branching control in the host plant. Both systems illustrate how related protein folds can be recruited for different biological purposes. The distinction is valuable in a comparative examination: if a mutation in a parasite receptor prevents a seed from germinating in response to a host exudate, it need not affect the host plant's ability to suppress its own branches.

A dose-response experiment with parasitic seeds can reveal how strongly a particular population responds to different compounds. Suppose two natural SLs produce the same maximal fraction of germinating seeds when supplied at sufficiently high concentrations, but one achieves half of its own maximum at a much lower concentration. That compound is more potent under the conditions of the assay, although the maximal efficacy is similar. Differences in potency might reflect receptor properties, ligand stability or availability to the seed. Neither observation alone tells us which compound contributes most strongly to germination in soil. The actual concentrations released by a host, diffusion and degradation in soil are also important.

The proportion of germinating seeds is bounded between zero and one. If a set of fifty viable and appropriately conditioned seeds contains thirty germinated seeds after treatment, its observed germination fraction is 0.60. If forty seeds out of fifty in a different set are viable but only twenty actually germinate, the fraction calculated relative to all fifty is 0.40 and the fraction relative to the forty viable seeds is 0.50. Either denominator may serve a particular purpose, but a comparison between treatments requires the same definition. Seed viability should therefore be measured independently where relevant. A lower observed fraction could arise from seed mortality or inadequate conditioning rather than weaker perception of the tested stimulant.

A crop may produce several SL derivatives that differ in activity towards a parasite. Selective changes in the profile of exudates are consequently conceptually distinct from a complete disruption of the conserved early biosynthetic pathway. Blocking the early pathway can affect both endogenous signals and many externally released compounds. Altering one late enzymatic branch might change the composition of exudates while retaining other metabolites. Whether the latter intervention has a smaller cost to growth or symbiosis must be determined empirically. This principle is an application of metabolic branching: a perturbation upstream of a common intermediate may influence many products, whereas a perturbation downstream of that intermediate can have a narrower effect.

Agricultural evaluation should also consider the environmental context in which a trait is advantageous. More tillers may increase the number of reproductive units if resources permit their development, but excess nonproductive tillers can divert resources from reproductive structures. Increased fungal recruitment may be beneficial in phosphate-poor soil yet provide less advantage in a heavily fertilized environment. Reduced stimulation of a parasitic seed population may be valuable in infested soil but irrelevant where the parasite is absent. These tradeoffs explain why a desired phenotype cannot be predicted from a simple direction of change in SL abundance. A breeding program would evaluate plant performance, nutrient status and the relevant interacting organisms together.

11. Experimental reasoning and quantitative examples

A well-designed mutant rescue experiment asks whether a defect lies upstream of a supplied active signal. Compare wild type, a CCD8-deficient mutant and a D14-deficient mutant, each treated with solvent alone or with a suitable SL analogue. If excessive branching of the CCD8 mutant is reduced by the analogue while the D14 mutant remains branched, this supports the placement of CCD8 in synthesis and D14 in perception. Solvent controls ensure that the observed effect is not attributable to the application medium. An adequate design also compares developmental stage, treatment duration and growth conditions. Lack of rescue can be caused by poor delivery or insufficient dose, so the response of an appropriate positive control is indispensable.

Consider illustrative branch counts of 3 and 3 for wild type with solvent and hormone respectively, 11 and 4 for CCD8-deficient plants, and 12 and 12 for D14-deficient plants. A useful measure is the fraction of mutant excess branching removed relative to untreated wild type. For the CCD8 mutant this equals (11 − 4)/(11 − 3) = 7/8 = 0.875, or 87.5 percent. This measures recovery relative to the excess rather than relative to the mutant's total branches. It is not a statistical test. Individual plant measurements, biological replication and an analysis of uncertainty would be needed before drawing an experimental conclusion. The values here are constructed for teaching and are not observations from a published experiment.

A second useful calculation concerns exudation. Suppose the collection solution contains 18 nanograms of an identified strigolactone after six hours from roots weighing 0.30 grams dry mass. The mean mass-normalized release rate is 18/(6 × 0.30) = 10 nanograms per gram dry root mass per hour. If another treatment gives 24 nanograms over six hours from 0.60 grams of roots, its normalized rate is 24/(6 × 0.60) = 6.67 nanograms per gram per hour. Although the second sample contains more total compound, the first releases more per unit root mass per time. This example assumes complete recovery and negligible degradation over collection. It illustrates why raw exudate amount is not sufficient to distinguish changes in root size from changes in release physiology.

Protein turnover can also be examined quantitatively. In a simplified model, suppose degradation follows first-order kinetics and synthesis is temporarily negligible. The abundance P at time t is P0 exp(−kt), where k has units of inverse time. The half-life is ln(2)/k. If a repressor falls to one-quarter of its starting abundance in 20 minutes, this corresponds to two half-lives of ten minutes each, giving k approximately 0.0693 per minute. A slower loss in a receptor mutant under the same conditions would support a requirement for the receptor in stimulated turnover. Real cells continue synthesizing proteins, and measured abundance depends on both synthesis and degradation; the simple model cannot be applied blindly to every immunoblot time course.

Finally, a root exudate bioassay must distinguish a chemical response from a change in nutrient content. A fungal preparation can be exposed to solvent control, an authentic standard, wild-type root exudate and exudate from an SL biosynthetic mutant, with matched collection and exposure conditions. If the mutant exudate shows lower hyphal branching activity and supplementation restores activity, this supports a missing stimulant. The assay alone does not exclude the existence of additional inhibitors or stimulants, so chemical fractionation and direct measurement strengthen the inference. This reasoning generalizes across plant hormone experiments: a genetic perturbation, chemical identification and functional rescue are complementary forms of evidence.

Genetic epistasis provides a powerful way of examining the order of a signaling pathway. Two mutations are combined in a single organism, and the resulting phenotype is compared with the two single mutants. In a simplified linear pathway in which D14 and MAX2 promote removal of SMXL repressors, loss of those repressors can bypass the need for upstream removal machinery. If a double or multiple mutant lacks the relevant repressor activity and regains branch suppression despite a defect in perception, the result supports the interpretation that the repressors function downstream. This prediction assumes that the removed proteins are the relevant redundant substrates and that the measured phenotype is governed largely by that pathway. If several SMXL proteins substitute for one another, loss of only one may produce little effect.

Epistasis is not defined merely by observing that two mutant plants look alike. If a biosynthetic mutant and a receptor mutant both produce many branches, this resemblance tells us that normal synthesis and normal perception both contribute to the observed phenotype. A double mutant may also have many branches, but that observation alone cannot determine their order because disruption at either of two essential steps can produce the same final failure. A downstream bypass or a proximal molecular readout, such as the abundance of a target repressor, provides the additional information. The logic is particularly relevant to NET Part C problems that combine hormone application with double-mutant phenotypes.

A reciprocal graft design uses four combinations of wild-type and mutant scions and stocks. The wild-type scion on wild-type stock provides a grafted baseline, and the mutant scion on mutant stock controls the effect of grafting on the mutant phenotype. The two reciprocal combinations then test whether changing the genotype of one part alters the behavior of the other. If the wild-type stock reduces branching of a biosynthetic mutant scion, the result supports movement of some complementing influence. If it fails to suppress branching of a receptor-deficient scion, that is compatible with a requirement for local perception. The results should be interpreted for the species, tissues and developmental stage actually tested because shoots may synthesize signaling compounds independently and rootstock effects can involve multiple signals.

In a chemical complementation experiment it is helpful to distinguish an upstream precursor from an active ligand. Let a plant lacking CCD8 receive carlactone, and let a plant lacking a required downstream MAX1-dependent conversion receive the same compound. If the first plant responds while the second fails to respond under identical delivery conditions, this supports the placement of the required MAX1 activity after carlactone. If both respond, several explanations are possible: the downstream mutation may not abolish all relevant conversion, carlactone may have activity through another route, or the applied compound may be transformed by other enzymes. Chemical rescue is a test of functional bypass, not a direct biochemical demonstration of the exact reaction.

Measurements of messenger RNA and protein often answer different questions. A transcript assay showing that SMXL messenger RNA increases after hormone treatment does not contradict a simultaneous reduction in the abundance of the corresponding protein. The transcript can be synthesized as part of feedback regulation while the existing protein is being degraded. To infer protein turnover, one should measure the relevant protein directly, with appropriate normalization. To infer a transcriptional response, one measures RNA or a suitable promoter reporter. The two data types complement one another but cannot be substituted without justification.

A simple time-course can illustrate this distinction. Suppose a normalized repressor protein signal is 1.00 before treatment, 0.50 after ten minutes, 0.25 after twenty minutes and 0.25 after forty minutes. The first three points are compatible with a half-life of ten minutes under a first-order approximation. The later plateau indicates that the same declining exponential is no longer an adequate description. It may reflect ongoing protein synthesis, an insensitive subpopulation, altered degradation or the lower detection limit of the assay. A student should not extrapolate the initial rate to predict indefinitely falling abundance when the measured data have already deviated from that trend. Models are evaluated against observations rather than imposed on them.

Dose-response data provide another useful link between molecular competence and physiology. Define a normalized bud inhibition index as the reduction in bud extension relative to a matched solvent control, divided by the control extension. If untreated buds elongate by eight millimetres over a defined interval and treated buds elongate by two millimetres, the inhibition index is (8 − 2)/8 = 0.75. A receptor-deficient genotype might have a much lower index at the same applied dose. However, if the control bud hardly grows, the index becomes unstable because the denominator approaches zero. This is why both absolute and normalized measures should be examined, particularly when treatments alter the baseline growth of the plant.

When several concentrations are tested, the curve may rise, level off or change direction at high doses. A plateau is compatible with saturation of some part of the response system but does not prove that receptor occupancy alone determines the phenotype. Tissue uptake, conversion and maximum attainable growth inhibition can also limit the response. At high doses, nonspecific effects on growth may complicate interpretation. For an ideal one-site equilibrium binding model, fractional occupancy is L/(Kd + L), where L denotes free ligand concentration and Kd denotes a dissociation constant with the same concentration units. At L equal to Kd, occupancy is one-half. This equation is useful as a reference model, but D14-dependent signaling involves ligand chemistry and downstream protein turnover, so a whole-plant half-maximal response must not be identified automatically with the molecular Kd.

Replication is an important part of evidence quality. Measuring ten buds on a single plant gives useful information about within-plant variation, but the buds share a common transport and nutritional system. They are not necessarily ten independent biological replicates. Comparing several independently grown plants permits inference about variation among plants exposed to the treatment. If nutrient treatments are delivered to different trays, tray-level conditions may also create dependencies. A sound interpretation of a graph depends on the experimental unit and the meaning of the error bars. A large difference between means does not establish a statistically reliable treatment effect unless the variation and replication support that conclusion.

Factorial experiments are particularly appropriate because hormone responses depend on context. Consider wild type and an SL-deficient genotype, each grown under adequate and limiting phosphate. If branch number declines under phosphate limitation in wild type but declines less in the mutant, there is evidence that the genotype modifies the phosphate response. A simple difference of differences illustrates the concept. Suppose the adequate-to-low phosphate decrease is four branches in wild type and one branch in the mutant. The contrast between responses is three branches. This is not by itself a significance test, but it identifies the interaction that a statistical model should assess. A main effect of phosphate alone would not reveal whether its effect depends on the SL pathway.

12. Connecting biochemical evidence, genetic evidence and physiological outcomes

A researcher can make a series of mechanistic claims of increasing specificity. The first may be that an unknown metabolite present in a root exudate affects a particular bioassay. The second may be that a particular chemical compound accounts for much of the observed activity. A third may be that the compound is produced by a specified enzyme pathway. A fourth may be that a defined receptor and downstream machinery mediate its response. Finally, the researcher may argue that the response contributes to the performance of a plant in a given environment. Each claim requires a different kind of evidence. The strength of the strigolactone story lies in the convergence of these levels rather than in any single experiment.

Bioassay-guided fractionation provides a way of linking a mixture to a candidate chemical. An exudate can contain nutrients, metabolites, signaling molecules and possible inhibitory compounds. If the unfractionated mixture stimulates a fungus, the active constituents are initially unknown. Separating the mixture into fractions and testing each one can help locate activity. Identification of a candidate molecule and comparison with an authentic standard then strengthen the assignment. Even if one purified compound reproduces an effect, a mixture may contain additional active substances. The concentration and combination of components can influence the overall response. It is therefore important to distinguish identification of a sufficient stimulant from an exhaustive characterization of every biologically relevant exudate component.

The Akiyama study is a clear example of the difference between a precontact response and symbiotic outcome. Germinating fungal spores were exposed to defined compounds, and hyphal branching was assessed. The ability of several natural SLs and GR24 to promote this response indicated that the activity was not limited to an unidentified component of a crude root extract. It also established that the fungus could respond before intimate exchange structures formed within a root. Nevertheless, this assay cannot establish that each compound is equally important in soil, that every fungus responds identically or that the observed hyphal architecture guarantees successful nutrient transfer. The experimental question determines the appropriate boundary of the conclusion.

The branching studies of Gomez-Roldan and Umehara provide an example of convergence between chemical analysis and genetics. If mutant plants are deficient in particular SL-related metabolites and display excessive branching, the correlation is compatible with a hormone role but is not yet decisive. A mutation could have several biochemical consequences. The recovery of branch inhibition after supplying a suitable compound makes the causal interpretation stronger. The comparison with signaling mutants is important because a general toxic effect on growth would not necessarily depend on the pathway expected to perceive the hormone. A carefully interpreted rescue therefore links a chemical intervention with the proposed genetic mechanism.

The biosynthetic work of Alder and colleagues addressed the chemical order rather than the whole-plant phenotype alone. The early enzymes produce a sequence of chemically related intermediates. By establishing the transformations catalyzed by D27, CCD7 and CCD8, the work explained why mutations in different loci could cause similar physiological deficiencies. A useful mental distinction is between a genetic map and a reaction map. The genetic map relates genes to inheritance and phenotype; the reaction map relates substrates, catalysts and products. An enzyme gene appears in both maps, but the evidence needed to place it in each is different. A whole-plant phenotype cannot by itself establish the stereochemistry or structure of the intermediate formed by the enzyme.

The D53 studies moved the explanation to the level of protein turnover. A receptor model that simply said the ligand changes gene expression was incomplete because it did not identify the molecular events connecting perception to transcription. Demonstrating hormone-dependent interaction among signaling components and the degradation of a regulatory substrate supplied a mechanistic bridge. Mutants that impair D14 or D3-dependent signaling disrupt the expected reduction of the substrate. A stabilized form of the repressor reveals an additional way to uncouple hormone availability from physiological response. These observations provide a causal explanation of why some highly branched plants fail to respond to applied SL.

The PDR1 study added a spatial dimension. A transporter defect can influence the quantity of compound reaching the external environment without being a defect in the catalytic pathway that constructs the compound. Comparing the material recovered from roots and from their surrounding medium can therefore help distinguish transport from synthesis. The observed mycorrhizal and branching effects of the petunia transporter establish that spatial distribution matters physiologically. The observation must still be kept species-specific: it does not prove an identical transporter distribution in rice or Arabidopsis, or specify one universal chemical form for movement between distant tissues.

An examination may supply a new experimental observation that appears to challenge a familiar textbook conclusion. Rather than discarding the established mechanism immediately, first identify the level of the claim being tested. For example, the absence of branch suppression after an SL treatment might reflect impaired delivery, perception or downstream signaling rather than the absence of a normal biosynthetic role. The failure of one root exudate to stimulate a fungal isolate might reflect its chemical profile, concentration or incompatibility rather than the absence of the whole class of compounds. Such reasoning is not an excuse to ignore contradictory data. It is a way to design discriminating experiments that separate multiple plausible explanations.

The auxin-transport framework has a useful experimental refinement. Shinohara, Taylor and Leyser reported that SL treatment can rapidly reduce the abundance of PIN1 at the plasma membrane and that a model incorporating this effect can reproduce differing branching outcomes depending on the existing transport state. The important lesson is that the effect of a change in an efflux carrier is not determined by the carrier in isolation. In a stem already supporting several active auxin-export sources, changes in transport capacity can modify competition among those sources. A student should not conclude that SL necessarily suppresses every branch under every arrangement of sources and sinks. In most introductory comparisons the branch-suppressing role is a useful baseline, but a carefully designed experiment may reveal context-dependent effects.

The receptor specificity of synthetic analogues also provides a clear example of how chemical and genetic evidence complement each other. Scaffidi and colleagues compared the responses of Arabidopsis to natural SLs and stereoisomers of GR24. Receptor-specific mutant comparisons helped distinguish responses mediated by D14 from those associated with KAI2. This was more informative than treating all analogue-induced changes as evidence for a single hormone receptor. For an examination involving a racemic preparation, it is reasonable to ask whether different components of that preparation may produce different physiological outputs. Subsequent work has identified additional context-dependent overlap between the two downstream signaling systems, including D14-associated SMAX1 turnover under particular conditions. The conventional distinction between SMXL6/7/8 and SMAX1/SMXL2 is a useful starting framework, but it is not an assertion that substrate specificity can never overlap. The correct level of detail is to identify the canonical pathways and recognize the experimental conditions under which exceptions are reported.

13. Integrating plant hormone mechanisms and physiological tradeoffs

The physiological effects of a hormone are easiest to understand when one compares them at the level of the process regulated rather than merely classifying them as promoters or inhibitors. Auxin is frequently associated with elongation and tropism but can inhibit growth in sensitive tissues at particular concentrations. Gibberellin often promotes stem extension, whereas strigolactones can restrict bud outgrowth while contributing to other aspects of plant growth. Cytokinin is associated with division and meristem activity yet can influence root and shoot development in different ways. These examples show that an adjective such as growth-promoting is an incomplete description of any major plant hormone.

At the molecular level, strigolactone signaling is similar in broad logic to several previously studied pathways. A ligand makes the removal of a regulatory protein possible. Yet the sign of the final physiological response varies with the regulatory function of that protein and the tissue-specific targets of the pathway. Gibberellin-dependent DELLA removal commonly releases constraints on stem elongation. In the branching response, SL-dependent SMXL removal contributes to restraint of lateral outgrowth. Therefore, removal of a repressor cannot be translated into a universal prediction of increased organ growth. The sign of the output has to be traced through all levels of regulation.

Brassinosteroid signaling offers a contrasting mechanism. The BRI1 receptor kinase system changes a phosphorylation network that affects transcriptional regulators such as BES1 and BZR1. Although protein stability can also change in that network, the characteristic core is not the D14–MAX2 recognition of SMXL repressors. Cytokinin perception provides another contrast through histidine kinase receptors and phosphorelay components. These comparisons help students classify unfamiliar signaling pathways using the experimentally identified receptor and immediate molecular event rather than relying on the chemical name or visible phenotype of the hormone.

The connection with ABA is particularly instructive because both ABA and strigolactones can arise from carotenoid metabolism. Their biosynthetic routes diverge: ABA formation involves different carotenoid cleavage chemistry, including NCED-dependent reactions in higher plants. Their canonical signaling mechanisms also differ. ABA perception involves PYR/PYL-family proteins, inhibition of PP2C phosphatases and activation of downstream SnRK2 kinases. Strigolactone signaling instead uses D14, MAX2 and specific SMXL repressors. In drought or nutrient limitation a plant may alter several hormone systems at once, but a shared environmental trigger does not make those systems molecularly identical.

A plant must balance carbon supply and demand. Initially a developing shoot is a sink that consumes carbohydrate; after its leaves expand, it may become an important source. Root growth and fungal symbiosis also consume carbon, yet can improve the availability of mineral nutrients required for subsequent photosynthesis and biosynthesis. The relation between carbon investment and nutrient acquisition can change over time. A reduction in early branching may conserve resources, but prolonged restriction may limit later photosynthetic capacity or reproductive sites. Hormonal regulation is valuable because it allows these allocations to respond to the plant's situation rather than forcing a fixed architecture irrespective of conditions.

One can illustrate the allocation problem with a deliberately simplified mass balance. Suppose a plant has 100 arbitrary carbon units available for new growth over a defined period. It invests 55 in shoot development, 35 in roots and 10 in supporting a fungal association. A second plant invests 75 in shoots, 20 in roots and 5 in the association. The second plant allocates more carbon above ground, but this information alone does not show that it will gain more biomass later. If the first plant obtains more phosphate through its below-ground investment, its future photosynthetic capacity could change. Real plants also have respiratory losses, stored reserves and changing assimilation rates. The example is not a mechanistic SL model; it illustrates why current allocation and future return must be distinguished.

The spatial separation between signal production and physiological response is central to a whole-plant view. Some signals are produced near a particular site of action, others can move between tissues, and extracellular cues may act on another organism. Strigolactones participate in all three kinds of context, but the chemical identity and transport route need to be established for each. A root exudate component may be abundant outside the plant and scarce within a shoot bud. Conversely, a locally produced derivative could regulate the bud while contributing little to exudate activity. The same biosynthetic precursor family can therefore serve different spatial roles without a single molecule being responsible for every effect.

This distinction between internal and external signaling also suggests an important approach to interpreting agricultural mutants. A mutation affecting the conserved early pathway may reduce production of many compounds and change several phenotypes together. A mutation affecting a downstream metabolic branch may change only selected compounds. A mutation in a host perception component may alter endogenous responses while preserving the production of some external cues, although feedback can modify their abundance. A transport mutation may redistribute compounds across compartments. The predicted effects of these four perturbations are different even if they initially produce similar changes in branching. A precise mechanistic classification helps decide what additional measurements are needed.

The next major hormone system to be studied is abscisic acid, whose roles in seed dormancy and water conservation illustrate another way of coordinating growth with environmental conditions. The transition from strigolactones to ABA is not a biological sequence in which one hormone must act before the other. It is a conceptual progression from architecture and below-ground communication to control of developmental timing and water relations. A student who understands the distinction between signal production, ligand availability, tissue competence and regulatory output can transfer that reasoning to ABA without assuming that its receptor mechanism is the same.

The distinction between branch suppression and branch dormancy deserves particular attention. Dormancy can describe a bud that remains viable yet fails to elongate substantially under the current conditions. A restrained bud may still undergo molecular changes, and its growth can resume if signals or resources change. A dead bud, by contrast, cannot resume ordinary development. Thus a phenotype in which a branch fails to appear does not demonstrate cell death. Histological examination and a subsequent release treatment can distinguish a living restrained bud from loss of the meristem. This is important when comparing a physiological hormone treatment with nonspecific damage caused by excessive concentrations of an experimental compound.

A further distinction is between a developmental delay and a permanent change in the number of branches. Suppose two genotypes both have four branches at maturity, but one begins elongation of lateral buds considerably later. A single count at maturity conceals the earlier difference. Conversely, an early difference in branch number could disappear if later growth compensates for it. Time-resolved observations of individual buds make it possible to distinguish the timing of activation, the rate of elongation and the persistence of growth. These measures correspond to different biological questions and need not change in parallel.

Consider a hypothetical split-root experiment, in which the root system of one plant is divided between two compartments that can receive different nutrient supplies. One compartment has adequate phosphate while the other is phosphate-limited. If SL release increases only from the phosphate-limited half, the pattern is compatible with local regulation. If release changes from both halves, systemic information about whole-plant nutritional state may contribute. Yet local and systemic regulation can coexist: a larger response on the deficient side accompanied by a smaller response on the other side is consistent with both. The two halves share a shoot and vascular connection, so they are not biologically independent plants. A sound design includes plants with both halves supplied adequately and plants with both halves limited, and evaluates root mass in each compartment.

This split-root approach also helps interpret a difference in mycorrhizal colonization. Suppose one side receives fungal inoculum and the other does not, with comparable phosphate availability. If phosphate supplied through the colonized side changes hormone production on the noncolonized side, the observation suggests a whole-plant response to altered nutritional conditions. It does not establish the chemical identity of the systemic signal. In particular, the movement of phosphate or a change in systemic nutrient-status signaling may account for effects otherwise attributed to SL movement. To investigate transport of a specific hormone derivative, chemical identity and movement must be tested separately from the physiological response.

The validity of a chemical measurement depends on the distinction between detection and quantification. An analytical signal above a detection threshold suggests the compound is present; accurate quantification additionally requires calibration, control of recovery and awareness of interferences. If one derivative is present below the detection limit in a mutant exudate, the correct statement is that it was not detected under the specified conditions. Its absence cannot automatically be established. Likewise, comparing peak areas of different chemical species does not necessarily compare their molar abundances because their analytical responses may differ. Standard curves for identified compounds help convert signals into amounts.

These distinctions are useful even without specialist laboratory knowledge. In a typical examination, the measurements have already been made and the student must choose the strongest warranted conclusion. If a low-phosphate treatment doubles a validated mass-normalized release rate and a biosynthetic mutation prevents the increase, the data support involvement of the affected biosynthetic pathway in that response. If the same experiment provides no information about receptor function or shoot phenotype, it cannot establish that the increase causes suppression of branching. Adding a perception mutant and measuring the downstream phenotype would address that separate question.

The strongest interpretation usually respects the direction of causality. Changing phosphate availability can alter a plant's signal production. Signal perception can modify physiology. Modified physiology can then change the plant's nutrient acquisition and its future internal phosphate status. This creates a feedback loop, not a one-way sequence in which phosphate and hormones are forever independent variables. Short-term perturbations help separate initiating events from later feedback, while long-term measurements reveal the integrated outcome. The two types of evidence are complementary. A short-term assay that demonstrates a molecular response does not substitute for a long-term assessment of resource allocation, and an eventual increase in nutrient acquisition does not identify the molecular event that initiated it.

14. Misconceptions and examination traps

SL always inhibits all growth
SL restrains many axillary buds but can influence other organs differently. Calling it an inhibitor without specifying the endpoint obscures tissue-dependent responses; check which trait was measured.
Every highly branched mutant lacks SL
A receptor or degradation defect can leave ligand abundant. Similar shoot phenotypes encourage this confusion; use chemical rescue and protein measurements.
CCD7 and CCD8 are interchangeable
They carry out different sequential conversions. Their similar enzyme-family names can mislead; trace substrate and product.
MAX1 is the F-box protein
MAX1 is a CYP711A enzyme; MAX2 is the F-box component. Similar MAX names are not a functional classification.
D14 is D53
D14 is the receptor/hydrolase; D53 is a regulatory substrate in rice. Follow signal recognition versus downstream turnover.
Removing a repressor always increases growth
Repressor removal activates the signaling output; that output can restrain bud outgrowth. Track signs at each regulatory level.
GR24 is one universally specific natural ligand
It is synthetic, and stereoisomers may engage different receptor pathways. Inspect ligand identity and mutant controls.
All SLs use a single long-distance vascular route
Mobile forms and routes vary and remain incompletely mapped. Graft rescue establishes mobility of an influence, not an exclusive xylem route.
Greater exudate amount means greater biosynthesis
Export, root size and degradation also matter. Normalize by mass and time and measure the internal pool.
Low phosphate always causes longer roots
Root traits depend on species and environment. Read the actual endpoint rather than generalizing a nutrient response.
More lateral roots means higher density
Density includes the primary-root length denominator. Compare counts and lengths before drawing conclusions.
Fungal branching proves colonization
It is a precontact response. Later entry and nutrient exchange require additional evidence.
AM symbiosis is rhizobial nitrogen fixation
AM fungi facilitate mineral acquisition, especially phosphate; rhizobial nodules involve bacteria and nitrogenase. Distinguish partners and nutrient transactions.
A germination stimulant is a nutrient for Striga
It is a cue. Subsequent host attachment supplies resources; germination is not successful parasitism.
MAX2 mutant responses are uniquely D14-specific
MAX2 is shared with KAI2-related signaling. Receptor-specific genetics and defined ligands are required.
Higher transcript means higher protein
Synthesis and degradation can move protein abundance in the opposite direction. Look for protein-level data and sampling time.

A. D27 changes carotenoid geometry. CCD8 forms carlactone; SCF machinery ubiquitinates the repressor; MAX1 performs downstream oxidation.

  2. MAX1
  3. D14
  4. MAX4
Answer and explanation

D. MAX4 encodes CCD8, MAX3 encodes CCD7, MAX1 encodes a P450 enzyme and MAX2 an F-box component.

  2. Carlactone
  3. Orobanchol in every species
  4. 9-cis beta-carotene
Answer and explanation

B. CCD8 converts the apo-carotenal intermediate to carlactone. Downstream enzymes generate diverse derivatives; ABA is made through a different route.

5. MAX1 belongs to which enzyme family?

  1. Histidine kinase
  2. CYP711A cytochrome P450
  3. ABC transporter
  4. Alpha/beta hydrolase receptor
Answer and explanation

B. MAX1 catalyzes downstream oxidative modification. The other classes correspond to distinct signaling or transport functions.

6. Which protein is the principal canonical SL receptor in shoot branching?

  1. PYL
  2. BRI1
  3. GID1
  4. D14
Answer and explanation

D. D14 is an alpha/beta-hydrolase family receptor. PYL, BRI1 and GID1 perceive ABA, BR and GA respectively.

  1. D3
  2. D10
  3. D53
  4. D27
Answer and explanation

A. D3 is the F-box component of SCF signaling; D10 encodes CCD8, D53 is a repressor and D27 is an isomerase.

  2. Synthesis of D53 as the only response
  3. Ubiquitination and degradation of a relevant D53/SMXL repressor
  4. Activation of nitrogenase
Answer and explanation

C. The SCF ligase targets the substrate for proteasomal degradation. It does not directly perform proteolysis, and nitrogenase is unrelated.

  2. DELLA alone
  3. JAZ alone
  4. SMXL6, SMXL7 and SMXL8
Answer and explanation

D. These SMXL proteins mediate key canonical SL responses. Aux/IAA, DELLA and JAZ belong to auxin, GA and JA pathways.

  2. A receptor for cytokinin
  3. A carotenoid isomerase
  4. A fungal phosphate transporter
Answer and explanation

A. PDR1 is an ABC transporter involved in SL distribution and exudation in petunia; it is neither a receptor nor a biosynthetic enzyme.

  1. A translated peptide chain
  2. A purine base
  3. An ABC ring region linked to a butenolide D ring
  4. A steroid nucleus identical to brassinolide
Answer and explanation

C. Canonical SL structures contain an ABC region and linked D ring. Noncanonical members lack the complete ABC arrangement.

  1. Always abolish all SL synthesis
  2. Increase production or exudation of certain SLs
  3. Fix atmospheric phosphate
  4. Convert SL directly into phosphate
Answer and explanation

B. In diverse plants, phosphate limitation enhances some SL-related signals. The response is species dependent and does not create phosphate.

  1. Increased hyphal branching
  2. Formation of bacterial nodules
  3. Nitrogenase induction as the primary response
  4. Plant seed dormancy

A. Hyphal branching was demonstrated in fungal bioassays. It is not nitrogen fixation or proof of completed colonization.

  1. A shoot meristem
  2. A parasite's seed coat
  3. A carotenoid cleavage site
  4. An interface for plant-fungus exchange

D. Arbuscules in colonized root cortical cells provide an exchange interface; nutrient transport requires additional machinery.

  2. Arabidopsis thaliana
  3. Petunia
  4. A compatible legume

  2. All SLs move only in phloem
  3. The precise mobile forms and routes are not universal
  4. SLs never move between cells
Answer and explanation

C. Local synthesis, tissue transport and external release must be distinguished. There is no universal exclusive vascular route established for the family.

  2. DNA replication versus translation only
  3. Bud initiation versus sustained bud outgrowth
  4. Pollen germination versus fertilization only
Answer and explanation

C. Increased visible branches may arise from activation of pre-existing buds, not increased meristem initiation.

  1. A natural compound universally identical to strigol

  4. A proteasomal enzyme

  3. Defective receptor-dependent turnover
  4. Loss of a fungal arbuscule alone

  1. 10 min
  2. 5 min
  3. 20 min
  4. 40 min
Answer and explanation

A. A decline to one quarter represents two half-lives. Twenty divided by two gives ten minutes.

  3. A 60, B 40 ng/g/h
  4. A 12, B 8 ng/g/h

D. A = 30/(0.5×5) = 12; B = 40/(1×5) = 8. Total recovered mass alone reverses the normalized ranking.

  1. Only mature strigol moves in xylem
  2. The scion must lack D14
  3. A mobile complementing influence can be supplied by the stock
  4. Grafting identifies the chemical structure

C. Rescue supports movement of some functional influence, not a specific molecule or route. A perception defect would generally not be bypassed this way.

  4. MAX2 is a fungal receptor

A. MAX2 participates in D14 and KAI2 pathways; receptor specificity can distinguish responses despite shared machinery.

  2. High hormone necessarily produces maximal response

  4. Branches are formed only by fungi

16. Revision and mastery

Strigolactones connect carotenoid metabolism with shoot architecture, root nutrient responses and chemical communication. The conserved biosynthetic trunk produces carlactone, while MAX1-dependent and other reactions create diverse products. D14-dependent perception and SCF–MAX2 action remove specific SMXL/D53 repressors. Distinguish ligand abundance from sensitivity, root export from translocation, fungal exploration from completed nutrient exchange, and parasite germination from host attachment.
