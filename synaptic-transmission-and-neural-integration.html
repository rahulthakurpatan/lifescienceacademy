From impulse conduction to neural communication

The action potential solves the problem of transmitting information along an excitable membrane over a long distance. It does not by itself explain how information passes from one cell to another. Neurons are distinct cells whose membranes normally prevent ions from moving directly between their cytoplasms. A second mechanism must therefore connect the electrical activity of one neuron to the electrical or biochemical activity of its target. A specialised junction that performs this function is called a synapse. The cell delivering a signal is presynaptic and the receiving cell is postsynaptic. These descriptions refer to one connection rather than to permanent identities: a neuron can be postsynaptic to hundreds of partners while providing presynaptic output to many others.

A nervous system must do more than reproduce every incoming impulse. Consider a spinal motor neuron receiving activity from sensory afferents, inhibitory interneurons and descending pathways. Its output must depend on the relative timing and strength of these signals, not simply on the presence of one impulse. The individual synaptic events usually generate graded changes in voltage or conductance. Their combined effects influence whether the neuron produces its own action potential. This conversion of many inputs into an output pattern is neural integration. A single incoming impulse can therefore fail to evoke a spike, contribute to a later spike or reduce the probability of another input evoking one. The apparently simple distinction between a conducting axon and an integrating neuron is central to understanding reflexes, brain circuits and motor regulation.

A chemical synapse converts a presynaptic electrical event into secretion of a messenger and then into a response determined by the receiving cell. An electrical synapse instead permits direct movement of current between the two cells through intercellular channels. Both use electrochemical gradients established and maintained by living cells; neither provides a source of free electrical energy. Electrical coupling can be useful where cells need coordinated timing. Chemical transmission allows amplification, selective inhibition, modulation and flexible control of response duration. The two modes can coexist between a pair of neurons, and the existence of one does not exclude the other.

The terms excitatory and inhibitory describe effects on the generation of output, not moral qualities of a signal or simply the direction in which the voltage changes. Excitation raises the likelihood of firing, whereas inhibition lowers it under specified conditions. Some synapses regulate transmitter secretion without directly changing the voltage of the final target. Others alter the response to subsequent stimuli. Before examining detailed nervous-system anatomy, it is necessary to establish these cellular rules, because anatomical connectivity alone cannot tell us the functional effect of a pathway.

How experiments separated chemical and electrical transmission

An important historical problem was whether communication between neurons and their targets depended on electrical currents alone or whether nerve activity could liberate a chemical messenger. In 1921, the physiological evidence for chemical signalling was strengthened by Otto Loewi's experiments with isolated frog hearts. Stimulation of the vagus nerve slowed the first heart. Fluid from the environment of that heart was transferred to a second preparation, which also slowed. A direct electrical connection between the hearts was not required. The transferable inhibitory substance, subsequently associated with acetylcholine, therefore offered evidence for chemical mediation. The experiment does not establish that every synapse is chemical; it demonstrates that some neural effects can be conveyed by a released chemical. A useful control is to transfer fluid from the first preparation without stimulating the nerve, because the receiving heart might otherwise respond to the composition of the fluid rather than to a substance produced by stimulation.

The delay between presynaptic excitation and the beginning of a postsynaptic response was another observation requiring explanation. Axon conduction takes time, but a measurable delay at the junction persists even after accounting for the time required for the signal to travel along the axon. Chemical transmission includes channel activation, secretion, movement of transmitter and receptor activation. None of these steps requires that the presynaptic and postsynaptic action potentials have identical amplitudes. The presence of a delay was consistent with chemical transmission, but delay measurements alone could not determine the nature of the intervening events. The availability of intracellular recording and preparations such as the squid giant synapse enabled investigators to relate the timing of terminal depolarisation, calcium entry and postsynaptic current much more directly.

A useful conceptual voltage-clamp experiment holds the presynaptic ending at different command voltages. At voltages insufficient to open calcium channels, transmitter secretion is small. Moderate depolarisation increases calcium channel activation, but near the calcium equilibrium potential the driving force for calcium entry becomes small. Thus secretion is not necessarily an increasing function of the amplitude of depolarisation over every possible voltage range. An investigator may observe a nonmonotonic relation between presynaptic voltage and evoked postsynaptic response. This observation supports a role for ionic calcium current rather than voltage alone as the immediate signal for release. It is important to distinguish this general experimental logic from any particular published voltage dependence or numerical value, since channels and release sites differ across preparations.

One can strengthen a causal test by manipulating the intermediate variable. If extracellular calcium is reduced and the presynaptic depolarisation is held constant, a reduced evoked postsynaptic response indicates that voltage alone is insufficient. If calcium is restored and the response returns, the reversible nature of the effect helps exclude irreversible damage. A separate experiment that applies transmitter directly to the postsynaptic membrane tests whether the target cell can still respond. A normal response to applied transmitter in the presence of severely reduced evoked transmission places the primary defect upstream of receptor activation. It does not identify a particular protein without further evidence.

Electrical transmission was established by complementary physiological approaches. In paired recordings, voltage changes in one cell may induce a nearly immediate graded change in the partner without the sequence of calcium-dependent secretion and chemical receptor activation. The presence of a response to a hyperpolarising current step is especially informative because a negative change in voltage can travel through a passive junction. Unlike a conventional action-potential-driven chemical synapse, this transfer does not require a depolarisation sufficient to release transmitter. A combined electrical and chemical synapse may produce a rapid component followed by a delayed receptor-mediated component. Separating these components by their sensitivity to distinct perturbations is more convincing than classifying the synapse by latency alone.

Chemical signalling and electrical coupling should therefore be understood as mechanisms established through converging observations, not as categories memorised from diagrams. Electrophysiology provides timing and quantitative evidence; structural methods identify potential routes of communication; pharmacological and genetic perturbations test molecular requirements. Each approach has limitations. A structural contact is not necessarily functional, and a pharmacological effect is not automatically specific. Students should look for a consistent chain of predictions rather than treating one measurement as a complete explanation.

Electrical transmission and coupling

At an electrical synapse, channels in adjacent plasma membranes form a continuous aqueous route between cells. In vertebrates, six connexin proteins generally assemble into a hemichannel, also called a connexon, and compatible hemichannels from the two cells dock to create an intercellular gap-junction channel. Many channels can form a plaque. In invertebrates, innexins perform analogous intercellular channel functions. Individual channels pass ions and, depending on their composition, some small molecules. They do not create wholesale continuity of cytoplasmic proteins or organelles.

The physical basis of coupling is a potential difference between the cells. If cell A becomes more positive than cell B, the junctional current tends to redistribute charge and depolarise B while loading A. Under an approximately ohmic description, current from A toward B equals junctional conductance multiplied by the difference between their membrane potentials. The conductance is measured in siemens and the voltage difference in volts. A conductance of 2 nS and a potential difference of 15 mV give 30 pA. This is current between the cells, not a prediction that both cells immediately reach the same voltage. Each cell has its own leak conductance and membrane capacitance, so current is also lost through the membrane or used to change stored charge.

In a simple steady-state two-cell experiment, if A is held at a fixed voltage increment and B has membrane conductance gB to its resting reference voltage, the voltage increment in B is the increment in A multiplied by gj/(gj + gB). This coupling coefficient is dimensionless. Suppose gj is 2 nS and gB is 8 nS. A sustained 20 mV change in A produces a 4 mV change in B under the stated assumptions. Doubling the junctional conductance does not double the response: the coefficient becomes 4/12, giving about 6.67 mV. The result illustrates diminishing returns as the two cells approach electrical equality. The coefficient can differ with the direction of testing even if the junction itself is symmetric, because the two cells can have different membrane conductances. Rectification of the channel itself is a separate possibility.

Electrical synapses have little intrinsic transmission delay compared with chemical synapses, although charging the receiving membrane still requires time. Slowly changing signals are often transmitted more effectively than the very brief peak of an action potential because membrane capacitance filters rapid voltage fluctuations. An action potential in A can therefore appear in B as a small spikelet rather than as an identical full-amplitude action potential. The spikelet triggers a regenerative spike only when the receiving neuron becomes sufficiently excitable. Hyperpolarisation can also pass through the junction. This explains why electrical coupling is not equivalent to universal excitation.

Paired intracellular recording offers a direct conceptual test. Inject current into one neuron, record its voltage and simultaneously record the second neuron. An immediate graded response in the second cell that follows both depolarising and hyperpolarising injections supports electrical coupling. Reverse the direction of injection to test reciprocity. Appropriate comparisons include uncoupled cell pairs, conditions that alter junctional conductance and examination of membrane resistance. A rapid response alone does not conclusively establish direct coupling: electrical stimulation can recruit other pathways or produce recording artefacts. The paired voltage response, anatomical evidence and experimental perturbation together establish the mechanism more convincingly.

Electrical synapses can help synchronise groups of interneurons or coordinate rapid escape circuits in some animals. Their properties are regulatable, and they are not merely rigid wires. Coupling can distribute subthreshold activity among neurons and can sometimes reduce the size of a local perturbation by providing a current sink. The consequences depend on the network configuration and relative input strengths.

Architecture and causal sequence of chemical transmission

A typical central chemical synapse contains a presynaptic terminal with clusters of small synaptic vesicles, an active zone that specialises in exocytosis, an extracellular cleft, and a postsynaptic membrane carrying receptor proteins. A postsynaptic density is often visible at excitatory contacts under electron microscopy. Axodendritic, axosomatic and axoaxonic synapses differ in the cellular compartment contacted by the presynaptic ending. Their arrangement helps determine whether a signal influences a dendritic branch, spike initiation, or the release of transmitter from another terminal. Shape alone is not a definitive indicator of transmitter identity or function.

When a presynaptic action potential invades the terminal, membrane depolarisation increases the opening probability of voltage-gated calcium channels. Because free cytosolic calcium is maintained at a concentration much lower than that outside the cell, calcium enters through open channels. The electrical driving force at ordinary terminal voltages also favours entry. A brief local calcium increase near channels and release sites provides the signal that triggers vesicle fusion. The calcium rise is highly local and transient; an average concentration measured over the entire terminal need not represent the concentration experienced by the release sensor.

A vesicle must first reach a release site and become competent for rapid fusion. Docking describes its positioning at the membrane, while priming describes molecular preparation for fusion. Vesicular synaptobrevin, also called VAMP, associates with the plasma membrane proteins syntaxin and SNAP-25 to form a SNARE complex. The assembly of this complex brings the two membranes together. Synaptotagmin acts as an important calcium sensor for fast synchronous secretion at many synapses. Its interaction with membrane lipids and the fusion machinery upon calcium binding promotes rapid opening of a fusion pore. Other proteins regulate readiness and the timing of release; not every vesicle is equally available.

The released transmitter molecules move by diffusion across the extracellular cleft and bind receptors on the target. At an ionotropic synapse, binding alters an ion channel's opening probability. At a metabotropic synapse, receptor activation changes intracellular signalling and may indirectly influence ion channels or other cellular processes. The transmitter is not itself the electrical current that flows through the postsynaptic membrane. Its function is to change receptor activity; the current is carried by ions crossing the membrane through channels. The consequences depend on receptor type, ionic gradients, membrane voltage and other simultaneous conductances.

Membrane retrieval and vesicle recycling maintain the ability to transmit repeatedly. Endocytosis restores membrane components, vesicles are reacidified, and transmitter transporters use the proton electrochemical gradient to fill many vesicle classes. The proton gradient is generated by an ATP-dependent pump. Thus an impulse depends not only on ion gradients at the neuronal surface but also on metabolic maintenance of the secretory apparatus. Failure of transmitter filling can reduce the amount released by individual fusion events, whereas failure of priming can decrease the number of available fusion events.

The causal order offers powerful predictions. A selective block of presynaptic calcium channels can leave the action potential intact yet greatly reduce evoked transmission. A postsynaptic receptor antagonist can abolish the measured postsynaptic current even when the transmitter is secreted normally. Impairment of clearance may prolong receptor exposure without increasing the initial number of vesicles released. These distinct possibilities explain why observing a small postsynaptic response is insufficient to localise a defect without additional evidence.

Calcium dependence and the reliability of release

At rest, a presynaptic terminal contains very little free cytosolic calcium compared with the extracellular fluid. Transport systems, binding proteins and intracellular stores help maintain this difference. The gradient is especially important because calcium acts as a signal as well as a transported ion. A large increase in basal calcium would change the probability of spontaneous secretion and could disturb cellular processes. The terminal must therefore both generate a local calcium signal quickly and remove or buffer it after activity.

A voltage-gated calcium channel converts a change in membrane voltage into a change in conductance. The amount of calcium entering during a presynaptic spike depends on the channel's activation kinetics, the time spent at depolarised voltages and the instantaneous driving force. Widening a presynaptic action potential can increase the duration of calcium entry under suitable conditions, even when its peak amplitude remains similar. Such an effect explains why a presynaptic potassium channel that contributes to repolarisation may influence synaptic output. Blocking the potassium channel is not equivalent to directly activating the vesicle fusion sensor; the effect is mediated through the altered voltage waveform and calcium entry.

Calcium-dependent release is often steeply nonlinear over a limited physiological range. As an illustrative approximation, suppose release probability is proportional to the fourth power of a relevant local calcium signal when release is far from saturation. Doubling that signal would then increase the predicted release probability sixteenfold. This does not imply that a real probability can exceed one. If the initial probability is 0.01, the model predicts 0.16; if it is 0.2, the prediction of 3.2 is impossible and the approximation has clearly left its useful range. The apparent exponent is not a universal value or a direct count of the number of calcium ions binding every fusion sensor. Spatial arrangement, channel number, cooperativity and saturation influence the observed relationship.

The steepness of the calcium-release relation provides a mechanism for sensitive regulation. A small reduction in calcium entry can lead to a much larger reduction in secretion. In an illustrative experiment, control evoked responses average 80 pA and a calcium-channel perturbation lowers them to 20 pA. It is not justified to conclude that the calcium current has fallen by 75 percent, since the output is nonlinear. Under the fourth-power model, if every other condition were constant, a fourfold reduction in secretion would correspond to a calcium signal ratio of the fourth root of 0.25, approximately 0.707. This would imply about a 29 percent reduction in the relevant calcium signal, not a 75 percent reduction. The calculation demonstrates the importance of distinguishing a measured output from an inferred intermediate.

The spatial arrangement of release sites also matters. Calcium entering through one channel creates a concentration gradient around its mouth. A sensor located nearby may receive a larger and faster signal than a more distant sensor. Buffering molecules compete for calcium and reduce its spread, while diffusion and transport help terminate the signal. An experiment that compares the influence of relatively fast and slow calcium buffers can provide evidence about the functional proximity of channels to the release machinery. The conclusion is relative rather than an exact molecular distance unless supported by additional measurements.

Fast synchronous release follows closely after the presynaptic stimulus, whereas some transmitter release occurs with a longer delay and is termed asynchronous release. Spontaneous release can also occur without an evoked action potential. These forms overlap in parts of their molecular machinery but can differ in regulation and calcium dependence. A miniature event recorded in the absence of action potentials is not evidence that all release is calcium-independent. It is evidence that a full propagated presynaptic spike is not a prerequisite for every elementary event.

The readily releasable pool represents vesicles that can undergo fusion rapidly in response to a suitable stimulus. Additional vesicles can replenish that pool. A finite pool places a limit on release during sustained activity. Consider a simplified terminal with ten available vesicles and a probability of one half that each fuses in response to a stimulus. On average five fuse. If there is no replenishment and the second stimulus acts on the remaining pool with the same probability, an average of 2.5 fuse on the second stimulus. The fractional expected count does not describe an individual event; it is the mean over repetitions. If residual calcium increases the second probability to 0.8, the expected count is four. Facilitation of release probability and depletion of availability can therefore occur simultaneously even when the measured second response is smaller than the first.

Recovery between stimuli may allow vesicles to become ready again. A long rest interval can restore the response size even if the transmitter receptors themselves have not changed. This makes stimulation frequency a critical experimental variable. To distinguish a reduced pool from reduced probability, a researcher can examine responses across different stimulus patterns and intervals, and assess postsynaptic sensitivity independently. A single pair of responses rarely distinguishes the mechanisms uniquely. For exam reasoning, identify what was measured directly, what changed experimentally and which assumptions connect the observations to the proposed explanation.

Transmitter supply, vesicular storage and clearance

A chemical synapse repeatedly loses messenger molecules from the presynaptic compartment. Continued transmission therefore requires a supply of transmitter, a means of concentrating it near the release sites, and a mechanism for restoring a low extracellular concentration between signals. These requirements are distinct. A terminal may synthesise sufficient transmitter yet fail to concentrate it within vesicles. Alternatively, storage and release may work normally while extracellular clearance is impaired. This separation of tasks permits an investigator to use the shape and frequency of synaptic responses to identify which phase of transmission is likely to be affected.

Small-molecule transmitters are often synthesised in the terminal from precursor molecules using enzymes supplied by the cell body. For example, choline acetyltransferase catalyses formation of acetylcholine from choline and acetyl-CoA. Choline recovered from the extracellular environment helps supply substrate for repeated synthesis. Glutamate can be formed from glutamine, and glutamate decarboxylase produces GABA from glutamate in appropriate neurons. Thus two classes of neuron may use the same metabolic intermediate but release different transmitters because they express different enzymes and vesicular transporters. The availability of the precursor is necessary but not sufficient to determine the phenotype of the synapse.

The small vesicles at many fast synapses are chemically specialised. A proton pump uses ATP hydrolysis to move protons into a vesicle, establishing an electrochemical gradient that vesicular transport proteins exploit. Different transporters use components of the gradient in different ways. Vesicular glutamate transporters, vesicular inhibitory amino acid transporters and vesicular monoamine transporters are not interchangeable simply because all are located on vesicles. If a neuron expresses the enzyme needed to make a transmitter but lacks a suitable vesicular transporter, efficient conventional vesicular release of that substance may be compromised. The identity of a released molecule therefore depends on synthesis and sorting as well as on the presence of the fusion apparatus.

Large peptide transmitters are generally made as precursor proteins in the cell body and processed along the secretory pathway. Dense-core vesicles containing peptides can be transported along axons to regions of release. Peptide secretion often requires different patterns of activity from rapid release of small clear vesicles, although the exact dependence varies. A terminal that contains both types of vesicle can produce a rapid receptor-mediated response and a slower modulation of excitability in the same target. This provides a physiological explanation for the observation that a prolonged burst may have consequences that cannot be predicted by simply multiplying the response to one spike by the number of spikes.

An important distinction concerns the biochemical fate of a released molecule. Acetylcholine is rapidly hydrolysed, whereas many monoamines are retrieved through plasma membrane transporters. The glial participation in glutamate handling provides another example of division of labour. Astrocytes can take up extracellular glutamate and contribute to formation of glutamine, which can be returned to neurons as a substrate for transmitter synthesis. This glutamate–glutamine cycle helps maintain both transmitter supply and the extracellular environment. It is not a simple transfer of unchanged glutamate from the postsynaptic receptor back to its own presynaptic vesicle. Metabolic conversion and transport across different cellular membranes are involved.

In a conceptual experiment, an extracellular transporter is inhibited while the vesicular loading transporter remains functional. A brief stimulus may produce a normal early response followed by an increased late component if clearance is slowed. After repeated stimulation, the effect may become more complicated because receptor desensitisation, feedback receptors and altered recycling of transmitter precursors can influence the system. In a different experiment, vesicular loading is impaired, but extracellular clearance remains functional. Miniature responses may become smaller as vesicles with reduced contents are released, even if the number of fusion events has not changed. Comparing current amplitude, decay and event frequency helps distinguish the alternatives.

Energy supply is relevant at several stages of this cycle. Ion gradients drive action potentials and calcium entry; ATP-dependent transport maintains the gradients over longer periods; vesicle filling and recycling also require energy. An energy deficit can therefore affect multiple steps at once. One should not interpret a fall in synaptic output under metabolic stress as definitive evidence for a particular receptor or fusion protein. Measurements of presynaptic voltage, calcium signals, spontaneous responses and receptor sensitivity are useful because they separate different points of failure.

Chemical messenger removal also creates a spatial boundary. When a transmitter diffuses away from its original cleft, it may reach receptors located outside the immediate contact. This is sometimes called spillover. The degree of spillover depends on the amount released, geometry, diffusion and uptake. Not all neurotransmitter signalling is confined to a precisely opposed pair of membranes; some modulatory systems act across a wider extracellular region. The classical fast synapse is nevertheless a useful basic unit, because it permits precise relationships between presynaptic activity and postsynaptic responses. The distinction between local and wider signalling concerns spatial and temporal organization rather than an absolute division between neurotransmitters and hormones.

Quantal transmission and experimental evidence

One of the decisive advances in synaptic physiology came from recording small spontaneous depolarisations at the neuromuscular junction. Fatt and Katz (1952) found miniature end-plate potentials in the absence of deliberately evoked nerve impulses. The events resembled a much smaller version of the response produced by nerve stimulation. This suggested that release occurred in discrete packages. Experiments by del Castillo and Katz (1954) reduced transmitter release probability and showed that evoked responses could fail or cluster around amplitudes corresponding to small integer multiples of a unitary event. The vesicular interpretation was reinforced by structural and physiological evidence.

A quantum is the effective package of transmitter contributing to one elementary postsynaptic response, commonly corresponding to release of a single vesicle. Quantal size is the mean response per elementary event; quantal content is the mean number of quanta released per presynaptic stimulus. It is useful to distinguish the number of transmitter molecules per vesicle from the postsynaptic voltage or current caused by those molecules. The latter also depends on the number and properties of available receptors, the postsynaptic driving force and recording location.

A simple binomial model assumes N independent release sites, each having a probability p of releasing one quantum during a stimulus, and a fixed mean quantal response q. Expected quantal content is Np and expected postsynaptic response is Npq if contributions add linearly. For an illustrative synapse with four available sites, release probability 0.25, and quantal current magnitude 10 pA, the mean current magnitude is 4 × 0.25 × 10 = 10 pA. The probability of zero released quanta is (1 − 0.25)^4, approximately 0.3164. Failure is therefore expected on about 32 percent of trials, although the stimulus and recording are identical.

Under the same model the number of released quanta has variance Np(1 − p). This variance is 4 × 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.75 quanta squared in the example. With constant q the response variance attributable to release is 75 pA squared. Real recordings additionally contain recording noise, differences in vesicular content, variability in receptor responses and possible correlations among release sites. The simple binomial model is a useful testable approximation rather than an exact description of all synapses.

If N is large and p small, release counts may be approximated by a Poisson distribution with mean m. The probability of failure is then exp(−m), giving m = −ln(P0). If 40 of 100 stimuli fail, the illustrative estimate is −ln(0.40) = 0.916 quanta per stimulus. This failure method is not reliable when failure is caused by failure of the axon to conduct, an undetected small response or receptor block rather than absence of release. It also relies on the distributional assumptions; it is not a universally valid way of determining vesicle numbers.

Modern central synaptic recordings can examine miniature postsynaptic currents with action potentials blocked by tetrodotoxin. A decrease in miniature frequency with unchanged amplitude is consistent with reduced spontaneous release or fewer functional contacts, but it does not uniquely prove a particular presynaptic molecular defect. A decrease in amplitude with unchanged frequency is consistent with altered postsynaptic sensitivity or reduced vesicular filling. Comparisons of evoked responses, paired-pulse behaviour and receptor responses to applied agonists help discriminate between possibilities. This distinction between a plausible inference and a logically unique explanation is particularly important in analytical examination questions.

Transmitters and receptor-dependent effects

Neurotransmitters are diverse. Small molecule transmitters include acetylcholine, amino acids such as glutamate, GABA and glycine, monoamines such as dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin and histamine, and purines such as ATP and adenosine. Peptide transmitters include many short polypeptides released from dense-core vesicles. A single neuron can release more than one signalling substance. Consequently, the older simplified picture of one transmitter per neuron cannot be applied as an absolute rule. Different release mechanisms and stimulation patterns may influence which substances are secreted.

The same chemical can act through receptor families with very different properties. Acetylcholine activates nicotinic ionotropic receptors and muscarinic metabotropic receptors. Glutamate commonly produces fast excitation through AMPA receptors and voltage-dependent signalling through NMDA receptors, while metabotropic glutamate receptors can modulate release and postsynaptic excitability. GABA can activate GABA-A receptors, which are chiefly permeable to chloride and also bicarbonate, and GABA-B receptors, which signal through G proteins. Glycine activates inhibitory anion channels at many mature spinal and brainstem synapses. It also acts as a coagonist at NMDA receptors, showing why transmitter names alone do not predict responses.

Ionotropic receptors connect transmitter binding directly to channel gating. Their fast current can influence voltage within milliseconds, making them suitable for precise timing. Metabotropic receptors can act through G-protein subunits or second messengers. For example, GABA-B receptor signalling can increase potassium conductance postsynaptically or inhibit voltage-gated calcium channels presynaptically. Metabotropic action is not necessarily slower in every measurable respect, but its effects commonly outlast the transient transmitter exposure and can alter the gain of other responses.

Receptor desensitisation means that a receptor can respond less during continued or repeated exposure to an agonist even when the agonist remains present. This mechanism differs from clearance of transmitter. It also differs from depletion of presynaptic vesicles, although all three can contribute to changes in an evoked current. To isolate postsynaptic desensitisation experimentally, one could compare responses to controlled repeated transmitter applications while bypassing presynaptic release. The timing and concentration of the test stimulus must be controlled, as a difference in the delivered stimulus can imitate a receptor defect.

Termination of signalling also differs among transmitters. Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses acetylcholine; the liberated choline may be taken up for reuse. Glutamate transporters in neurons and astrocytes remove glutamate from extracellular fluid and help prevent prolonged activation. GABA and monoamines can be cleared by specific uptake transporters. Peptides are often removed by diffusion and extracellular proteolysis. An uptake transporter does not function in the same way as the vesicular transporter that fills a secretory vesicle. One clears an extracellular space, the other prepares an intracellular release package.

Persistent transmitter exposure can prolong receptor activation, but the result depends on receptor kinetics, diffusion and desensitisation. Blocking reuptake need not cause a proportionate increase in every postsynaptic current. If most receptors already bind transmitter near the peak of release, the largest change may occur during decay rather than the peak. If clearance also normally prevents spillover, blockade may recruit receptors beyond the synaptic cleft. Thus peak amplitude, decay time and spatial range must be considered separately.

Identifying receptor mechanisms through perturbations

When a neuron responds to transmitter, the first experimental question is whether the response is generated by a receptor that directly gates a channel or by a receptor coupled to a separate signalling mechanism. A fast current with a well-defined ionic reversal is consistent with direct channel gating, but kinetics alone cannot prove receptor class. A metabotropic pathway may rapidly regulate an existing ion channel, and the measured response time of an ionotropic receptor can be prolonged by transmitter exposure or membrane filtering. Receptor-selective agonists, antagonists and analysis of current-voltage behaviour supply complementary evidence.

Consider two receptor populations activated by glutamate. The first produces a rapidly rising inward current at negative membrane voltages. The second contributes a slower component whose magnitude at negative voltages is reduced by extracellular magnesium. In a common mammalian central synapse, the first response is compatible with an AMPA receptor current and the second with an NMDA receptor current. Both are receptor-mediated cation conductances, but the NMDA receptor is subject to voltage-dependent block by magnesium. Depolarisation can relieve much of the block, allowing current to flow when glutamate and the coagonist are available. Depolarisation by itself is not sufficient to activate a receptor that also requires ligand binding.

NMDA receptors can conduct calcium as well as other cations. A channel may therefore have an approximately zero mixed-cation reversal potential while allowing calcium entry at voltages where the total net current is small. The net current sums contributions from several ionic species; cancellation of those contributions does not require every ion to be at its own equilibrium. This is an example of the distinction between a channel's reversal potential and the equilibrium potential of an individual ion. It also explains why receptor-mediated calcium signalling cannot always be inferred solely from total current amplitude.

A recording from a synapse held at a negative voltage might show a large early AMPA-dominated current and a small late NMDA-dominated current. At a moderately positive holding voltage, magnesium block is reduced and the relative contribution of the late component may become more apparent. The sign of the total current may also reverse because the holding voltage exceeds the mixed-cation reversal. To compare receptor conductances across voltages, use the current divided by the appropriate driving force where an ohmic approximation is valid, rather than directly comparing raw current magnitudes. An NMDA receptor current may be nonlinear, so its effective conductance changes with voltage as well.

Acetylcholine provides a particularly clear example of the physiological consequences of receptor identity. At a fast nicotinic receptor, transmitter binding opens a cation channel and may generate excitation. At a muscarinic receptor coupled to an inhibitory G protein, signalling can open potassium channels in an appropriate target cell and reduce excitability. This is why the inhibitory effect of vagal acetylcholine on the heart does not contradict the excitatory effect of acetylcholine at the skeletal neuromuscular junction. The detailed cardiac and skeletal muscle mechanisms are treated in their respective physiology chapters; here the contrast serves to establish that a transmitter has no single fixed electrical sign.

A metabotropic receptor can influence the response to another transmitter without itself producing a prominent immediate postsynaptic potential. For example, a modulatory receptor might change the availability of voltage-gated potassium channels, altering how quickly a neuron repolarises or how easily it fires in response to subsequent inputs. A presynaptic receptor might alter calcium entry and thereby change the response to every action potential invading a terminal. Such actions change the gain or timing of signalling. When an experimenter detects no immediate voltage change after applying a modulator, it is therefore premature to infer that the cell lacks receptors for the substance. Test responses to a standard stimulus before and after modulation.

Receptor pharmacology also requires controls. If a drug reduces an evoked postsynaptic current, it may act at a postsynaptic receptor, at a presynaptic receptor, on voltage-gated channels or by an unrelated effect on the preparation. A direct agonist response recorded from the postsynaptic cell can help isolate receptor function. If the drug reduces both responses in a comparable way, postsynaptic action becomes more plausible. If it reduces evoked transmission but leaves the direct agonist response intact, a presynaptic mechanism becomes more plausible. These tests are not logically exhaustive, because a direct agonist application may activate receptors outside the synapse or fail to reproduce its normal ligand concentration.

Competitive antagonism and channel block should also be distinguished. A competitive antagonist reduces access of agonist to a receptor's binding site; under ideal equilibrium conditions increasing agonist concentration can overcome competitive inhibition. A channel blocker may obstruct ion passage or stabilise a nonconducting state rather than compete with the transmitter itself. In a brief synaptic event, equilibrium may not be reached, so a simple dose-response rule must be applied with care. The useful exam-level conclusion is that receptor binding, channel opening, current and postsynaptic voltage are separate stages that can be perturbed independently.

The presence of a transmitter in tissue is insufficient to establish its role in transmission at a particular connection. Evidence can include its presence in the presynaptic cell, release associated with appropriate stimulation, a receptor-mediated response to application of the substance, and disruption of the normal synaptic response when the relevant receptor is blocked. These criteria are especially useful when several transmitters coexist. A biochemical assay establishes chemical presence but not necessarily which receptor mediates an observed current. Electrophysiology identifies functional effects but cannot by itself prove which molecule was released without additional evidence.

Reversal potentials, excitation and inhibition

The electrical consequence of opening a channel is governed by its conductance and reversal potential. With outward current defined as positive, a simplified synaptic current is Is = gs(Vm − Es), where Vm is the membrane potential and Es is the reversal potential of the receptor-mediated current. When Vm equals Es there is no net current through those channels, but the channels can remain open. The reversal potential of a channel permeable to one ion corresponds to that ion's equilibrium potential under the usual assumptions. A mixed-ion receptor has a reversal potential determined by its combined permeabilities and ionic gradients rather than by any single ion's equilibrium potential.

A mixed cation conductance with reversal potential near 0 mV carries inward net current at −65 mV. With gs = 1 nS, the outward-positive current is −65 pA. At +10 mV, the same conductance carries +10 pA. The current changes direction at the reversal potential. In an unclamped neuron, opening the conductance drives the voltage toward the reversal potential. In a voltage-clamped cell the experimenter instead supplies compensating current to keep the voltage at the command value, allowing the current-voltage relationship to be measured. Voltage clamp and current clamp therefore answer complementary questions.

An excitatory postsynaptic potential is a graded voltage response that increases the probability of spike initiation. An inhibitory postsynaptic response reduces that probability. In a simple neuron resting at −70 mV with a threshold near −50 mV, an anion conductance reversing at −75 mV tends to hyperpolarise the membrane. If it reverses at −60 mV instead, it can depolarise the resting neuron yet oppose excitation approaching threshold. Opening a conductance whose reversal lies below threshold can restrain voltage near that reversal and drain concurrent excitatory current. This is an important form of shunting inhibition.

The membrane can be modelled as a capacitance C in parallel with several conductances. At steady state and in the absence of injected current, the weighted average of reversal potentials determines the membrane voltage if all conductances are fixed and ohmic. Consider leak conductance 10 nS reversing at −70 mV and an excitatory conductance 5 nS reversing at 0 mV. The steady voltage is (10 × −70 + 5 × 0)/15 = −46.67 mV. Adding an inhibitory conductance of 15 nS reversing at −65 mV changes the voltage to (−700 − 975)/30 = −55.83 mV. The inhibitory conductance alone would move the resting voltage to −67 mV, a depolarisation. Nevertheless, when combined with excitation it prevents this idealised steady voltage from crossing a −50 mV threshold. The example assumes simultaneous constant conductances and ignores regenerative channels, so it is a prediction of passive voltage tendency, not an exact spike waveform.

Spatial summation occurs when multiple synapses at different positions influence the neuron over overlapping time intervals. Temporal summation occurs when successive responses overlap before earlier responses have decayed. In a simple passive compartment, after an instantaneous voltage increment, the response decays approximately exponentially with time constant tau = RmC. A 4 mV response with a 20 ms time constant leaves 4 exp(−10/20) = 2.43 mV after 10 ms. A second identical increment then gives approximately 6.43 mV. After 40 ms only 0.54 mV remains, and the second peak is about 4.54 mV. These calculations assume small responses and a constant membrane resistance; synaptic conductances themselves change resistance, limiting linear addition.

Dendritic geometry affects the extent to which a local synaptic potential reaches the axon initial segment. A distal input often attenuates more than a proximal input, but branch diameter, passive properties, active dendritic conductances and receptor distribution can alter this pattern. The axon initial segment usually possesses a high density of voltage-gated sodium channels, making it a common location for spike initiation. The soma is not a literal arithmetic adder. Its voltage reflects currents distributed across a complex electrical structure, and threshold itself is affected by recent activity. The simple summation model is a starting point for reasoning about integration, not a replacement for the biology.

Time course of synaptic currents and membrane responses

Voltage and current are distinct physical quantities. Current measures charge movement per unit time, whereas voltage is a difference in electrical potential. When a receptor opens channels, the new current changes charge stored across the membrane. This change produces a voltage response if the voltage is not clamped. A brief current may therefore generate a broader voltage response because charge stored on the membrane does not vanish when the channel closes. Leakage and other conductances discharge the membrane, giving the voltage response its characteristic decay.

For a single passive compartment, current conservation can be written as C dV/dt = −gL(V − EL) − gs(t)(V − Es) + Iinj. Here C is membrane capacitance, V is membrane voltage, gL and EL are leak conductance and its reversal potential, gs(t) and Es are the time-dependent synaptic conductance and its reversal potential, and Iinj is externally injected inward-positive current. With capacitance in pF, conductances in nS, voltage in mV and time in ms, all terms have units of pA. This relation is not a full Hodgkin–Huxley equation because the active voltage-gated currents have been omitted. It describes the passive or subthreshold tendency of voltage in a simplified model.

For a constant total conductance and a fixed injected current, the steady-state voltage is obtained by setting dV/dt to zero. The time required to approach that voltage is governed by the effective time constant C divided by total conductance. A neuron with 200 pF capacitance and 10 nS total resting conductance has a time constant of 20 ms. If an inhibitory conductance of 10 nS is added, total conductance doubles to 20 nS and the time constant becomes 10 ms. Thus inhibition can narrow the temporal integration window even when its reversal potential is exactly equal to resting voltage. A small voltage response at rest can conceal a large change in the cell's capacity to integrate inputs.

Suppose a cell has a capacitance of 100 pF and a leak conductance of 5 nS, giving a 20 ms resting time constant. A sustained inward current of 50 pA would shift its steady voltage by I/g = 10 mV. At one time constant after the beginning of this constant current, the voltage shift reaches about 63 percent of the final value, or 6.32 mV. If the same current lasts only 2 ms, the shift at its end is approximately 10[1 − exp(−0.1)] = 0.95 mV. The total charge delivered and the duration relative to the time constant matter. For a real synaptic event the current is not constant, since the voltage changes the driving force and the receptor opens and closes.

A large conductance can cause saturation of a voltage response even when release continues to increase. For example, a leak reversal of −70 mV and an excitatory reversal of 0 mV give an equilibrium voltage of −35 mV when equal leak and excitatory conductances are activated in a passive model. Doubling excitation relative to leak gives −23.3 mV rather than 0 mV or +35 mV. Further increases move the voltage closer to the excitatory reversal. The voltage response is therefore not simply proportional to conductance over large changes. Voltage-clamp recordings at a suitable fixed command potential help separate conductance changes from this voltage-dependent reduction in driving force.

The current measured at a somatic electrode can also differ from the actual current at a distal synapse. Dendritic membranes possess capacitance and conductances, and axial resistance separates distant regions from the electrode. This means that the command voltage at the soma may not accurately control every branch, a limitation called imperfect space clamp. For standard examination problems, the assumption of a single compartment is often explicit or implicit. In a real experiment, a change in apparent response size may partly reflect changes in the location of active synapses or their electrical filtering.

A voltage-clamp current–voltage plot can identify the reversal potential when an approximately linear synaptic conductance is present. For illustrative data, currents at −80, −60, −40 and −20 mV are −40, −20, 0 and +20 pA. Each 20 mV increase produces a 20 pA increase in current, so the slope is 1 nS and the reversal is −40 mV. If the spike threshold is −45 mV, the reversal lies above threshold and this conductance could support excitation in a simple passive-threshold model. If threshold is instead −35 mV, the same conductance cannot by itself drive voltage beyond that level in the model. To classify the real functional effect, consider competing conductances and voltage-dependent processes as well as the reversal.

This quantitative framework connects the membrane physiology of the preceding nodes to neural computation. It explains why two currents of the same peak amplitude can generate different potentials, why a conductance can inhibit without producing a negative voltage deflection, and why summation is only approximately linear. It also demonstrates the value of recording both current and voltage when trying to determine the mechanism of a response.

Chloride gradients and context-dependent inhibition

In many mature central neurons, chloride-permeable receptors provide rapid inhibition. The electrochemical gradient of chloride differs from that of sodium or potassium because chloride is negatively charged. For a membrane selectively permeable to chloride, the Nernst equation has valence minus one. At 37 degrees Celsius, using base-ten logarithms, the approximate equilibrium voltage is −61.5 mV times log10 of extracellular concentration divided by intracellular concentration. If extracellular chloride is 120 mM and intracellular chloride is 10 mM, log10(12) is about 1.079 and ECl is approximately −66.4 mV. Increasing intracellular chloride to 30 mM gives a ratio of four and an equilibrium potential of about −37.0 mV. The increased internal concentration makes the reversal less negative.

A chloride channel does not intrinsically pump chloride inward. Its net flux depends on the electrochemical driving force. When a chloride-selective channel opens at a voltage more positive than ECl, chloride tends to enter the cell, producing outward-positive conventional current. At voltages more negative than ECl, net chloride flux can be outward, producing inward conventional current and depolarisation. The sign convention for current refers to the movement of positive charge, so the direction of a negative ion is opposite to the direction of conventional current. Confusing these two directions leads to many examination errors.

GABA-A receptors are also permeable to bicarbonate, so their reversal potential need not equal the chloride equilibrium potential exactly. The relative permeabilities and gradients determine the combined reversal. In mature neurons chloride transporters, including the potassium-chloride cotransporter KCC2, help maintain a low intracellular chloride concentration. In other developmental or physiological contexts, intracellular chloride may be higher, and GABA-A activation may produce depolarisation or excitation depending on the total conductance and threshold. NKCC1 can contribute to chloride accumulation in some cells. These transporters influence the gradient over time; they are not the channels responsible for the immediate receptor-mediated current.

The functional consequences of depolarising GABA cannot be decided merely from the sign of the voltage change. If a receptor-mediated conductance reverses above rest but below threshold, a large conductance can oppose coincident excitation by clamping the membrane toward a subthreshold voltage. On the other hand, a sufficiently depolarised reversal, interaction with voltage-dependent channels or the timing of excitation can make the signal excitatory. The effects depend on the cell's state. A mature-neuron example should not be generalised to every developmental stage, and an immature-neuron example should not be generalised to every synapse in the adult nervous system.

Investigators must take care when measuring chloride-dependent reversal potentials. Whole-cell recording can alter intracellular ion concentrations because the pipette solution exchanges with cytoplasm. A preparation in which intracellular chloride is artificially high may display a depolarising GABA response even when the native cell would not. Methods that preserve the native chloride gradient can be preferable for determining the physiological reversal potential. The important lesson for examination interpretation is that the experimental solution is part of the system: ionic gradients cannot be assumed if the protocol changes them.

In a neural circuit, inhibition also acts on the timing and location of excitation. A dendritic inhibitory contact can strongly limit excitation from the same branch, whereas a more proximal inhibitory contact can influence inputs converging from many branches. A synapse near the initial segment can affect the final conversion into action potentials. These are tendencies, not immutable anatomical laws. The actual effect depends on conductance, reversal potential and timing as well as placement. A circuit diagram indicating an inhibitory connection should be interpreted in relation to these physical properties rather than as a simple subtraction symbol detached from membrane physiology.

Dendritic integration and the spatial organisation of synapses

The position of a synapse is one of the variables that determines its influence on neuronal output. A dendrite is not an ideal conductor. Current entering its membrane can flow in either direction along the intracellular space, leave through membrane conductances or alter the charge stored on its membrane. A voltage change recorded close to a synapse can consequently be much larger than the change recorded at the soma. The extent of this reduction depends on the electrical properties and shape of the intervening membrane. A distant synapse cannot be classified as unimportant simply because a particular recording location detects a small voltage.

An elementary passive cable model represents a long, uniform cylindrical neurite. Membrane resistance per unit length is denoted by rm and axial resistance per unit length by ri. The characteristic length constant is the square root of rm/ri. If rm is measured in ohm metres and ri in ohms per metre, the ratio has units of square metres and its square root is a length. Under the simplified assumptions of a long passive cable and a sustained voltage perturbation, the voltage change decreases approximately as exp(−x/λ), where x is distance from the perturbation and λ is the length constant. At one length constant, approximately 37 percent of the original voltage change remains. At two length constants, approximately 14 percent remains.

For a hypothetical cable with length constant 200 micrometres, a sustained local voltage increment of 10 mV would be reduced to about 6.07 mV at 100 micrometres and 3.68 mV at 200 micrometres. If another cable has twice the length constant, attenuation over 200 micrometres is less severe and the predicted change is about 6.07 mV. This comparison assumes identical starting voltage increments and the same simple boundary conditions. A real dendritic branch has a finite length, variable diameter, changing membrane properties, branches and potentially active conductances. For a brief synaptic signal, the spatial attenuation and temporal filtering also depend on signal frequency. The passive model is therefore a useful conceptual baseline rather than a detailed reconstruction of a real dendritic tree.

Increasing membrane resistance reduces current lost through the membrane, tending to increase the distance over which voltage spreads. Decreasing axial resistance makes it easier for current to flow along the interior and also tends to increase the length constant. A thin dendritic segment has relatively high axial resistance; a thicker one provides a larger cross-sectional route for intracellular current. These principles explain why geometric differences alter the effective weight of otherwise similar synapses. They also explain why a conductance increase between a distal excitatory synapse and the soma can diminish transmission of voltage along that route.

The arrangement of contacts on dendritic spines introduces an additional spatial feature. A spine is a small protrusion from a dendrite, often receiving an excitatory synapse. Its head may be connected to the shaft by a narrow neck. The resulting electrical and diffusional properties can influence the local response and biochemical signalling. Local calcium changes may be restricted more strongly than changes in somatic voltage. It would therefore be mistaken to assume that a small response at the soma rules out a substantial receptor-mediated signal in the spine. Structural isolation is not complete, and the degree of compartmentalisation varies between spines.

Synapses are not always linearly additive even when the individual inputs are subthreshold for a somatic action potential. When two excitatory contacts open simultaneously on one branch, the local depolarisation reduces the driving force for their cation currents. This can make the combined voltage response smaller than the sum of the two individual responses. Conversely, if local depolarisation recruits voltage-dependent channels or relieves magnesium block of NMDA receptors while glutamate is bound, the response may be larger than predicted by linear addition. The terms sublinear and supralinear describe the direction of deviation from an explicitly defined additive prediction; neither is a synonym for inhibition or excitation.

Consider two hypothetical excitatory inputs that individually produce somatic increments of 2 and 3 mV at their peaks. If their peaks coincide, the simplest linear prediction is 5 mV. A measured increment of 3.8 mV is sublinear relative to that prediction. It could arise from reduced driving force, conductance changes, filtering or concurrent inhibitory activity. A measured increment of 7 mV is supralinear and could reflect recruitment of active dendritic mechanisms. The voltage measurements alone do not uniquely identify the responsible channels. Comparing the combined response with independently measured single responses, and testing a relevant receptor or channel perturbation, makes the reasoning more informative.

The cell body and initial segment do not merely receive a static total of excitatory and inhibitory signals. Their voltage changes during time, and their own voltage-gated conductances respond to that changing voltage. An excitatory input arriving shortly after a spike may fail to evoke another spike during a refractory period even if the same input would be effective after rest. Likewise, a prior inhibitory input can alter the availability of voltage-gated channels and affect the response after the inhibitory conductance has decayed. The exact sign of this delayed influence depends on the channel properties. Spike initiation must therefore be understood as a dynamic interaction between synaptic inputs and intrinsic membrane excitability.

These principles provide a way to interpret changes in firing independently of changes in synaptic strength. A neuron can fire more often because excitatory synapses are stronger, because inhibition is weaker, or because intrinsic membrane excitability has increased. An experiment that only counts action potentials cannot distinguish these possibilities. Recording subthreshold potentials or currents, testing responses to injected current, and examining synaptic inputs separately provide distinct measures. If the current required to evoke firing decreases while the same synaptic current is maintained, a change in intrinsic excitability becomes a plausible explanation. If the response to injected current is stable but an evoked synaptic current increases, altered transmission becomes more plausible. This distinction is fundamental when assessing physiological plasticity.

Reading physiological traces and distinguishing causes

Electrophysiological records are central to synaptic physiology because the relevant events can unfold within milliseconds. A useful analysis begins by identifying what the vertical and horizontal axes represent. A trace labelled membrane potential in millivolts measures voltage. A trace labelled current in picoamperes measures electrical current, usually under specified voltage-clamp conditions. A graph of evoked response magnitude against stimulus interval measures neither an instantaneous voltage nor an instantaneous current waveform; it compares a summary measure from repeated trials. Confusing these kinds of plots leads to conclusions that cannot follow from the observations.

In current clamp, the electrode records how the membrane voltage changes when synaptic inputs occur. A depolarising response may be followed by a regenerative spike if the voltage reaches a sufficiently excitable state. The spike should not be included in an estimate of the underlying subthreshold potential amplitude. In voltage clamp, the experimenter attempts to maintain a chosen voltage and measures the current required to do so. A receptor-mediated current may therefore be visible even if the membrane voltage does not change. A flat voltage trace under successful voltage clamp does not demonstrate absence of synaptic signalling.

The time between a stimulus marker and onset of an evoked response is a latency. It includes any axonal conduction time from the point of stimulation, delay at the synapse and contributions of the recording and detection method. If stimulation is moved farther from the recording site while axonal conduction speed remains constant, latency can increase without any change in synaptic release kinetics. Conversely, a change in synaptic latency does not necessarily mean that axon conduction has slowed. Separate stimulation and recording sites, together with direct recording of the presynaptic impulse, make these contributions easier to distinguish.

Peak amplitude, rise time, decay time and area under the current trace contain different information. The peak indicates the maximum measured current; rise time reflects how rapidly current develops; decay time reflects how long it persists; and the area under a baseline-subtracted current trace measures net transferred charge. In a simple rectangular approximation, an inward current with magnitude 20 pA lasting 5 ms transfers 100 pA·ms, equivalent to 100 femtocoulombs, or 0.1 picocoulomb. A 10 pA current lasting 10 ms transfers the same magnitude of charge. Nevertheless, these two currents may have different effects on peak voltage and on coincidence with other inputs.

A common experimental comparison uses the ratio of responses before and after a perturbation. Suppose two preparations both exhibit an evoked current that decreases from 100 to 60 pA. In preparation A, the mean miniature event magnitude decreases from 20 to 12 pA while frequency is approximately constant. In preparation B, miniature amplitude remains at 20 pA but frequency decreases substantially. Under simplified assumptions, preparation A is consistent with a smaller response per quantum, while preparation B is consistent with reduced spontaneous secretion or fewer functional contacts. Neither observation by itself rules out all mixed mechanisms. Evoked and spontaneous release can be regulated differently, and changes in detection threshold can distort apparent event frequency if responses become very small.

Another comparison concerns variability. A preparation may display small evoked responses with frequent failures rather than a uniformly reduced response on every trial. The failures are compatible with low release probability, particularly if successful events maintain the normal miniature amplitude. However, failures could also arise when the stimulation sometimes fails to excite the axon or when the recording electrode loses stability. A reliable presynaptic response and stable recording conditions are therefore essential controls before interpreting failures as a property of the release process.

Miniature amplitude histograms are useful when analysing quantal responses. A histogram with peaks near integer multiples of a unit amplitude can support discrete release, but biological variability and noise may cause peaks to overlap. The absence of perfectly separated peaks does not disprove quantal secretion. At a central synapse, the responses of different release sites may be filtered differently by dendrites, so the measured amplitude of one vesicle release is not identical at all contacts. Statistical averages must be interpreted with regard to the assumptions of the recording arrangement.

In experimental designs, a rescue or recovery test can be especially informative. If lowering extracellular calcium reduces evoked release and restoration of calcium restores transmission, the reversibility strengthens the causal interpretation. If a postsynaptic receptor antagonist abolishes an evoked current and the current returns after washout, reversible receptor inhibition is plausible. However, an intervention may have multiple effects that all reverse when it is removed. Rescue demonstrates a connection between the intervention and response; it does not automatically identify every molecular step in the causal chain.

Analytical examination problems often provide only a few measurements from a hypothetical preparation. The correct response is the one best supported by the stated observations and assumptions, not the most elaborate possible biological explanation. An increase in paired-pulse ratio with reduced first response can support a presynaptic decrease in release probability, but a change in receptor desensitisation or saturation could produce a similar pattern. When the question explicitly states that postsynaptic receptor responses and axonal conduction are unchanged, the inference becomes stronger. This habit of reading experimental controls is as important as memorising the molecular names of synaptic proteins.

Presynaptic regulation and plasticity

Synaptic strength can change during repeated use without changing the anatomical existence of the synapse. An axoaxonic connection can regulate the activity of another presynaptic terminal. In one common mechanism, metabotropic inhibitory receptors reduce calcium channel activity, decreasing release probability. The postsynaptic neuron then receives a smaller response despite an unchanged action potential in the signalling axon. Other presynaptic mechanisms include changes in potassium conductance and regulation of fusion machinery. A receptor activated by transmitter from the same neuron is an autoreceptor; one activated by a different signalling substance may be a heteroreceptor. These terms describe the source and identity of the regulating signal, not an invariant excitatory or inhibitory outcome.

Short-term facilitation describes increased synaptic efficacy during closely spaced stimuli. A residual calcium signal after the first impulse can increase the probability of release during the second. The second postsynaptic response may therefore be greater without an increase in the amplitude of either presynaptic action potential. Short-term depression describes a reduction in efficacy. Depletion of readily releasable vesicles, receptor desensitisation and other mechanisms can all contribute. At a synapse with high initial release probability, the first stimulus may consume much of the available pool, reducing release at a second stimulus before recovery. A low initial probability synapse can instead show facilitation. These tendencies are not absolute rules.

The paired-pulse ratio is often defined as the amplitude of the second evoked response divided by that of the first. Two currents with magnitudes 40 and 60 pA yield a ratio of 1.5; currents of 80 and 40 pA yield 0.5. The ratio is a descriptive measure, not direct measurement of release probability. Changing the stimulus interval, extracellular calcium or receptor properties can help test the mechanism. A smaller first response can make the ratio appear larger even when the second response is unchanged; inspection of both absolute amplitudes is essential.

Long-term potentiation and long-term depression describe persistent increases and decreases in synaptic efficacy after particular activity patterns. A classical example of potentiation occurs at many hippocampal CA3-to-CA1 glutamatergic synapses. AMPA-mediated depolarisation relieves the voltage-dependent magnesium block of NMDA receptors while glutamate and a coagonist are bound. Calcium entry can activate intracellular mechanisms that increase the efficacy of AMPA-mediated responses, including increased receptor availability. Some forms of depression involve different calcium signals and phosphatase-dependent changes that reduce AMPA receptor availability. The calcium pattern, receptor composition and synapse type determine the outcome. Not all LTP or LTD requires NMDA receptors, nor do these descriptions identify a complete mechanism of memory.

A conceptual experiment stimulates two independent input pathways to one hippocampal region. Record baseline responses, apply a conditioning pattern to one pathway, and continue testing both pathways with the original low-frequency stimuli. A persistent increase restricted to the conditioned input supports input-specific potentiation. The control pathway helps distinguish a synaptic change from a general change in recording quality or cellular excitability. Test whether an NMDA receptor antagonist applied during induction prevents the increase. This can support receptor involvement in induction but does not show that the receptor alone stores the persistent change. Distinguishing induction, expression and maintenance clarifies experimental interpretations.

A synapse is therefore best understood as a dynamic link whose effect depends on the history of activity as well as on present voltage and transmitter exposure. The same incoming spike train can be transformed differently by facilitating and depressing synapses. The cellular rules developed here provide the foundation for studying the organised pathways of the brain and spinal cord.

Time-dependent synaptic filtering and short-term plasticity

Synaptic efficacy is often defined operationally as the response evoked by a specified presynaptic stimulus under specified recording conditions. This definition is important because efficacy is not identical to the amount of transmitter in the tissue, the number of receptors visible under microscopy or the total number of terminals. The measured response reflects several variables in combination. It can change because the probability of vesicle fusion changes, because the number of available vesicles changes, because the response to one vesicle changes or because the postsynaptic electrical environment changes. A meaningful interpretation specifies which variables are controlled.

Consider a two-stimulus experiment repeated with several intervals. If facilitation depends in part on a residual calcium signal, its contribution should usually diminish as the interval becomes longer and calcium returns toward baseline. If depression reflects depletion of a releasable vesicle pool, its contribution should also diminish as the pool recovers. The two processes may occur together with different time courses. A synapse can show facilitation at one interval and depression at another because the relative magnitudes of these processes change. Such an observation does not require that the synapse abruptly switches from one exclusive molecular mechanism to another.

The depletion model can be extended with a simple recovery assumption. Suppose a pool initially contains twenty ready vesicles. Each stimulus releases half the available vesicles on average. Ten remain immediately after the first stimulus. Assume that half of the missing ten are replenished before the second stimulus. The second stimulus then encounters fifteen ready vesicles and releases an expected 7.5. If the elementary response size is constant and individual responses add, the second-to-first ratio is 0.75. Without replenishment the ratio would be 0.5, and with complete replenishment it would be 1.0. These predictions are illustrative expected values, not measured vesicle counts from a particular preparation.

Now suppose the second stimulus has a release probability of 0.8 because of facilitation. With fifteen available vesicles, the second expected count is twelve and the ratio becomes 1.2. Thus a paired-pulse ratio greater than one does not prove that the first stimulus caused no depletion. The readily releasable pool may still be smaller than at baseline, but an increase in fusion probability more than compensates. Conversely, a ratio smaller than one does not prove that residual calcium is absent. A reduced pool may outweigh an increased probability. The observed ratio reveals the net response, while the underlying mechanisms can act in opposite directions.

An additional determinant is the recovery of receptors from desensitisation. Two stimuli can release comparable amounts of transmitter but evoke different currents if receptors have not recovered between them. A useful test is to compare paired responses to controlled applications of agonist that bypass release. If repeated direct applications also show depression, postsynaptic receptor behaviour becomes a candidate mechanism. If direct responses recover fully while evoked responses remain depressed, a presynaptic factor becomes more plausible. Differences in agonist delivery and activation of extrasynaptic receptors limit a perfect comparison. Experimental controls increase confidence rather than eliminating every alternative.

Short-term plasticity allows synapses to function as temporal filters. A facilitating synapse can transmit isolated impulses weakly but transmit a burst more effectively. A depressing synapse can respond strongly to the beginning of a stimulus sequence and less strongly to a sustained high-frequency input. These tendencies can be described as preferential transmission of bursts or changes in activity, but the precise frequency preference depends on recovery, release probability, receptor kinetics and the range of frequencies examined. A synapse that depresses in one stimulation protocol is not necessarily ineffective during all high-frequency activity.

A hypothetical series illustrates the distinction. Five equal presynaptic spikes evoke response magnitudes of 10, 15, 18, 20 and 20 pA at synapse F. At synapse D, the same stimulation evokes 40, 30, 22, 17 and 14 pA. The first response at D is four times that at F, but the fifth response at F exceeds the fifth at D. If each synapse contacts a different target with otherwise identical excitability, the relative strength of signalling changes during the burst. If the two inputs converge on one target, the timing and net conductance must also be considered. Adding peak amplitudes is insufficient when the peaks occur at different times or conductances interact.

A longer period of enhanced release after a conditioning train can include augmentation and post-tetanic potentiation. These are generally distinguished from rapid paired-pulse facilitation by their time course and experimental context. They can involve changes in residual calcium signalling and release machinery. The term potentiation by itself does not imply the same mechanism as hippocampal long-term potentiation. The duration of a measured response change, the induction pattern and the cellular site of expression are all relevant when choosing terminology.

Depression during a sustained train should similarly be distinguished from permanent loss of synapses or long-term depression. A response that recovers within a short rest period is compatible with transient regulation of release or receptors. A response that remains reduced long after the conditioning activity requires examination of longer-lasting mechanisms. Measuring recovery is thus an essential part of classifying plasticity. A single measurement immediately after conditioning cannot establish whether a change is genuinely persistent.

The physiological role of short-term plasticity is not simply to make transmission better or worse. It changes the relationship between the temporal pattern of an input and its influence on downstream cells. This provides a mechanistic basis for the fact that different neural pathways with similar numbers of connections can transmit different aspects of the same activity pattern. It also establishes a bridge to later studies of neural circuits, where the timing of excitation and inhibition shapes coordinated behaviour.

Evidence and interpretation of long-term synaptic changes

Long-term changes in transmission are studied by comparing the response to a standard test input before and after a conditioning pattern. A stable baseline is necessary because synaptic responses may drift even without experimental conditioning. The test stimulus is ordinarily kept comparable across the experiment, and the persistence of the change is measured over time. The term long-term potentiation describes a persistent enhancement relative to baseline under the conditions of the experiment; long-term depression describes persistent weakening. These are operational descriptions of physiological changes, not declarations that a specific molecular pathway has been proven.

Classical studies by Bliss and Lømo (1973) in the hippocampal formation established that a brief period of patterned stimulation could produce a sustained increase in synaptic responses. The key question was whether recent activity could change the effectiveness of a neural connection for a time longer than the stimulus itself. Monitoring responses after conditioning allowed the investigators to distinguish persistent changes from immediate summation during stimulation. The original work concerned the hippocampal dentate region; the frequently taught NMDA-dependent CA3-to-CA1 example is a related but distinct preparation. It is important not to collapse every experiment on hippocampal plasticity into one universal experimental protocol.

A standard teaching example at a glutamatergic hippocampal synapse involves an association between transmitter release and postsynaptic depolarisation. Glutamate binds NMDA receptors while postsynaptic depolarisation relieves voltage-dependent magnesium block. Calcium entry provides a signal that can trigger changes in synaptic efficacy. This dependence on both ligand and voltage makes the receptor a useful example of a molecular coincidence detector. The term detector is an analogy: the receptor does not evaluate information consciously, but its current depends on two physical conditions being satisfied. Coagonist availability and other cellular conditions also influence activation.

At many CA1 synapses, an appropriate calcium signal recruits protein kinase-dependent processes including CaMKII activity. Increased AMPA receptor contribution can enhance the response to subsequent glutamate release. The initial calcium signal is involved in induction, whereas the changed AMPA-mediated response is an aspect of expression. The persistence of the changed state is maintenance. An intervention that prevents induction when given during conditioning may not reverse expression after potentiation has been established. Conversely, interfering with receptor availability after induction can reduce the expression of an established response without proving that those receptors were the sole induction signal.

A controlled experiment can distinguish these temporal roles. Imagine that a receptor antagonist is applied to group A only during conditioning, to group B only after conditioning, and to group C not at all. All groups receive the same test input and a control procedure. If the persistent response increase is absent in A but remains in B and C, the result supports the requirement for that receptor during induction under the experimental conditions. It does not demonstrate that the receptor is unnecessary for every form of maintenance, since the timing and duration of the antagonist application matter. If the drug also suppresses the baseline test response, the measurement must be interpreted relative to that direct effect.

An illustrative dataset can help separate the relevant quantities. Suppose responses are normalised to a baseline of 100 percent. At ten, thirty and sixty minutes after conditioning, a control pathway shows 101, 99 and 102 percent, while a conditioned pathway shows 160, 151 and 148 percent. The persistent pathway-specific enhancement is consistent with LTP under these hypothetical conditions. It is not appropriate to report a precise population estimate or statistical significance without information about variability, sample size and experimental replication. The data are a teaching example designed to show how to interpret a trend, not published experimental findings.

Input specificity means that enhancement can be restricted to activated connections rather than indiscriminately increasing every response of a neuron. Associativity means, in the relevant paradigm, that a weak input can undergo potentiation when its activity coincides with sufficiently strong depolarising activity supplied by another input. Cooperativity describes conditions in which multiple inputs together provide sufficient activation for induction when their separate activity would be inadequate. These properties follow naturally from the requirement for transmitter binding combined with postsynaptic depolarisation in many NMDA-dependent forms of LTP. They should not be treated as mandatory properties of every plasticity mechanism.

Long-term depression is equally important to an adaptable system. If synaptic efficacy could only increase, many responses would approach saturation and the relative differences among inputs could be lost. Some activity patterns evoke a persistent decrease in efficacy. In certain NMDA-dependent hippocampal forms, a calcium signal with a different time course or magnitude favours phosphatase-dependent processes and reduced AMPA-mediated transmission. This example is useful for understanding how one intracellular ion can participate in opposite outcomes. The response depends on which signalling pathways are recruited, not merely on whether calcium was present. Different brain regions and synapse types can employ distinct mechanisms of LTD.

The relationship between synaptic plasticity and learning requires careful reasoning. A lasting change in synaptic efficacy is a candidate cellular mechanism by which past activity influences future circuit function. Demonstrating LTP in an isolated preparation shows that a connection can change persistently. It does not by itself demonstrate that the animal has acquired a particular memory. Evidence connecting a plasticity mechanism to behaviour requires additional experiments that relate the mechanism to a learning task while controlling for sensory, motor and motivational effects. The distinction protects students from the overly broad claim that every increase in synaptic strength is equivalent to memory.

It is also necessary to distinguish changes in the strength of existing contacts from changes in synapse number or intrinsic excitability. A persistent increase in an evoked postsynaptic response may involve increased receptor contribution, increased transmitter release, recruitment of previously weak contacts or changes in electrical filtering. Tests of presynaptic function, miniature responses, receptor properties and neuronal excitability can help narrow the mechanism. The correct inference depends on which measurements were actually made. A persistent increase in firing without a measured change in synaptic transmission is not sufficient to establish LTP at a particular synapse.

These examples complete the progression from an individual action potential to a flexible network. Electrical coupling and chemical secretion establish communication; receptor conductances transform incoming signals into graded responses; dendritic and somatic processes integrate those responses; short-term and long-term changes alter the weighting of inputs over time. The next chapter can therefore treat anatomical regions of the nervous system not as isolated labelled structures but as organised collections of cells connected by mechanisms whose consequences are experimentally testable.

Neural circuits as combinations of synaptic operations

A neuron receives convergent inputs when multiple upstream cells contact it. A neuron produces divergent outputs when its axon distributes activity to multiple targets. Convergence permits integration of different sources of information, while divergence enables one signal to coordinate several downstream processes. Neither property establishes the sign of the interaction. A divergent axon can contact target cells with different receptors, so the downstream consequences can differ. Convergence can combine excitation, inhibition and modulation rather than summing signals of a single kind.

Feedforward inhibition occurs when an input activates a principal neuron and also recruits an inhibitory interneuron that acts on that neuron or on a related target. If the direct excitatory path is faster than the indirect inhibitory path, a brief window of excitation may precede inhibition. The arrangement can sharpen the timing of the principal neuron's output. Increasing the strength of feedforward inhibition can shorten that window without necessarily abolishing the initial excitatory response. Changing the latency of the interneuron can change the width of the window. Thus circuit timing depends not only on the number of synapses in each route but also on their receptor kinetics and the excitability of the intervening cells.

Feedback inhibition occurs when activity in a principal neuron or population recruits an inhibitory neuron that acts back on the original population. This arrangement can limit excessive activity and shape the duration of responses. Feedback is distinguished from feedforward inhibition by the origin of the signal that recruits the inhibitory path. Both mechanisms can be present in the same network. In a simplified experimental diagram, tracing arrow direction and identifying whether inhibition requires output from the principal neuron usually resolves the distinction.

Disinhibition is an increase in activity that results from removing or reducing an inhibitory influence. Suppose neuron A inhibits neuron B, and B inhibits neuron C. Activation of A reduces the activity of B, thereby reducing inhibition of C. The net effect of the two inhibitory connections may be increased C activity. This effect is not the same as a direct excitatory synapse from A to C. If B was inactive initially, there may be little inhibition to remove; if other inputs dominate C, disinhibition may be insufficient to cause spiking. The sign of a multistep pathway is therefore conditional on the activity state of its components.

These motifs help explain why anatomical descriptions of the brain and spinal cord must be interpreted together with synaptic physiology. A sensory afferent can recruit excitation of one motor output and inhibition of an antagonistic output through different routes. The detailed spinal circuits belong to the later reflex chapters, but their basic logic is already available: direct transmission, convergence, divergence, inhibitory interneurons and integration at spike-generating compartments.

Repeated activity can modify the effective balance of a circuit even without altering the number of connections. If one excitatory input facilitates strongly and a competing pathway depresses, the first may become more influential during a burst. A circuit can therefore discriminate between low-frequency and high-frequency input patterns. In a hypothetical example, an excitatory input initially produces a 2 mV response and increases to 4 mV during closely spaced stimulation, while a second input begins at 4 mV and declines to 2 mV. At the beginning of the sequence the second input dominates; later the first dominates. The same anatomical map produces a changing functional map as the history of activity changes. This is a physiological consequence of synaptic plasticity and not evidence of a new anatomical connection.

Integrated analysis of compound responses and circuits

A useful example of the interaction between electrical and chemical processes is a recording in which the target receives a compound response. Imagine that a single stimulus causes a small rapidly developing voltage deflection followed several milliseconds later by a much larger deflection. A selective antagonist of the postsynaptic chemical receptor eliminates the late deflection but leaves the early component. When the junctional conductance is reduced, the early component decreases. These observations support a mixed connection, provided that the interventions are sufficiently selective and the presynaptic waveform is unchanged. The two components do not necessarily sum linearly in an intact neuron: the early component can influence the voltage-dependent properties of receptors or channels that contribute to the later response. The experiment therefore identifies the two routes while leaving their functional interaction as a separate question.

In this example, the early voltage deflection is not required to have the same polarity as the late deflection. Direct junctional current can be depolarising or hyperpolarising according to the relative voltages of the two cells, whereas the late chemical component tends toward its own receptor-mediated reversal potential. A presynaptic afterhyperpolarisation might even pass through a junction after an initial depolarising spikelet. Measuring a single peak of the compound response could conceal both the change in sign and the relative delays. Recording the complete waveform and testing responses at different holding potentials provides much more useful information than measuring peak voltage alone.

The need to identify the causal route also arises when a synaptic response decreases during repeated stimulation. In a hypothetical circuit containing two sequential chemical synapses, depression recorded in the final neuron might be caused at either junction or by failure of the intervening neuron to produce a spike. An experimenter can stimulate the intermediate neuron directly and compare the resulting final response with the response obtained by stimulating the original input. If the directly evoked response remains stable while the response through the whole circuit declines, the defect is more likely to be in the upstream connection or in recruitment of the intermediate neuron. If both responses decline, the downstream junction or the final cell becomes a candidate. This logic requires that direct stimulation recruit a comparable output from the intermediate cell; stimulating a larger population would make the comparison ambiguous.

Finally, a change in the probability that a neuron fires cannot always be predicted from the average synaptic current alone. Consider two sets of equally strong excitatory inputs with the same number of presynaptic spikes per second. One set delivers its spikes independently over time and the other delivers them in brief coincident groups. The total number of incoming spikes can be identical, but the second set is more likely to create large short-lived voltage increments if the inputs converge and their effects overlap. Conversely, strong depression or coincident inhibition may prevent a synchronous burst from becoming effective. Mean firing rate, coincidence and synaptic history describe different features of the signal. This distinction is particularly useful in questions about why a circuit responds differently to patterns that deliver the same average input.

The physiological significance of integration is precisely this dependence on the timing, spatial distribution and previous activity of inputs. The nervous system can transform signals rather than simply relay them. The mechanisms are sufficient to explain why changes in receptor conductance or release probability can alter a reflex even if the anatomical connections remain intact. Later chapters identify the specific pathways that recruit these mechanisms. Here the explanatory goal is to establish the properties of the junctions and excitable cells from which such pathways are assembled.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Every synapse is chemical

Correct concept: Electrical synapses directly pass current. Why the trap arises: The familiar vesicle diagram is mistaken for a universal model. Exam reasoning: A paired recording showing rapid passive transfer supports electrical coupling.

2. Electrical synapses are always bidirectional and excitatory

Correct concept: Rectification, different membrane properties and voltage polarity affect coupling. Why the trap arises: The gap-junction connection is confused with an ideal symmetric wire. Exam reasoning: Determine current direction from the voltage difference and distinguish channel rectification from cellular asymmetry.

3. Calcium enters the postsynaptic cell to trigger all transmitter release

Correct concept: Fast evoked secretion normally requires calcium at the presynaptic release site. Why the trap arises: Postsynaptic calcium signalling is confused with release induction. Exam reasoning: Locate the calcium measurement relative to the cleft.

4. One spike always releases one vesicle

Correct concept: Release is probabilistic and multiple sites can contribute. Why the trap arises: An all-or-none action potential is confused with quantal output. Exam reasoning: Calculate expected quantal content separately from spike occurrence.

5. Quantal size is the same as quantal content

Correct concept: Size is response per quantum; content is the expected number of quanta. Why the trap arises: Both use the word quantal. Exam reasoning: Identify whether the units describe response amplitude or count.

6. Miniatures require propagated spikes

Correct concept: Spontaneous quantal events can persist with action potentials blocked. Why the trap arises: Students equate evoked and spontaneous secretion. Exam reasoning: Compare TTX effects on evoked responses versus miniatures.

7. A neurotransmitter has one universal electrical sign

Correct concept: Receptor subtype, gradients and cellular state determine effects. Why the trap arises: One familiar example is overgeneralised. Exam reasoning: Compare nicotinic and muscarinic acetylcholine signalling.

8. Zero current at reversal means the channel is closed

Correct concept: The net driving force is zero despite open channels. Why the trap arises: Conductance is confused with current. Exam reasoning: Use I = g(V − E) rather than treating I as a direct measure of open channels.

9. An IPSP must hyperpolarise

Correct concept: Inhibition can occur through a conductance with a reversal above rest but below threshold. Why the trap arises: Voltage direction is substituted for functional outcome. Exam reasoning: Test whether the conductance suppresses threshold crossing.

10. Opening chloride channels always moves chloride inward

Correct concept: Flux direction depends on electrochemical driving force. Why the trap arises: Negative charge is confused with inward conventional current. Exam reasoning: Compare Vm with ECl and account for ion valence.

11. All postsynaptic potentials add exactly

Correct concept: Driving force, conductances and active channels can make summation nonlinear. Why the trap arises: Linear classroom examples are treated as exact. Exam reasoning: Calculate the additive prediction then compare with actual combined response.

12. A large paired-pulse ratio proves absence of depletion

Correct concept: Facilitation can outweigh concurrent depletion. Why the trap arises: A net amplitude ratio is mistaken for a single mechanism. Exam reasoning: Inspect both absolute amplitudes and recovery across intervals.

13. An unchanged miniature amplitude proves presynaptic change

Correct concept: It supports certain interpretations but does not uniquely locate every defect. Why the trap arises: Statistical associations are treated as exclusive evidence. Exam reasoning: Check miniature frequency, receptor response and evoked transmission separately.

14. LTP is simply an EPSP lasting longer

Correct concept: LTP is a persistent change in efficacy measured with later test stimuli. Why the trap arises: The word potentiation is confused with the duration of a single response. Exam reasoning: Separate conditioning from later testing.

15. NMDA receptors open on depolarisation alone

Correct concept: Ligand and coagonist binding are also required. Why the trap arises: Relief of magnesium block is confused with ligand gating. Exam reasoning: Track both receptor binding and voltage requirements.

16. Presynaptic inhibition necessarily hyperpolarises the final postsynaptic neuron

Correct concept: Reduced transmitter release can change the final response without a direct inhibitory conductance there. Why the trap arises: The location of inhibition is ignored. Exam reasoning: Trace the inhibited terminal and its target separately.

17. A greater firing rate proves increased synaptic strength

Correct concept: Intrinsic excitability, inhibition and input timing can change firing. Why the trap arises: Output is mistaken for a direct measure of one synapse. Exam reasoning: Compare injected-current responses and isolated synaptic currents.

21. Two pathways contact one target. Only the conditioned pathway shows enhancement for 60 minutes. The control pathway remains stable. What is supported?

  1. Universal increase in intrinsic excitability as the only explanation
  2. A transient EPSP during the conditioning stimulus
  3. Input-specific persistent potentiation
  4. Proof of a specific behavioural memory
Answer and explanation

C. Input-specific persistent potentiation

Persistent enhancement restricted to the conditioned input supports input-specific LTP. It does not by itself identify a behavioural memory or a unique molecular mechanism.

22. An NMDA antagonist prevents potentiation only when applied during conditioning, not when applied after establishment. What is the strongest inference?

  1. NMDA receptors alone store every memory
  2. NMDA activity is required for induction in this preparation
  3. Maintenance can never involve NMDA receptors
  4. The drug has proven that all synapses use the same mechanism
Answer and explanation

B. NMDA activity is required for induction in this preparation

The timing experiment supports induction dependence, subject to drug selectivity and adequate controls; broader claims exceed the evidence.

23. A inhibits B and B inhibits C. B is tonically active. Activating A increases C firing. Which explanation fits?

  1. Disinhibition
  2. Increased inhibition from B
  3. A cannot affect C
  4. Direct excitation from A is proven
Answer and explanation

A. Disinhibition

A suppresses B, reducing its inhibitory influence on C. Other circuit pathways are possible, but the stated motif explains the observation.

24. A response vanishes when extracellular Ca²⁺ is lowered and returns when restored. The presynaptic spike and direct postsynaptic agonist response persist. What is best supported?

  1. A direct electrical-only synapse
  2. Calcium-dependent presynaptic transmission
  3. A permanent loss of postsynaptic receptors
  4. Failure of axonal conduction
Answer and explanation

B. Calcium-dependent presynaptic transmission

The reversible intervention affects the intermediate calcium-dependent release step while input and receptor responsiveness persist.

Original SET and integrated practice

1. Assertion: A synaptic conductance can inhibit without hyperpolarising. Reason: Conductance opening may reduce membrane resistance and clamp voltage below threshold.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both are true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion

Shunting inhibition can occur at or above resting voltage because the conductance opposes other currents and shortens the integration time.

2. Assertion: An open mixed-ion receptor at its reversal potential has no net current. Reason: Every permeant ion must be at its own equilibrium potential there.

  1. Both true and explanatory
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

B. Assertion true, reason false

Individual currents can cancel. The combined reversal need not equal each individual ion equilibrium potential.

3. Which observation most directly supports electrical coupling in a paired recording?

  1. The target contains synaptic vesicles
  2. The target has a nucleus
  3. A hyperpolarising step in one cell produces an immediate graded hyperpolarisation in the other
  4. A transmitter is found in the tissue
Answer and explanation

C. A hyperpolarising step in one cell produces an immediate graded hyperpolarisation in the other

Direct passive voltage transfer in both directions where present supports electrical coupling. Additional controls distinguish recording artefact.

4. An inward current magnitude of 25 pA persists for 4 ms. What is the magnitude of charge transfer in a rectangular approximation?

  1. 0.1 fC
  2. 100 pC
  3. 6.25 fC
  4. 100 fC
Answer and explanation

D. 100 fC

25 pA × 4 ms = 100 pA·ms = 100 fC = 0.1 pC.

5. Which observation best distinguishes impaired extracellular clearance from reduced vesicle filling?

  1. A smaller miniature amplitude in isolation
  2. Complete loss of presynaptic action potential
  3. Prolonged current decay with preserved early amplitude
  4. A reduction in axonal diameter
Answer and explanation

C. Prolonged current decay with preserved early amplitude

A prolonged decay with preserved initial response supports prolonged exposure, whereas reduced loading can decrease quantal size. Receptor kinetics must also be considered.

6. In a feedforward inhibitory circuit, the principal neuron's excitation precedes inhibition. What happens if inhibitory onset becomes earlier with comparable strength?

  1. The circuit necessarily becomes electrical
  2. The inhibitory neuron stops having receptors
  3. The presynaptic conduction speed must double
  4. The window for excitation is likely shortened
Answer and explanation

D. The window for excitation is likely shortened

Earlier inhibition can limit the period during which excitation effectively drives firing, assuming other timing and conductance factors are unchanged.

Revision and mastery

Electrical coupling transfers current directly; chemical transmission uses calcium-dependent secretion and receptor activation. The membrane integrates conductances and their reversal potentials in space and time. Synaptic plasticity changes the relationship between presynaptic activity and postsynaptic output.

Core equations

Junctional current: I = gj(VA − VB); synaptic current: I = g(V − E); mean quantal content: m = Np; failure probability in the independent binomial model: (1 − p)N; passive time constant: τ = C/g; paired-pulse ratio = second amplitude / first amplitude.
