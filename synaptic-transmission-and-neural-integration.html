From impulse conduction to neural communication

The action potential solves the problem of transmitting information along an excitable membrane over a long distance. It does not by itself explain how information passes from one cell to another. Neurons are distinct cells whose membranes normally prevent ions from moving directly between their cytoplasms. A second mechanism must therefore connect the electrical activity of one neuron to the electrical or biochemical activity of its target. A specialised junction that performs this function is called a synapse. The cell delivering a signal is presynaptic and the receiving cell is postsynaptic. These descriptions refer to one connection rather than to permanent identities: a neuron can be postsynaptic to hundreds of partners while providing presynaptic output to many others.

A nervous system must do more than reproduce every incoming impulse. Consider a spinal motor neuron receiving activity from sensory afferents, inhibitory interneurons and descending pathways. Its output must depend on the relative timing and strength of these signals, not simply on the presence of one impulse. The individual synaptic events usually generate graded changes in voltage or conductance. Their combined effects influence whether the neuron produces its own action potential. This conversion of many inputs into an output pattern is neural integration. A single incoming impulse can therefore fail to evoke a spike, contribute to a later spike or reduce the probability of another input evoking one. The apparently simple distinction between a conducting axon and an integrating neuron is central to understanding reflexes, brain circuits and motor regulation.

A chemical synapse converts a presynaptic electrical event into secretion of a messenger and then into a response determined by the receiving cell. An electrical synapse instead permits direct movement of current between the two cells through intercellular channels. Both use electrochemical gradients established and maintained by living cells; neither provides a source of free electrical energy. Electrical coupling can be useful where cells need coordinated timing. Chemical transmission allows amplification, selective inhibition, modulation and flexible control of response duration. The two modes can coexist between a pair of neurons, and the existence of one does not exclude the other.

The terms excitatory and inhibitory describe effects on the generation of output, not moral qualities of a signal or simply the direction in which the voltage changes. Excitation raises the likelihood of firing, whereas inhibition lowers it under specified conditions. Some synapses regulate transmitter secretion without directly changing the voltage of the final target. Others alter the response to subsequent stimuli. Before examining detailed nervous-system anatomy, it is necessary to establish these cellular rules, because anatomical connectivity alone cannot tell us the functional effect of a pathway.

Electrical transmission and coupling

At an electrical synapse, channels in adjacent plasma membranes form a continuous aqueous route between cells. In vertebrates, six connexin proteins generally assemble into a hemichannel, also called a connexon, and compatible hemichannels from the two cells dock to create an intercellular gap-junction channel. Many channels can form a plaque. In invertebrates, innexins perform analogous intercellular channel functions. Individual channels pass ions and, depending on their composition, some small molecules. They do not create wholesale continuity of cytoplasmic proteins or organelles.

The physical basis of coupling is a potential difference between the cells. If cell A becomes more positive than cell B, the junctional current tends to redistribute charge and depolarise B while loading A. Under an approximately ohmic description, current from A toward B equals junctional conductance multiplied by the difference between their membrane potentials. The conductance is measured in siemens and the voltage difference in volts. A conductance of 2 nS and a potential difference of 15 mV give 30 pA. This is current between the cells, not a prediction that both cells immediately reach the same voltage. Each cell has its own leak conductance and membrane capacitance, so current is also lost through the membrane or used to change stored charge.

In a simple steady-state two-cell experiment, if A is held at a fixed voltage increment and B has membrane conductance gB to its resting reference voltage, the voltage increment in B is the increment in A multiplied by gj/(gj + gB). This coupling coefficient is dimensionless. Suppose gj is 2 nS and gB is 8 nS. A sustained 20 mV change in A produces a 4 mV change in B under the stated assumptions. Doubling the junctional conductance does not double the response: the coefficient becomes 4/12, giving about 6.67 mV. The result illustrates diminishing returns as the two cells approach electrical equality. The coefficient can differ with the direction of testing even if the junction itself is symmetric, because the two cells can have different membrane conductances. Rectification of the channel itself is a separate possibility.

Electrical synapses have little intrinsic transmission delay compared with chemical synapses, although charging the receiving membrane still requires time. Slowly changing signals are often transmitted more effectively than the very brief peak of an action potential because membrane capacitance filters rapid voltage fluctuations. An action potential in A can therefore appear in B as a small spikelet rather than as an identical full-amplitude action potential. The spikelet triggers a regenerative spike only when the receiving neuron becomes sufficiently excitable. Hyperpolarisation can also pass through the junction. This explains why electrical coupling is not equivalent to universal excitation.

Paired intracellular recording offers a direct conceptual test. Inject current into one neuron, record its voltage and simultaneously record the second neuron. An immediate graded response in the second cell that follows both depolarising and hyperpolarising injections supports electrical coupling. Reverse the direction of injection to test reciprocity. Appropriate comparisons include uncoupled cell pairs, conditions that alter junctional conductance and examination of membrane resistance. A rapid response alone does not conclusively establish direct coupling: electrical stimulation can recruit other pathways or produce recording artefacts. The paired voltage response, anatomical evidence and experimental perturbation together establish the mechanism more convincingly.

Electrical synapses can help synchronise groups of interneurons or coordinate rapid escape circuits in some animals. Their properties are regulatable, and they are not merely rigid wires. Coupling can distribute subthreshold activity among neurons and can sometimes reduce the size of a local perturbation by providing a current sink. The consequences depend on the network configuration and relative input strengths.

Architecture and causal sequence of chemical transmission

A typical central chemical synapse contains a presynaptic terminal with clusters of small synaptic vesicles, an active zone that specialises in exocytosis, an extracellular cleft, and a postsynaptic membrane carrying receptor proteins. A postsynaptic density is often visible at excitatory contacts under electron microscopy. Axodendritic, axosomatic and axoaxonic synapses differ in the cellular compartment contacted by the presynaptic ending. Their arrangement helps determine whether a signal influences a dendritic branch, spike initiation, or the release of transmitter from another terminal. Shape alone is not a definitive indicator of transmitter identity or function.

When a presynaptic action potential invades the terminal, membrane depolarisation increases the opening probability of voltage-gated calcium channels. Because free cytosolic calcium is maintained at a concentration much lower than that outside the cell, calcium enters through open channels. The electrical driving force at ordinary terminal voltages also favours entry. A brief local calcium increase near channels and release sites provides the signal that triggers vesicle fusion. The calcium rise is highly local and transient; an average concentration measured over the entire terminal need not represent the concentration experienced by the release sensor.

A vesicle must first reach a release site and become competent for rapid fusion. Docking describes its positioning at the membrane, while priming describes molecular preparation for fusion. Vesicular synaptobrevin, also called VAMP, associates with the plasma membrane proteins syntaxin and SNAP-25 to form a SNARE complex. The assembly of this complex brings the two membranes together. Synaptotagmin acts as an important calcium sensor for fast synchronous secretion at many synapses. Its interaction with membrane lipids and the fusion machinery upon calcium binding promotes rapid opening of a fusion pore. Other proteins regulate readiness and the timing of release; not every vesicle is equally available.

The released transmitter molecules move by diffusion across the extracellular cleft and bind receptors on the target. At an ionotropic synapse, binding alters an ion channel's opening probability. At a metabotropic synapse, receptor activation changes intracellular signalling and may indirectly influence ion channels or other cellular processes. The transmitter is not itself the electrical current that flows through the postsynaptic membrane. Its function is to change receptor activity; the current is carried by ions crossing the membrane through channels. The consequences depend on receptor type, ionic gradients, membrane voltage and other simultaneous conductances.

Membrane retrieval and vesicle recycling maintain the ability to transmit repeatedly. Endocytosis restores membrane components, vesicles are reacidified, and transmitter transporters use the proton electrochemical gradient to fill many vesicle classes. The proton gradient is generated by an ATP-dependent pump. Thus an impulse depends not only on ion gradients at the neuronal surface but also on metabolic maintenance of the secretory apparatus. Failure of transmitter filling can reduce the amount released by individual fusion events, whereas failure of priming can decrease the number of available fusion events.

The causal order offers powerful predictions. A selective block of presynaptic calcium channels can leave the action potential intact yet greatly reduce evoked transmission. A postsynaptic receptor antagonist can abolish the measured postsynaptic current even when the transmitter is secreted normally. Impairment of clearance may prolong receptor exposure without increasing the initial number of vesicles released. These distinct possibilities explain why observing a small postsynaptic response is insufficient to localise a defect without additional evidence.

Quantal transmission and experimental evidence

One of the decisive advances in synaptic physiology came from recording small spontaneous depolarisations at the neuromuscular junction. Fatt and Katz found miniature end-plate potentials in the absence of deliberately evoked nerve impulses. The events resembled a much smaller version of the response produced by nerve stimulation. This suggested that release occurred in discrete packages. Later experiments by del Castillo and Katz reduced transmitter release probability and showed that evoked responses could fail or cluster around amplitudes corresponding to small integer multiples of a unitary event. The vesicular interpretation was reinforced by structural and physiological evidence.

A quantum is the effective package of transmitter contributing to one elementary postsynaptic response, commonly corresponding to release of a single vesicle. Quantal size is the mean response per elementary event; quantal content is the mean number of quanta released per presynaptic stimulus. It is useful to distinguish the number of transmitter molecules per vesicle from the postsynaptic voltage or current caused by those molecules. The latter also depends on the number and properties of available receptors, the postsynaptic driving force and recording location.

A simple binomial model assumes N independent release sites, each having a probability p of releasing one quantum during a stimulus, and a fixed mean quantal response q. Expected quantal content is Np and expected postsynaptic response is Npq if contributions add linearly. For an illustrative synapse with four available sites, release probability 0.25, and quantal current magnitude 10 pA, the mean current magnitude is 4 × 0.25 × 10 = 10 pA. The probability of zero released quanta is (1 − 0.25)^4, approximately 0.3164. Failure is therefore expected on about 32 percent of trials, although the stimulus and recording are identical.

Under the same model the number of released quanta has variance Np(1 − p). This variance is 4 × 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.75 quanta squared in the example. With constant q the response variance attributable to release is 75 pA squared. Real recordings additionally contain recording noise, differences in vesicular content, variability in receptor responses and possible correlations among release sites. The simple binomial model is a useful testable approximation rather than an exact description of all synapses.

If N is large and p small, release counts may be approximated by a Poisson distribution with mean m. The probability of failure is then exp(−m), giving m = −ln(P0). If 40 of 100 stimuli fail, the illustrative estimate is −ln(0.40) = 0.916 quanta per stimulus. This failure method is not reliable when failure is caused by failure of the axon to conduct, an undetected small response or receptor block rather than absence of release. It also relies on the distributional assumptions; it is not a universally valid way of determining vesicle numbers.

Modern central synaptic recordings can examine miniature postsynaptic currents with action potentials blocked by tetrodotoxin. A decrease in miniature frequency with unchanged amplitude is consistent with reduced spontaneous release or fewer functional contacts, but it does not uniquely prove a particular presynaptic molecular defect. A decrease in amplitude with unchanged frequency is consistent with altered postsynaptic sensitivity or reduced vesicular filling. Comparisons of evoked responses, paired-pulse behaviour and receptor responses to applied agonists help discriminate between possibilities. This distinction between a plausible inference and a logically unique explanation is particularly important in analytical examination questions.

Transmitters and receptor-dependent effects

Neurotransmitters are diverse. Small molecule transmitters include acetylcholine, amino acids such as glutamate, GABA and glycine, monoamines such as dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin and histamine, and purines such as ATP and adenosine. Peptide transmitters include many short polypeptides released from dense-core vesicles. A single neuron can release more than one signalling substance. Consequently, the older simplified picture of one transmitter per neuron cannot be applied as an absolute rule. Different release mechanisms and stimulation patterns may influence which substances are secreted.

The same chemical can act through receptor families with very different properties. Acetylcholine activates nicotinic ionotropic receptors and muscarinic metabotropic receptors. Glutamate commonly produces fast excitation through AMPA receptors and voltage-dependent signalling through NMDA receptors, while metabotropic glutamate receptors can modulate release and postsynaptic excitability. GABA can activate GABA-A receptors, which are chiefly permeable to chloride and also bicarbonate, and GABA-B receptors, which signal through G proteins. Glycine activates inhibitory anion channels at many mature spinal and brainstem synapses. It also acts as a coagonist at NMDA receptors, showing why transmitter names alone do not predict responses.

Ionotropic receptors connect transmitter binding directly to channel gating. Their fast current can influence voltage within milliseconds, making them suitable for precise timing. Metabotropic receptors can act through G-protein subunits or second messengers. For example, GABA-B receptor signalling can increase potassium conductance postsynaptically or inhibit voltage-gated calcium channels presynaptically. Metabotropic action is not necessarily slower in every measurable respect, but its effects commonly outlast the transient transmitter exposure and can alter the gain of other responses.

Receptor desensitisation means that a receptor can respond less during continued or repeated exposure to an agonist even when the agonist remains present. This mechanism differs from clearance of transmitter. It also differs from depletion of presynaptic vesicles, although all three can contribute to changes in an evoked current. To isolate postsynaptic desensitisation experimentally, one could compare responses to controlled repeated transmitter applications while bypassing presynaptic release. The timing and concentration of the test stimulus must be controlled, as a difference in the delivered stimulus can imitate a receptor defect.

Termination of signalling also differs among transmitters. Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses acetylcholine; the liberated choline may be taken up for reuse. Glutamate transporters in neurons and astrocytes remove glutamate from extracellular fluid and help prevent prolonged activation. GABA and monoamines can be cleared by specific uptake transporters. Peptides are often removed by diffusion and extracellular proteolysis. An uptake transporter does not function in the same way as the vesicular transporter that fills a secretory vesicle. One clears an extracellular space, the other prepares an intracellular release package.

Persistent transmitter exposure can prolong receptor activation, but the result depends on receptor kinetics, diffusion and desensitisation. Blocking reuptake need not cause a proportionate increase in every postsynaptic current. If most receptors already bind transmitter near the peak of release, the largest change may occur during decay rather than the peak. If clearance also normally prevents spillover, blockade may recruit receptors beyond the synaptic cleft. Thus peak amplitude, decay time and spatial range must be considered separately.

Reversal potentials, excitation and inhibition

The electrical consequence of opening a channel is governed by its conductance and reversal potential. With outward current defined as positive, a simplified synaptic current is Is = gs(Vm − Es), where Vm is the membrane potential and Es is the reversal potential of the receptor-mediated current. When Vm equals Es there is no net current through those channels, but the channels can remain open. The reversal potential of a channel permeable to one ion corresponds to that ion's equilibrium potential under the usual assumptions. A mixed-ion receptor has a reversal potential determined by its combined permeabilities and ionic gradients rather than by any single ion's equilibrium potential.

A mixed cation conductance with reversal potential near 0 mV carries inward net current at −65 mV. With gs = 1 nS, the outward-positive current is −65 pA. At +10 mV, the same conductance carries +10 pA. The current changes direction at the reversal potential. In an unclamped neuron, opening the conductance drives the voltage toward the reversal potential. In a voltage-clamped cell the experimenter instead supplies compensating current to keep the voltage at the command value, allowing the current-voltage relationship to be measured. Voltage clamp and current clamp therefore answer complementary questions.

An excitatory postsynaptic potential is a graded voltage response that increases the probability of spike initiation. An inhibitory postsynaptic response reduces that probability. In a simple neuron resting at −70 mV with a threshold near −50 mV, an anion conductance reversing at −75 mV tends to hyperpolarise the membrane. If it reverses at −60 mV instead, it can depolarise the resting neuron yet oppose excitation approaching threshold. Opening a conductance whose reversal lies below threshold can restrain voltage near that reversal and drain concurrent excitatory current. This is an important form of shunting inhibition.

The membrane can be modelled as a capacitance C in parallel with several conductances. At steady state and in the absence of injected current, the weighted average of reversal potentials determines the membrane voltage if all conductances are fixed and ohmic. Consider leak conductance 10 nS reversing at −70 mV and an excitatory conductance 5 nS reversing at 0 mV. The steady voltage is (10 × −70 + 5 × 0)/15 = −46.67 mV. Adding an inhibitory conductance of 15 nS reversing at −65 mV changes the voltage to (−700 − 975)/30 = −55.83 mV. The inhibitory conductance alone would move the resting voltage to −67 mV, a depolarisation. Nevertheless, when combined with excitation it prevents this idealised steady voltage from crossing a −50 mV threshold. The example assumes simultaneous constant conductances and ignores regenerative channels, so it is a prediction of passive voltage tendency, not an exact spike waveform.

Spatial summation occurs when multiple synapses at different positions influence the neuron over overlapping time intervals. Temporal summation occurs when successive responses overlap before earlier responses have decayed. In a simple passive compartment, after an instantaneous voltage increment, the response decays approximately exponentially with time constant tau = RmC. A 4 mV response with a 20 ms time constant leaves 4 exp(−10/20) = 2.43 mV after 10 ms. A second identical increment then gives approximately 6.43 mV. After 40 ms only 0.54 mV remains, and the second peak is about 4.54 mV. These calculations assume small responses and a constant membrane resistance; synaptic conductances themselves change resistance, limiting linear addition.

Dendritic geometry affects the extent to which a local synaptic potential reaches the axon initial segment. A distal input often attenuates more than a proximal input, but branch diameter, passive properties, active dendritic conductances and receptor distribution can alter this pattern. The axon initial segment usually possesses a high density of voltage-gated sodium channels, making it a common location for spike initiation. The soma is not a literal arithmetic adder. Its voltage reflects currents distributed across a complex electrical structure, and threshold itself is affected by recent activity. The simple summation model is a starting point for reasoning about integration, not a replacement for the biology.

Presynaptic regulation and plasticity

Synaptic strength can change during repeated use without changing the anatomical existence of the synapse. An axoaxonic connection can regulate the activity of another presynaptic terminal. In one common mechanism, metabotropic inhibitory receptors reduce calcium channel activity, decreasing release probability. The postsynaptic neuron then receives a smaller response despite an unchanged action potential in the signalling axon. Other presynaptic mechanisms include changes in potassium conductance and regulation of fusion machinery. A receptor activated by transmitter from the same neuron is an autoreceptor; one activated by a different signalling substance may be a heteroreceptor. These terms describe the source and identity of the regulating signal, not an invariant excitatory or inhibitory outcome.

Short-term facilitation describes increased synaptic efficacy during closely spaced stimuli. A residual calcium signal after the first impulse can increase the probability of release during the second. The second postsynaptic response may therefore be greater without an increase in the amplitude of either presynaptic action potential. Short-term depression describes a reduction in efficacy. Depletion of readily releasable vesicles, receptor desensitisation and other mechanisms can all contribute. At a synapse with high initial release probability, the first stimulus may consume much of the available pool, reducing release at a second stimulus before recovery. A low initial probability synapse can instead show facilitation. These tendencies are not absolute rules.

The paired-pulse ratio is often defined as the amplitude of the second evoked response divided by that of the first. Two currents with magnitudes 40 and 60 pA yield a ratio of 1.5; currents of 80 and 40 pA yield 0.5. The ratio is a descriptive measure, not direct measurement of release probability. Changing the stimulus interval, extracellular calcium or receptor properties can help test the mechanism. A smaller first response can make the ratio appear larger even when the second response is unchanged; inspection of both absolute amplitudes is essential.

Long-term potentiation and long-term depression describe persistent increases and decreases in synaptic efficacy after particular activity patterns. A classical example of potentiation occurs at many hippocampal CA3-to-CA1 glutamatergic synapses. AMPA-mediated depolarisation relieves the voltage-dependent magnesium block of NMDA receptors while glutamate and a coagonist are bound. Calcium entry can activate intracellular mechanisms that increase the efficacy of AMPA-mediated responses, including increased receptor availability. Some forms of depression involve different calcium signals and phosphatase-dependent changes that reduce AMPA receptor availability. The calcium pattern, receptor composition and synapse type determine the outcome. Not all LTP or LTD requires NMDA receptors, nor do these descriptions identify a complete mechanism of memory.

A conceptual experiment stimulates two independent input pathways to one hippocampal region. Record baseline responses, apply a conditioning pattern to one pathway, and continue testing both pathways with the original low-frequency stimuli. A persistent increase restricted to the conditioned input supports input-specific potentiation. The control pathway helps distinguish a synaptic change from a general change in recording quality or cellular excitability. Test whether an NMDA receptor antagonist applied during induction prevents the increase. This can support receptor involvement in induction but does not show that the receptor alone stores the persistent change. Distinguishing induction, expression and maintenance clarifies experimental interpretations.

A synapse is therefore best understood as a dynamic link whose effect depends on the history of activity as well as on present voltage and transmitter exposure. The same incoming spike train can be transformed differently by facilitating and depressing synapses. The cellular rules developed here provide the foundation for studying the organised pathways of the brain and spinal cord.
