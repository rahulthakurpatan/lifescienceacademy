1. Why defense must extend beyond the site of attack

A leaf infected by a pathogen is not an isolated biological system. Its photosynthetic cells share water, mineral nutrients and carbon compounds with many other organs. Its vascular connections also provide routes through which information about local conditions can affect cells that have not yet encountered the pathogen. An infection in an older leaf may therefore change the defensive capacity of young leaves, even when the pathogen itself remains confined to the original site. This ability to coordinate responses across space is one of the central features of plant stress physiology. Plants do not need circulating antibodies or mobile populations of specialized lymphocytes to achieve such coordination. Instead, their existing cells alter signaling, metabolism, gene expression and defensive capacity in response to locally produced and remotely received information.

There are good reasons not to express every defense at its highest possible level all the time. A plant invests assimilated carbon in growing roots, expanding leaves, reproductive structures and storage products. It uses nitrogen for enzymes, structural proteins and many protective compounds. Induced defense may consume these resources, change the balance of hormones, constrain expansion or accelerate the replacement of damaged components. Some antimicrobial compounds would be harmful to the cells that produce them if accumulation were uncontrolled. A successful defensive strategy must therefore provide enough protection when required without permanently converting the entire plant into a defensive tissue. Local activation and systemic preparation provide complementary solutions to this problem.

The word local refers to the tissue directly experiencing the initiating stimulus. In a leaf infection experiment, the local leaf is the leaf originally inoculated with a microorganism. Systemic or distal tissue refers to another part of the same plant that is not directly treated by that initiating procedure. An experimentally untreated leaf is not necessarily physiologically unaffected: it can receive chemical messengers or signals generated in intervening tissues. This distinction matters when reading graphs. If a reporter increases in the infected leaf, that finding demonstrates a local response, not by itself a systemic response. If the reporter increases in a distant leaf, the finding establishes a change in that leaf, but resistance must still be demonstrated by an appropriate challenge.

Systemic acquired resistance, abbreviated SAR, describes an inducible defensive state that develops in distant tissues after a localized infection or related immune stimulus. The later challenge can be caused by a pathogen different from the initial inducing organism, within the range of protection provided by the particular plant and response. The expression broad spectrum does not mean that every pathogen and herbivore is inhibited. Rather, it emphasizes that protection need not depend on the initial and subsequent attackers presenting the same particular antigen. A plant may show reduced bacterial multiplication but remain sensitive to certain necrotrophs, viruses or herbivores. Host genotype and the nature of the challenge determine which defensive outputs are useful.

Induced systemic resistance, or ISR, is commonly used more narrowly for systemic defensive changes elicited by beneficial microorganisms associated with the roots. Many rhizobacteria and fungi can influence shoot defense without infecting the shoot. In some model cases the root treatment prepares the shoot to activate defenses more effectively during subsequent attack without substantially increasing classical pathogenesis-related gene expression beforehand. The organisms and signaling pathways responsible vary, so the distinction between SAR and ISR is most useful when it identifies the inducing stimulus and the experimentally established mechanism. It is not an absolute molecular division between two entirely noninteracting pathways.

The word acquired in SAR may cause confusion for students familiar with vertebrate adaptive immunity. A plant can enter an acquired state of enhanced defense because of prior exposure, while continuing to rely on innate immune recognition and genetically encoded cellular responses. No rearrangement of immunoglobulin genes, production of antibodies or clonal expansion of antigen-specific lymphocytes is needed. A systemic change in a plant is better understood as an altered readiness of tissues than as the plant version of immunological memory in a vertebrate. Some processes are comparable at the broad functional level of benefiting from prior experience, but their underlying cell types and molecular mechanisms are different.

The benefit of preparing distant tissue is greatest when the first attack provides useful information about future risk. A pathogen confined to one leaf may later reach another leaf by rain splash, vectors or contact. Defensive preparation of neighboring or vascularly connected tissues can reduce subsequent damage. Yet if a first stimulus is unrelated to the next threat, protection may be small or even negative. The plant cannot predict future events perfectly; it modifies its physiological state according to available signals. That state may also change as the plant grows, receives new environmental information or recovers from the initial attack.

It is necessary to distinguish resistance from tolerance when evaluating the value of such changes. Resistance reduces the success of an attacker, for example by restricting pathogen replication or reducing herbivore feeding. Tolerance reduces the adverse effect of a given amount of attacker damage on plant performance. A plant can maintain biomass despite carrying as many bacteria as a susceptible plant if it compensates effectively, but it has not demonstrated improved resistance by that observation alone. Conversely, an infected plant may strongly restrict pathogen growth at considerable reproductive cost. Measuring microbial population size and plant yield together is therefore more informative than treating either outcome as a complete account of stress adaptation.

2. The experimental foundations of systemic acquired resistance

A convincing demonstration of systemic protection requires two distinct events. First, an inducing treatment is applied to one part of the plant. Second, a subsequent challenge is applied to a previously untreated part, after an interval appropriate for systemic responses to develop. A control group receives a comparable initial procedure without the active inducer and receives the same challenge at the same time. If the initially induced plants restrict the second pathogen more effectively, the experiment supports the existence of an induced systemic defensive state. The treatment interval, leaf age, genotype, environmental conditions and method of measuring infection must be matched between groups.

Classical observations in tobacco helped establish the concept. Ross studied the consequences of localized tobacco mosaic virus infection in a resistant tobacco cultivar. Later inoculation of distant leaves produced a different disease response in plants that had experienced the first infection than in control plants. The use of separated sites was crucial: reduced lesions in the second leaf could not simply be explained by the physical barrier formed around a lesion in the first leaf. The original observations stimulated research into the chemical and physiological communication linking infected and healthy tissues. They did not, by themselves, identify the molecular signal or prove that every plant would respond to the same stimulus.

In tobacco mosaic virus systems, local necrotic lesions may occur as a consequence of resistance responses. Smaller lesions in a later challenge are useful evidence of altered disease response, but lesion size should not always be treated as a direct measure of viral abundance. Lesions can reflect the host's own cell death as well as pathogen activity. For bacteria, viable population counts give a more direct measure of multiplication; for a fungus or oomycete, pathogen biomass, spore production and lesion development may be combined. The best output is one suited to the biology of the attacker and independent enough of host damage to distinguish the processes being measured.

The distinction between inoculation and infection is also important. Inoculation is the experimental placement of a pathogen onto or into a plant tissue; infection is the biological establishment and development of the organism in that tissue. The initial inoculation is intended to activate a local immune response, but the distal challenge must be administered separately. If the first pathogen has spread throughout the plant before the challenge, it may be impossible to distinguish communication from ongoing infection. Researchers can use strains that remain localized under the chosen conditions, test for the presence of the inducer in distant tissue, or use a nonliving elicitor where that is biologically justified. Detection limits should be reported; failure to detect the organism is not a mathematical proof of absolute absence.

A mock-treated plant is more informative than a completely untouched plant when the induction method itself influences defense. Infiltration of a leaf with a liquid can change cellular hydration and pressure. Mechanical damage can activate wound responses and release damage-associated molecular patterns. A carrier compound may itself influence the tissue. The mock treatment therefore reproduces the relevant handling and carrier conditions without the active microorganism or chemical. If the active inducer still confers additional protection relative to the mock, the difference is less likely to arise solely from the treatment procedure. Including untouched controls as well may help estimate the consequences of the mock intervention.

Timing creates another experimental challenge. Directly after the initiating infection, signaling may be present before measurable protection has developed. A later stage may show both expression of protective genes and changes in the ability to respond to the challenge. At still later times the systemic state may decline. Consequently, a negative result at one sampling time does not exclude SAR under all conditions. A useful time course includes samples before induction, at relevant intervals after induction but before challenge, and after challenge. Measuring both molecular markers and the disease outcome reveals whether a particular marker changes before protection, accompanies protection or merely reflects the later pathogen burden.

The term systemic should not be interpreted as spatial uniformity. Leaves occupying different positions on a plant may have different vascular relationships to the inducing leaf. The movement of assimilates follows source and sink relations rather than an arbitrary equal distribution to every organ. Young expanding tissues are often sinks, whereas mature illuminated leaves can be sources. Different vascular connections can lead to different exposure to signals. A systemic phenotype may therefore be stronger in certain distal organs than in others, even when the genotype is identical. Spatially explicit sampling makes it possible to investigate this variation instead of hiding it in an average prepared from all leaves.

A second challenge is not mandatory to show every biochemical feature associated with systemic signaling, but it is indispensable when the conclusion concerns acquired resistance itself. For example, measurement of salicylic acid in an uninoculated leaf may show that a hormone has accumulated there. Increased abundance of a pathogenesis-related protein may support activation of defense gene expression. Neither observation establishes how much protection the leaf will show against a pathogen. The disease endpoint is particularly important for mutants: a mutant can fail to express one marker but still restrict some pathogens through alternative mechanisms, or can express the marker without generating effective downstream protection.

The idea of a signal moving through the plant led to a distinction between signal production and signal response. A donor region must detect the first attack and generate information. A recipient region must receive and interpret that information. If a mutant fails to develop SAR, the defect could be at either end of the process, or in transmission between them. Whole-plant mutant experiments establish a requirement somewhere in the response but cannot usually locate the required activity. Tissue-specific restoration, grafting and localized treatments can separate these alternatives. This is the logic behind many important experiments on salicylic acid and other systemic mediators.

3. Salicylic acid, NPR1 and defense gene expression

Salicylic acid, abbreviated SA, is a phenolic plant signaling molecule with important functions in resistance to many biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens. Its importance in systemic acquired resistance became clear when researchers observed that changes in SA accumulation accompanied immune activation and that disrupting SA accumulation or signaling impaired acquired protection. These observations led to two distinct questions. Is SA needed to establish resistance in a tissue? Is SA itself the long-distance signal that conveys the initial message? The first question concerns local biochemical function, whereas the second concerns interorgan communication. Their answers need not be identical.

In Arabidopsis thaliana, much of the pathogen-induced SA is synthesized through the isochorismate pathway. Chorismate, an intermediate of aromatic metabolism, is converted to isochorismate by ISOCHORISMATE SYNTHASE1, commonly called ICS1 or SID2. This step occurs in the plastid. Isochorismate is exported through the chloroplast envelope with the participation of EDS5, and subsequent cytosolic reactions involving PBS3 facilitate the production of SA from isochorismate-derived intermediates. The overall sequence is a useful illustration of cooperation between cellular compartments. A mutation preventing export can limit the final product even though the first enzyme remains present. Similarly, a defect in a downstream conversion can reduce SA without directly disrupting the synthesis of chorismate.

Another route to SA involves phenylalanine metabolism. Its quantitative contribution varies across plant species and conditions. For examination reasoning, the key distinction is that a major route in pathogen-challenged Arabidopsis uses isochorismate, but it is incorrect to conclude that every plant makes all of its SA exclusively by that route. The metabolic location of an enzyme, its expression pattern and available precursor pools determine the flux through a pathway. The concentration of SA measured at a particular time also reflects conjugation, degradation, transport and dilution, not only the rate of production.

SA accumulation has to be controlled. Free SA can participate in signaling, whereas glucosylated forms may contribute to storage or inactivation depending on the compound and location. Conversion to other derivatives can change volatility and mobility. For example, methyl salicylate is a volatile derivative with signaling relevance in certain systems. A measurement reporting total SA after hydrolysis may include conjugated material, while a measurement of free SA reports a different biochemical pool. These assays should not be interpreted as interchangeable. If a mutant accumulates large amounts of conjugated SA but has low free SA, the total amount alone may overstate its immediate signaling capacity.

A central regulator downstream of SA is NONEXPRESSER OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES1, NPR1. NPR1 is an SA-responsive regulatory protein that functions in the control of transcription, including through interactions with TGA family transcription factors. TGA factors bind specific DNA regulatory elements; NPR1 does not simply act as a conventional DNA-binding transcription factor that recognizes every defense promoter on its own. In the nucleus, the interaction of NPR1 with transcriptional regulators can facilitate activation of defense genes. NPR1 activity and abundance are regulated at several levels, including its conformation, interactions, localization and turnover. The examination-level causal relationship is that SA signaling through NPR1 helps control defense transcription and systemic resistance, rather than that NPR1 is the enzyme that synthesizes SA.

Older teaching diagrams often represent NPR1 as an inactive cytoplasmic oligomer that becomes monomeric after an SA-dependent redox change and then enters the nucleus. This model helped connect redox status to immune transcription, but the precise structural and regulatory states of NPR1 are more complex than a single binary switch. A student should understand the role of SA-responsive NPR1, nuclear transcriptional regulation and redox-sensitive processes without treating the simplified oligomer-to-monomer diagram as a complete structural account of all physiological NPR1 activity. Changes in protein accumulation and transcription may reflect several regulatory steps at once.

Pathogenesis-related proteins, abbreviated PR proteins, are proteins associated with plant responses to infection and other immune stimuli. PR1 is widely used as an SA-associated transcriptional marker in model plants. Other PR protein families include enzymes such as certain glucanases and chitinases that can contribute to defense against particular organisms. It is important to avoid attributing a universal direct antimicrobial mechanism to every PR family. PR1 transcript abundance, PR1 protein abundance and pathogen restriction are separate measurements. A large change in messenger RNA may precede protein accumulation, and an increase in a single protein may not be sufficient to resist a particular pathogen.

Genetic comparisons can establish the order of pathway components. Suppose a mutant impaired in SA biosynthesis fails to activate a defense marker after infection. Supplying SA externally may restore marker expression if the downstream regulatory machinery is functional and the molecule reaches the relevant tissue. If an NPR1-defective plant accumulates SA but still fails to activate the marker appropriately, additional SA need not rescue the defect because the response machinery is compromised. The comparison supports a biosynthetic step upstream of a signaling step. It does not imply that every effect of SA is NPR1 dependent, nor that a single marker captures the entirety of immunity.

A complementation experiment strengthens such an interpretation. Restoring a functional copy of the defective gene in the corresponding mutant can restore the missing phenotype, provided the gene is expressed in a relevant pattern and the comparison is otherwise controlled. This is different from chemical rescue. Chemical rescue supplies a product or stimulus to bypass a biochemical lesion; genetic complementation restores the gene's function. If SA treatment rescues a biosynthetic mutant, it is consistent with a shortage of the product being important. If a functional NPR1 transgene rescues an NPR1 mutant, it supports attribution of the phenotype to loss of that gene. Neither experiment alone proves direct physical binding between any two components.

The dependence of systemic resistance on SA was investigated using tobacco plants expressing the bacterial salicylate hydroxylase gene nahG, which reduces SA accumulation by converting SA to catechol. Grafting experiments compared the capacities of SA-depleted and normal tissues to generate and respond to systemic signals. The findings reported by Vernooij and colleagues in 1994 supported the requirement for SA in systemic signal transduction while arguing against SA being the necessary translocated signal in that experimental system. This was a major conceptual advance: a molecule can be indispensable in the receiving tissue without being the principal transported molecule that carried information from the donor tissue. The nahG manipulation also produces catechol, so conclusions about loss of SA are best supported by independent genetic and biochemical approaches.

The later identification of additional mobile or systemically accumulating metabolites has expanded this picture rather than eliminating the importance of SA. In Arabidopsis, the strong expression of systemic immunity involves cooperation between SA and N-hydroxypipecolic acid. SA remains an important regulator of defense transcription and protection in recipient leaves. The next section considers how an amino acid-derived metabolite participates in the generation and amplification of this systemic state. The conceptual lesson is that a long-distance signal, a hormone required in the receiving tissue and a transcriptional regulator are different roles, even though the same molecule may perform more than one role in some circumstances.

4. Pipecolic acid and N-hydroxypipecolic acid

The discovery of an important amino acid-derived component of systemic resistance illustrates how physiological observations, mutant analysis and biochemistry can converge. A plant produces many low molecular weight compounds after pathogen recognition. Some are metabolic by-products of the response, some directly inhibit pathogens, and others regulate the behavior of plant cells. Measuring accumulation is a useful starting point but does not distinguish these roles. A candidate signaling metabolite becomes especially interesting if its abundance changes in distant tissue, if the plant loses systemic resistance when production of the compound is disrupted, and if supplying the compound under controlled conditions restores resistance. Together these observations connect chemical identity with physiological function.

Pipecolic acid, often abbreviated Pip, is a cyclic amino acid derivative of lysine. In Arabidopsis the pathogen-inducible route toward Pip involves the aminotransferase ALD1 and the reductase SARD4. The intermediate reactions convert lysine into a cyclic precursor and then into pipecolic acid. A further oxidation of Pip by the flavin-dependent monooxygenase FMO1 generates N-hydroxypipecolic acid, abbreviated NHP. The hydroxylated nitrogen distinguishes NHP chemically from its precursor. Learning the sequence as lysine, ALD1 and SARD4, Pip, FMO1, NHP helps students predict which compounds might accumulate when a particular biosynthetic step fails. It is not necessary to memorize the detailed chemical structures of every intermediate to understand the genetic logic.

Loss of ALD1 can restrict pathogen-induced Pip accumulation and thereby limit production of the downstream compound. Loss of FMO1 can prevent efficient conversion of Pip into NHP even if the plant is capable of producing Pip. The two mutations have different biochemical consequences but may both impair the establishment of systemic resistance. This is a standard feature of sequential metabolic pathways: the physiological phenotype can be similar for different blocks because both reduce the final active product. The metabolite profile helps distinguish the position of each block. Failure to detect the final product alone is less informative about the exact affected step than measuring both the precursor and the final product.

The FMO1 reaction became a particularly important focus of research because FMO1 was already implicated genetically in systemic resistance. Independent studies published in 2018 by Hartmann and colleagues and by Chen and colleagues established a major role for NHP in Arabidopsis systemic immunity. The work connected the genetic requirement for FMO1 with its biochemical ability to produce NHP from Pip, and showed that supplied NHP could induce acquired resistance and restore systemic defense in plants impaired in NHP production. These results clarified why supplying Pip alone would not necessarily restore full resistance in a plant unable to convert it into NHP. Chemical rescue was especially informative because the administered product bypassed the defective enzyme.

In a simple teaching experiment, consider four genotypes and treatment groups: untreated wild type, a wild type given a candidate metabolite, an fmo1 mutant given Pip, and an fmo1 mutant given NHP. All groups are later challenged under comparable conditions. If Pip stimulates resistance in wild type but not in the mutant, while NHP restores protection in the mutant, the pattern is consistent with a required conversion of Pip into NHP. The inference depends on comparable delivery and physiological exposure. It would be weaker if the Pip treatment were unable to reach the relevant cells in the mutant for an unrelated reason. Measuring metabolite levels after application helps address that alternative. A mock-treated mutant and a mock-treated wild type should also be included to establish the baseline phenotype.

Exogenous rescue demonstrates that a compound can bypass a particular deficiency under the conditions of an experiment. It does not establish that the applied concentration duplicates the native concentration, or that the supplied molecule travels by precisely the same route as an endogenously produced molecule. A high dose could activate an alternative response not normally dominant. That is why evidence from biochemical enzyme assays, endogenous metabolite measurements and genetic restoration is valuable in conjunction with rescue. Each type of observation constrains a different aspect of the causal explanation. Biochemistry identifies the possible reaction; genetics establishes functional requirements; physiological challenge tests whether the reaction matters for protection.

NHP acts in concert with SA rather than simply replacing the SA pathway. Following local pathogen attack, NHP and SA can accumulate in both local and distal leaves. NHP contributes to immune reprogramming and can stimulate processes associated with SA production; SA-dependent signaling in turn contributes strongly to the expression of many NHP-induced defensive outputs. The relationship therefore includes feedback and amplification rather than a one-directional chain with a single terminal product. A plant defective in NPR1 may have a serious defect in NHP-induced transcriptional responses even if NHP is present. This makes NPR1 useful as a downstream regulatory requirement in experiments where NHP production and transport are intact.

An amplification circuit allows a modest initiating signal to produce a larger response at a distant site. Suppose a small amount of NHP reaching a recipient leaf stimulates processes that promote further defensive metabolism in that leaf. Newly generated molecules can influence adjacent cells and reinforce the state. The total amount measured later may then be much larger than the quantity directly delivered from the infected organ. This means that the amount of a compound detected in distal tissue cannot automatically be equated with the amount transported there. The distinction is analogous to a signal in a kinase cascade: the output can be much greater than the initiating input because existing cellular machinery responds and amplifies it. The analogy describes amplification, not the chemical mechanism of transport.

Negative control is as important as amplification. NHP can be converted into conjugated forms, including glucosylated derivatives, which helps regulate active metabolite pools. Excessive or poorly controlled immune signaling can impose costs even when a pathogen is absent. Regulation of synthesis, conjugation and response therefore contributes to the appropriate duration of systemic resistance. A metabolite detected after extraction may represent an active free form, a stored derivative or a mixture depending on the analytical method. When an exam describes total NHP-related metabolites, the student should check whether the measurement distinguishes free NHP from derivatives before interpreting its signaling implications.

NHP biosynthesis and immune functions have been investigated in both monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants, but Arabidopsis provides the best-known genetic framework for this chapter. The presence of a homologous enzyme or a detectable metabolite in a different species does not prove that the entire signaling network is identical. Physiological relevance should be established in the species and stress system concerned. This limitation is useful for crop applications: the model pathway provides testable predictions, not a guarantee that a treatment effective in a laboratory Arabidopsis assay will have the same benefit in rice, wheat or a fruit tree.

5. Distal signals, vascular routes and evidence for mobility

A systemic signal may travel as a chemical substance, as a physical change propagated through interconnected tissues, or as a sequence of locally regenerated signals. These possibilities should not be conflated. Movement of a molecule from one organ to another is a transport process. A wave of calcium elevation can spread because calcium-sensitive events in one cell induce changes in neighboring cells; it does not require the same calcium ions to move from a leaf all the way to a root. A hydraulic change is transmitted through the physical state of a water-conducting system. A plant can use several of these forms of communication together, and the molecular mechanism required for a rapid wave may differ from the mechanism maintaining resistance days later.

Vascular tissues provide obvious anatomical connections. Xylem transports water and dissolved substances, with bulk flow often directed from roots toward transpiring shoots. Phloem distributes assimilates from sources to sinks, and its transport direction depends on the source and sink relationship of particular connected regions. Neither tissue should be described as an unrestricted pipe through which every signaling compound moves equally. Loading into a vascular pathway, stability during transport, movement out of the pathway, metabolism and tissue connectivity can all influence the distribution of a messenger. An understanding of the pressure-flow model and source–sink relations from subunit 6.F makes spatial patterns of systemic signaling easier to interpret.

The observation that a compound is present in phloem-associated samples is consistent with transport, but is not decisive by itself. Sampling wounded vascular tissue may release substances from surrounding cells or alter composition. A molecule may be present in transport fluid without contributing significantly to the defensive response. Conversely, a signal effective at low concentration may be difficult to detect. A stronger argument links movement to physiological activity. For instance, a labeled compound applied locally can be detected in a distant tissue in its chemically identified form, and the tissue can subsequently display enhanced protection. Such experiments establish more than a correlation between local and distal concentrations.

The studies of NHP include evidence that locally supplied NHP can move systemically and elicit protection in Arabidopsis. This finding supports a role for NHP as a mobile immune mediator. It does not require all NHP found in distal tissue to originate in the first infected leaf. Local synthesis in the recipient tissue may contribute to amplification. Nor does it establish NHP as the only systemic mediator in every host and pathogen combination. Several compounds have been implicated in SAR-related communication, including methyl salicylate in particular species and contexts, and lipid-related mediators. The most defensible examination conclusion is that systemic immunity uses interacting signals, with NHP and SA having central roles in well-studied model systems.

Methyl salicylate illustrates a distinction between chemical identity and physiological activity. Modification of SA can produce a compound with different transport properties, and conversion back to SA in another tissue can alter the local signaling pool. This is conceptually similar to transporting a precursor and generating an active product near the site of action. The mechanism should not be generalized to every plant from one demonstration in tobacco. A study showing that a particular enzyme required for methyl salicylate conversion affects systemic resistance in one system establishes a requirement under those conditions. The strength of evidence must be considered separately from the attractiveness of the proposed model.

Grafting makes it possible to distinguish donor and recipient functions genetically. A graft joins living tissues from different plants so that vascular connections can develop. When the relevant organs are arranged and treated appropriately, a stimulus generated in one genotype can be tested for its effect in tissue of another genotype. If a mutant donor fails to activate resistance in a normal recipient, the missing activity could be required for generating or transmitting the signal. If a normal donor fails to activate resistance in a mutant recipient, a defect in interpreting the signal becomes more likely. Reciprocal grafts, normal-to-normal controls and mutant-to-mutant controls are needed to distinguish these patterns. The graft itself must be functional, and differences in development or transport capacity must be considered.

A donor mutation can also affect the intensity of the primary infection. For example, if the mutant responds weakly to the first inoculum or develops much more injury, it may generate a different amount of several signals rather than simply lack a particular mobile molecule. Comparing only the distal resistance phenotype would therefore be insufficient to identify the specific transported compound. Measuring local immune activation, pathogen populations and candidate metabolite levels can reveal whether the initial stimulus was comparable. A donor defect in signal production is best established when the candidate molecule is specifically missing, general communication is preserved and restoring the candidate or the relevant biosynthetic activity restores distal protection.

Tissue-specific expression is another way to examine where a gene is required. Consider a gene encoding a biosynthetic enzyme that is absent throughout a mutant plant. Expression of the gene only in the inducing tissue might restore generation of a mobile signal, whereas expression only in the distal tissue might restore local amplification or a required receiving response. The outcome depends on how strictly expression is confined to the intended tissue, whether enzyme products move and whether the promoter is active at the relevant time. A positive result shows that expression in a region can be sufficient under the chosen conditions; a negative result may reflect insufficient expression or an incomplete restoration rather than an absolute impossibility.

Rapid responses to wounding, high light and other stresses can also involve electrical changes, calcium signals and reactive oxygen species. Changes in membrane potential can influence the activation of channels in connected cells, and ROS produced by membrane-associated enzymes can contribute to cell-to-cell signaling. These processes can precede transcriptional and metabolic adjustment. Systemic wound responses involving jasmonates provide a useful comparison: information about tissue damage can arrive before distal cells have accumulated the full complement of defensive products. It would be an error to assume that a rapid calcium response is itself the complete SAR phenotype. The latter involves a later demonstrable change in disease resistance.

A time-resolved study can separate these forms of information. Early measurements might track membrane electrical changes or cytosolic calcium in distant tissue. Later measurements might quantify NHP, SA, defense transcripts and subsequent pathogen restriction. A mutation impairing a rapid response might influence later resistance, but proving mediation requires more than matching timing. If the early signal is absent and the later phenotype is unchanged, that early component is not indispensable for the measured outcome under those conditions. If both disappear, the mutation may disrupt a common upstream activity. The most informative experiments perturb the candidate step while preserving other aspects of initial perception and test whether downstream outputs can be rescued.

Finally, systemic communication is not limited to the interior of an individual plant. Volatile compounds released after damage can alter responses in other tissues or nearby plants in some situations. The chapter on herbivory introduced these ecological signals. Intra-plant transport and airborne communication are different mechanisms, even when the same treatment influences both. Enclosing treated tissue or separating the airspaces of different organs can help distinguish volatile-mediated effects from internal transmission, provided those manipulations do not unintentionally change temperature, humidity or light. The broader lesson is to identify the route rather than infer it merely from the distance between stimulus and response.

6. Induced systemic resistance through beneficial root microorganisms

Roots encounter microbial communities throughout their development. The rhizosphere is the region of soil influenced by roots, particularly by compounds released from them and by changes in nutrient availability. Some bacteria and fungi associated with roots can improve plant performance by affecting mineral acquisition, competing with pathogens or altering host physiological responses. Induced systemic resistance refers here to the enhancement of defensive capacity in tissues distant from beneficial root-associated microorganisms. Its defining experimental feature is that a root treatment influences the response of another organ to a later challenge. A reduction of disease limited to the zone occupied by the beneficial microbe is not sufficient to demonstrate systemic host-mediated resistance.

Beneficial is a functional description, not an indication that every strain belonging to a named genus produces the same phenotype. Some strains of Pseudomonas and Bacillus, and selected fungi including mycorrhizal partners, have been investigated as inducers of systemic resistance. The effectiveness of an association depends on the strain, plant genotype, soil properties, environmental conditions, the composition of the surrounding microbiota and the identity of the later attacker. A microbe that improves nutrient uptake can also change the plant's growth independently of defense. A useful comparison must distinguish better general nutrition from enhanced pathogen restriction and from direct antimicrobial activity of the microbe.

A model ISR experiment begins with inoculation or colonization of roots by a selected beneficial bacterium. Control plants receive a comparable treatment without the bacterium. The shoots are later challenged with a pathogen under matched conditions. If treated plants show less pathogen multiplication in leaves where the inducing bacterium is absent, the observation supports systemic protection. To determine whether the protection is mediated by the plant rather than a transported antibiotic, investigators can use plant mutants defective in candidate signaling pathways, assess the presence of the beneficial microorganism in the challenged tissue and compare the effect of the microorganism directly on the pathogen outside the plant. None of these tests alone excludes all indirect effects, but together they clarify the mechanism.

Many widely taught examples of rhizobacteria-induced ISR in Arabidopsis depend on jasmonate and ethylene responsiveness. Jasmonates participate in wound, insect and necrotroph-related responses, while ethylene interacts with several defensive and developmental pathways. NPR1 can also be required for certain forms of ISR, despite the fact that these examples need not show the same SA accumulation and constitutive PR gene activation characteristic of classical SAR. A protein reused in different pathways need not have identical upstream requirements in each pathway. The distinction between NPR1-dependent signaling and SA-dependent signaling is therefore important. If a particular ISR phenotype is lost in an npr1 mutant, it is not automatically evidence that the treatment induced the same SA-centered mechanism as SAR.

The distinction has experimental value. Compare normal plants with mutants defective in jasmonate perception, ethylene signaling or SA synthesis. Treat roots with the selected beneficial bacterium and challenge distal leaves. If a strain-induced protective effect is absent in the jasmonate- and ethylene-response mutants but retained in an SA biosynthetic mutant, that result supports dependence on jasmonate and ethylene signaling under the tested conditions. It does not establish that these hormones themselves traveled from the roots to the leaves. The loss of a hormone receptor in all tissues also does not indicate whether the receptor is essential in roots, shoots or both. Tissue-specific comparisons would be needed to localize its function.

The term often used to describe beneficial microbe-associated ISR is priming. A primed plant does not necessarily produce large amounts of defense proteins before the challenge. Instead, the subsequent attack elicits a more rapid, stronger or differently coordinated response than it does in an unprimed plant. Root recognition and downstream signaling can modify the capacity of shoot cells to respond. For instance, a leaf challenged after a root treatment might deposit callose faster or induce an antimicrobial pathway earlier. The early advantage can matter even if the final level of a transcript becomes similar in treated and untreated leaves. A pathogen that grows exponentially during the first phase of colonization may be restricted much more effectively when defense is activated a few hours earlier.

Some forms of microbial ISR involve SA-related mechanisms, and some microbes produce multiple effects that interact with plant hormones. Thus a strict rule that all SAR is exclusively SA driven and all ISR is exclusively independent of SA is too strong. The classical distinction is a helpful model: localized pathogen-induced systemic immunity commonly involves NHP, SA and NPR1, while many beneficial root microbe-induced responses involve JA and ethylene-dependent priming. Exceptions should be evaluated in terms of actual evidence rather than forced into the model. In an examination, a question may provide the mutant phenotype explicitly; the student should interpret those results rather than assume the answer solely from the label ISR.

Root-associated microbes can affect plant iron status and induce changes in root gene expression. Work reviewed by Pieterse and colleagues identifies the transcription factor MYB72 as important in the onset of ISR in particular model interactions. A root-specific requirement is conceptually useful because it illustrates the initiating side of the signaling network. It does not imply that MYB72 must travel to the shoot as the mobile signal. A transcription factor can regulate local metabolism and thereby modify production of other signals. Similarly, detection of a hormone-responsive transcript in a leaf does not mean that the corresponding hormone moved intact from the root. The role of local induction must be distinguished from the identity of the transmitted information.

Direct biological control and systemic resistance can coexist. A root-associated bacterium may compete with a soil-borne pathogen for space or nutrients, release compounds that inhibit its growth and stimulate the plant's own immune responsiveness. If the beneficial and harmful microorganisms occupy the same site, reduced disease may result from any combination of these processes. A separated-site challenge, including a shoot challenge or an appropriately designed split-root experiment, helps determine whether the plant-mediated component exists. Even then, the two mechanisms may contribute differently to disease outcomes in the field, so demonstrating one should not lead to an assumption that the other is absent.

Agricultural interest in ISR comes from its potential to reduce disease pressure and improve crop performance. However, a laboratory demonstration under controlled conditions is not a field guarantee. Introduced microbes must establish themselves sufficiently in the relevant root environment, compete with native organisms and remain functionally active as soil moisture and nutrient supply vary. The benefit also depends on which pests or pathogens are present and on how much the plant invests in responding. Evaluating crop yield, pathogen burden and treatment consistency across environments is necessary before judging practical effectiveness. This is a physiological and ecological problem, not just a matter of adding a microbe with a favorable label.

7. Defense priming as a change in response capacity

Direct induction and priming can occur together but are not identical. Direct induction occurs when a stimulus activates defense outputs before any later challenge. Priming refers to a change that enables a plant to respond differently when a subsequent challenge occurs. A priming stimulus can cause measurable molecular alterations during the preparatory period, so priming does not mean that nothing happens before attack. What distinguishes it experimentally is the relationship between the pretreated state and the later induced response. A plant that already has high antimicrobial compound levels may be protected without displaying a larger challenge-induced response. Another plant may have nearly normal basal levels yet activate the compound much faster after infection.

The appropriate comparison therefore has four groups. A mock-pretreated unchallenged group gives the baseline. A pretreated unchallenged group reveals changes caused by the initial stimulus alone. A mock-pretreated challenged group shows the ordinary response to the later stress. A pretreated challenged group reveals the response of a plant that experienced both events. The difference between the last two groups is informative, but interpreting it as priming requires knowledge of the two unchallenged groups. If pretreatment alone already increased a marker by the same amount, a larger final marker level after challenge might reflect a persistent baseline difference rather than enhanced inducibility.

Suppose an illustrative defense reporter has values of 1, 2, 5 and 12 arbitrary fluorescence units in these four groups at a chosen time. The challenge-associated increase above baseline is four units without pretreatment and ten units with pretreatment. The difference between these increments is six units, supporting enhanced responsiveness on the chosen additive scale. The value of 12 alone would not have been enough to establish this conclusion. If the pretreated baseline had instead been eight units, an increase to 12 would equal the four-unit increment in untreated plants, and the evidence for extra inducibility would be weaker. The experiment should also evaluate statistical variability and reporter behavior rather than treating hypothetical mean values as definitive evidence.

The time dimension can be more important than the final amplitude. An unprimed leaf might require a long period to accumulate the enzymes needed for a defense pathway, whereas a primed leaf begins with some regulatory machinery or accessible chromatin already in place. When a pathogen enters, the primed leaf can activate a useful response earlier. If both leaves reach the same marker level by the end of a day, a single late measurement would obscure the early difference. A time course can identify a shorter response latency, a steeper initial rise, a higher peak or a prolonged response. These are distinct properties and may have different effects on pathogen population dynamics.

A plausible mechanism of priming is the persistence of increased abundance of inactive or readily activatable signaling proteins. If a kinase is already synthesized but not strongly active, the next stimulus can rapidly activate it without waiting for transcription and translation. Other mechanisms include changes in the availability of metabolites, altered regulatory protein interactions or changes in chromatin organization at responsive genes. Chromatin is the complex of DNA and associated proteins that influences access to genetic information. Certain chromatin states may favor more rapid transcription upon challenge. No single mechanism explains all examples of priming; the relevant process depends on the inducing stimulus, gene, tissue and time interval.

To establish that a chromatin change contributes causally to priming, it is not enough to find a histone modification near a strongly expressed gene. The modification might be deposited as a consequence of the original transcriptional response. It might also be associated with a change that depends on another factor. A stronger experiment shows that the mark or the machinery responsible for it is needed for enhanced later responsiveness, while controlling for ordinary gene expression and developmental defects. The same principle applies to accumulation of inactive kinases: their presence is a candidate explanation, but functional perturbation and appropriate controls are needed to determine whether they enable the observed primed response.

Priming is attractive as a strategy for reducing the cost of unnecessary defense. A plant can invest in preparation rather than maintain every defensive product at its maximum level during periods without attack. Yet priming is not cost-free. Establishing and maintaining the preparatory state uses resources, and altering the responsiveness of a pathway can affect development or the plant's response to a different stress. The relative cost of direct activation versus priming depends on the duration and magnitude of the response, the frequency of challenges and the growth conditions. It cannot be predicted from a single defense marker. A useful experiment measures growth and reproduction during the preparatory period as well as resistance during the challenge.

Cross-protection occurs when exposure to one stimulus changes resistance to another. For example, a mild environmental stress can induce protective proteins or antioxidant capacity that later helps cells withstand a different stress. An earlier pathogen can similarly establish systemic protection against a different pathogen. Cross-protection is an outcome; priming is one possible process leading to it. If an earlier stimulus directly induces a protective product that persists into the second challenge, cross-protection can occur without enhanced later induction. If the product is low before the challenge but increases more rapidly afterward, a priming interpretation is more appropriate. Distinguishing these explanations requires sampling before the later stress.

There are limits to the useful duration of a primed state. Regulatory molecules can be degraded, metabolites can be consumed, chromatin states can change and organs can develop after the initial stimulus. A prolonged interval between exposures may eliminate the effect or change which tissues show it. Stronger priming is not invariably better if it increases sensitivity to an inappropriate signal or delays recovery. The physiology of priming therefore includes establishment, maintenance, expression and eventual resetting. Exam questions often focus on the first three phases, but resetting is important for understanding why an induced state is not a permanent alteration of every cell in the plant.

8. Hormonal cross-talk: coordinating competing defensive programs

A pathogen and an insect can attack the same leaf, but the plant responses useful against these attackers are not necessarily identical. A biotroph depends on living host tissue and frequently encounters SA-associated defenses. A necrotroph benefits from dead tissue and often encounters defenses regulated by jasmonates and ethylene. Chewing herbivores frequently induce jasmonate-dependent wound and chemical defense responses. A drought occurring at the same time may increase abscisic acid, or ABA, and change stomatal behavior and metabolic capacity. The signal controlling an output in one experiment may thus be modified in another experiment by a simultaneous hormonal input. Cross-talk is the functional interaction between signaling pathways such that activity of one modifies the output of another.

JA-Ile, the conjugate of jasmonic acid with isoleucine, is a major active jasmonate signal. The perception mechanism involving COI1 and JAZ regulatory proteins was introduced in the herbivory chapter. In the presence of a suitable jasmonate signal, JAZ repressors are targeted for proteasomal degradation through the COI1-containing ubiquitin ligase machinery. This releases regulatory proteins such as MYC transcription factors from repression, permitting changes in expression of defense-associated genes. The output depends on the factors present in the particular cell and the other signaling pathways active at that time. Consequently, increased JA-Ile concentration does not guarantee identical expression of every jasmonate-responsive gene in every organ.

The jasmonate response includes functionally distinguishable branches. A MYC-associated branch is prominent in many wound and herbivory responses. Another branch, in which ethylene can cooperate with jasmonate signaling, includes transcription factors such as ERF1 and ORA59 and is important in several responses to necrotrophic pathogens. PDF1.2, encoding a plant defensin, is often used as a marker of the JA–ethylene-associated response in Arabidopsis. The MYC and ERF-associated branches can influence each other. A statement that a plant has high jasmonate signaling is therefore incomplete unless the relevant output is identified. A treatment that increases a wound-associated gene may not increase PDF1.2 to the same extent.

SA can suppress expression of certain JA-associated outputs in many model systems. Such antagonism can help explain why treatment with an SA-associated inducer sometimes reduces the expression of genes needed for defense against a necrotroph or herbivore. However, antagonism is not a chemical rule that SA always neutralizes JA molecules. Cross-talk may occur at transcriptional regulation, through altered abundance of pathway components, at hormone biosynthesis or through feedback. For example, a plant can contain considerable jasmonate while expressing a particular jasmonate-responsive gene weakly because a downstream transcriptional regulator is inhibited. Measuring hormone amount and measuring hormone-responsive transcripts provide different kinds of information.

Ethylene provides a useful example of conditional cooperation. Its signaling pathway changes gene regulation through components including EIN2 and EIN3-related transcriptional regulators. In certain contexts ethylene supports the JA-associated ERF branch, increasing transcription of appropriate defense genes. In other contexts ethylene alters growth, senescence or other immune outputs in ways that depend on tissue and developmental status. Ethylene produced in waterlogged tissue also participates in responses to low oxygen and changes in growth form. It is therefore misleading to label ethylene as exclusively a disease-defense hormone. Its biological function is the outcome of the pathway in the context in which it is activated.

ABA is commonly associated with drought responses because it helps regulate stomata and protective gene expression. During a microbial challenge, ABA can contribute to preinvasive defense through stomatal closure, reducing entry by some pathogens that use stomata. Once a pathogen has entered, ABA-mediated changes may suppress particular SA-associated immune outputs in some systems, potentially favoring infection. These outcomes are not contradictory: a signaling molecule can strengthen one stage of defense while weakening another. The interpretation depends on whether the experiment measures entry, multiplication after entry, symptom development or whole-plant performance.

The inoculation method is particularly important when examining stomatal defense. If a bacterial suspension is placed on the leaf surface, stomatal closure may restrict access to internal spaces. If the suspension is introduced directly into the leaf interior, this physical entry barrier is bypassed. An ABA-associated genotype might therefore show resistance in a surface inoculation assay but not in an infiltration assay. Such a pattern would be consistent with an effect on the entry stage rather than necessarily on antibacterial responses inside the leaf. Controls for leaf anatomy and initial recovered bacterial numbers would make the comparison more convincing.

The timing of hormonal inputs can alter their interaction. If a plant encounters a pathogen first, SA-associated transcriptional changes may already be established when an herbivore arrives. If the order is reversed, jasmonate-induced products may already be present. Simultaneous hormone application does not reproduce either sequential exposure perfectly. The first stress may alter the tissue itself, changing carbon availability, water status or the number of living cells. Consequently, order effects cannot always be attributed exclusively to hormone cross-talk. A well-designed experiment compares both orders, equal exposure intervals and appropriate single-stress controls.

Hormonal cross-talk should be described as context dependent, not as a universal rule of fixed positive or negative signs. Cooperation and antagonism vary with hormone concentration, timing, pathogen lifestyle, plant genotype and the regulatory output being measured. Some pathogens manipulate plant hormonal networks, sometimes activating a pathway that interferes with a more appropriate host defense. Such manipulation further complicates simple predictions based only on the identity of the attacker. A hormone-dependent response can be beneficial for the host in one interaction and exploited by a pathogen in another. The examination task is to use the stated experimental evidence to determine what occurs in the given situation.

Consider a simplified regulatory problem. A treatment elevates PR1 expression, reduces PDF1.2 induction after a necrotroph challenge, and increases the growth of that necrotroph. The pattern is consistent with an SA-associated response antagonizing a useful JA–ethylene-associated output. It does not prove direct interaction between SA and an ERF transcription factor, and it does not demonstrate that every aspect of the JA pathway has been suppressed. If a mutant defective in SA signaling no longer shows the suppression of PDF1.2, that would support dependence on the SA response. A rescue experiment restoring the affected regulatory gene would provide further evidence. The logical distinction is between a functional interaction, a pathway requirement and a directly established molecular interaction.

The relationship between stress and growth hormones also deserves attention. Auxin participates in cell expansion and developmental patterning, and gibberellins promote growth in several contexts. Defensive responses can change sensitivity to these signals. The DELLA proteins involved in gibberellin signaling provide one familiar example of regulatory links between growth and defense, including interactions affecting jasmonate-associated responses. At NET level the essential idea is not an exhaustive map of every protein interaction, but the recognition that growth regulation and immune regulation share components and can influence each other. This connection leads directly to the physiology of combined stresses.

9. Combined stresses create a distinct physiological condition

Plants in a field rarely experience a single environmental variable changing while all others remain constant. A hot afternoon can coincide with low soil water availability, excess light and a high vapor pressure deficit. Roots exposed to saline soil can also experience nutrient imbalances. A leaf experiencing an insect attack may already have a fungal infection or may be recovering from a water deficit. Laboratory single-stress experiments are valuable because they isolate particular processes, but their conclusions cannot always be added together to predict the response to a combined stress. A combination can create a new physiological condition because the responses to its components interact.

Drought and heat provide a clear example. When water is limited, stomatal closure can decrease transpiration and conserve water. When a well-watered leaf becomes hot, transpiration can contribute to evaporative cooling if the surrounding atmosphere allows it. Under drought and heat together, keeping stomata open may accelerate dehydration, while closing them reduces the potential for evaporative cooling. The same stomatal conductance cannot simultaneously maximize water conservation and evaporative heat dissipation under otherwise identical conditions. A plant can adjust other features, such as leaf angle, reflective properties, root access to water and protective metabolism, but it cannot remove the physical conflict simply by activating both single-stress responses at full intensity.

Stomatal behavior also alters carbon dioxide delivery to photosynthetic tissue. Closure increases resistance to diffusion of carbon dioxide into the leaf. Under bright conditions, light harvesting may continue to deliver excitation energy while carbon assimilation is restricted. This imbalance can contribute to excess excitation and the formation of reactive oxygen species unless alternative energy dissipation or metabolism accommodates it. Heat may independently affect photosynthetic enzymes and membranes. Thus drought and heat together can influence leaf temperature, hydration, carbon fixation and oxidative stress through connected mechanisms. A decline in photosynthesis under the combination cannot be assigned solely to stomatal limitation without additional measurements.

The work of Rizhsky and colleagues on Arabidopsis showed that the transcriptional and physiological response to drought plus heat differs from the responses to either stress on its own. Some genes induced by one individual stress were also induced in the combination, whereas other expression patterns were specific to the combined condition. This supports the view that overlapping single-stress pathways do not fully describe multi-stress physiology. It is important to interpret the result at the correct level. An expression pattern unique to the combination establishes a difference in transcriptional regulation under those conditions. It does not prove that each such transcript provides protection, nor does it imply that the same set of genes will be specifically induced in every plant species.

Salinity and water deficit provide a partly overlapping combination. Both can reduce the availability of water to plant cells, creating osmotic stress. Salinity additionally introduces ionic effects when sodium and chloride accumulate excessively or disturb nutrient balance. A plant adjusted to a moderate nonionic osmotic stress may have protective solutes or ABA-associated responses that improve some aspects of salt tolerance. However, osmotic adjustment alone does not guarantee sodium exclusion, potassium retention or ion compartmentation. Cross-protection between stresses sharing one component may thus be incomplete. The molecular reason is that a common primary disturbance does not remove the additional, stress-specific component.

Flooding and infection can also interact. Waterlogged roots may experience oxygen limitation, constraining mitochondrial ATP production and changing the energy available for transport and biosynthesis. Water saturation may alter the microbial community and the dispersal of certain pathogens. A plant with compromised root function can become more vulnerable to an attacker, but some flooding-adapted plants develop anatomical changes that improve internal oxygen transport. The outcome depends on the duration of waterlogging, tissue oxygen levels, the pathogen's own ecological requirements and the plant's acclimation mechanisms. One cannot predict the disease outcome solely from the statement that the plant experienced flooding.

Excess light and drought commonly converge on chloroplast redox balance. Light provides the energy for photosynthesis, but a restricted capacity to use absorbed energy increases the importance of nonphotochemical quenching, alternative electron sinks and antioxidant systems. If antioxidant capacity is induced by an earlier moderate stress, it may contribute to protection during a second stress that also increases ROS production. Nevertheless, increasing antioxidant activity indiscriminately could alter ROS-dependent signaling. The protective goal is not zero ROS, because appropriate concentrations and locations of reactive species contribute to signaling. The goal is to maintain a regulated redox environment in which damaging reactions are limited and useful signals can still occur.

Interactions may be assessed on different scales. If two stresses each reduce growth by a certain amount, a combined reduction larger than the sum of the individual reductions on an additive scale is sometimes described as a more-than-additive negative effect. However, the meaning of additivity depends on the response scale. For a proportional survival endpoint, an independence model based on multiplying survival fractions may be more suitable. A student must identify the reference model specified in the problem before declaring synergy or antagonism. Biological interpretation should also distinguish an interaction in the measured endpoint from a direct molecular interaction between pathways.

A factorial experiment is a standard way to investigate combined stresses. For two stresses A and B, the simplest complete design includes a control, A alone, B alone and A plus B. All conditions should otherwise be matched. This arrangement makes it possible to estimate the effect of A in the absence of B and compare it with the effect of A in the presence of B. A difference between these effects represents an interaction on the chosen measurement scale. Biological replication and appropriate randomization are necessary to determine whether the interaction is larger than expected from ordinary variation. Four means are sufficient for illustrating the calculation but not for assessing uncertainty.

Suppose an illustrative experiment reports dry mass values of 10 grams for control plants, 8 grams for A, 7 grams for B and 4 grams for the combination. The loss due to A alone is 2 grams and the loss due to B alone is 3 grams. Under an additive model the expected combined mass is 5 grams. The observed value is one gram lower than that expectation. The same result can be written as an interaction contrast: 4 minus 8 minus 7 plus 10 equals negative one gram. This numerical result indicates a departure from additivity for these hypothetical means. It does not identify whether the physiological cause is impaired photosynthesis, increased respiratory costs, reduced leaf area or another process.

In a multiplicative model, the fraction remaining under A is 0.8 and under B is 0.7. If the proportional effects are independent, their product is 0.56, corresponding to 5.6 grams from the 10-gram control. The observed 4 grams is also below this alternative expectation, but the size of the departure differs. Neither model should be selected simply because it produces a larger apparent interaction. The experimental question and statistical assumptions should determine the scale. Growth measurements close to zero have physical bounds, and percentage losses cannot increase indefinitely. Such constraints can make additive interpretations misleading for very severe stresses.

To connect an interaction to a mechanism, measure intermediate physiological variables. Leaf water potential and relative water content describe aspects of hydration. Stomatal conductance and gas exchange help interpret diffusion limitation and carbon assimilation. Chlorophyll fluorescence can provide information about photosystem II performance and energy dissipation. Ion content can reveal disturbed mineral balance under salinity. Hormone concentrations, transcripts and protein activities reveal regulatory responses but should be interpreted alongside functional outcomes. If a combination produces severe growth loss despite relatively maintained water content, the cause may lie elsewhere. A single endpoint rarely distinguishes all plausible explanations.

Combined stress also raises a question about stress history. A plant exposed gradually to increasing heat may have time to induce protective chaperones, whereas an abrupt heat episode during drought may cause injury before these changes become effective. Two plants receiving the same final temperature and water potential may therefore show different responses because of their previous exposures. The duration and order of treatments should be described as part of the experimental conditions. Comparing plants only at the final environmental values may conceal an important causal difference in their acclimation histories.

10. Growth, defense and the economics of recovery

Growth and defense both require a living metabolic system. Leaves assimilate carbon through photosynthesis, roots acquire minerals, and respiration supplies ATP and metabolic intermediates. The products of these processes can support synthesis of cell walls, expansion, cell division, maintenance, reproduction or defensive compounds. The allocation of resources changes with the plant's developmental stage and environmental conditions. A plant investing in rapid leaf growth before an attack may redirect some of its metabolism after infection. The shift is not simply a division of a fixed stock of energy, because stress can also change the rate at which resources are acquired and the efficiency with which they are used.

One way to distinguish the costs of resistance is to separate allocation effects from regulatory effects. Producing a protective protein requires amino acids, ATP and other resources, so an allocation cost is plausible. Yet a plant may also grow more slowly because defense signals directly alter developmental regulation, even when a large shortage of carbon has not been demonstrated. In that case a growth change reflects a regulated physiological decision rather than only competition for substrates. Conversely, stress itself can damage the photosynthetic apparatus or roots, reducing available resources independently of defensive investment. Observing reduced biomass in an infected plant does not show that defense caused the entire reduction.

A convincing test of defense-associated costs needs an unchallenged comparison. Plants with elevated constitutive immunity can be compared with otherwise comparable control plants in an environment without the relevant attacker. If the constitutively defended plants grow more slowly or produce fewer seeds, the difference may indicate a cost associated with their altered state. But a mutation can affect development through multiple independent functions. Genetic complementation or comparisons among several independently generated lines can help distinguish defense-associated effects from unrelated consequences of the genetic change. A treatment that directly poisons cells is not a valid demonstration of the physiological cost of adaptive defense.

Benefits should be measured in the corresponding challenged environment. A defensively prepared plant may have slightly lower growth before infection but maintain more biomass or seed production after pathogen attack. The balance depends on the probability, intensity and timing of attack. A protective state with a cost during every day of growth may be beneficial where attacks are frequent but disadvantageous where they are rare. Priming can reduce some of the continuous investment by delaying the strongest outputs until a challenge, but the preparation and heightened responsiveness can still impose costs. These alternatives should be compared using plant performance across both challenged and unchallenged environments.

An illustrative numerical example makes the principle clear. Suppose an untreated genotype produces 100 seeds per plant in the absence of attack and 40 seeds under attack. A primed condition produces 95 seeds in the absence of attack and 70 under attack. Assume these values represent stable expected outcomes in two possible environments, and let p be the probability of attack. The average seed production of untreated plants is 100 times one minus p plus 40 times p, which simplifies to 100 minus 60p. The corresponding value for primed plants is 95 minus 25p. Priming is favored under this simplified arithmetic expectation when 95 minus 25p exceeds 100 minus 60p, or when 35p exceeds 5. The crossover occurs at p equal to one seventh, approximately 0.143. This is not a published field estimate; it illustrates how a small cost without attack and a larger benefit with attack can be weighed.

The model has important limitations. It assumes two environments, constant treatment effects, no changes in the timing of reproduction, no inherited variation in risk and a direct relationship between seed number and fitness. Real environments vary in stress severity and frequency, seeds differ in viability, and the survival of descendants can depend on factors not captured by seed count. It also assumes that the treatment does not change the probability of attack itself. Nevertheless, it helps explain why a mechanism should not be judged from growth measurements in an unstressed greenhouse alone or from disease measurements in an artificially severe challenge alone. Both benefits and costs are part of plant adaptation.

Tolerance offers another strategy for maintaining performance. Suppose an herbivore consumes a certain proportion of leaf area. A plant with remaining meristems, stored carbohydrates and sufficient water may replace damaged tissues or adjust the activity of undamaged leaves. Such compensation can reduce the impact of damage without necessarily changing the herbivore's survival or feeding. Tolerance should be quantified by comparing plant performance across damage levels rather than by measuring final biomass in a single treatment. If two genotypes have different unstressed growth rates, their absolute biomass after damage may differ even if their relative tolerance is identical. A response curve relating damage to fitness or growth is a better basis for comparison.

Recovery is not merely the reverse of stress perception. When drought ends, water availability may improve, but membranes, proteins and conducting tissues may still require repair. Stomata may reopen at a rate influenced by hormonal state and hydraulic function. If leaf damage has reduced carbon assimilation, a plant may initially depend more on stored compounds. When a pathogen is controlled, synthesis of defensive metabolites may decline as tissues reset, but some induced resistance can persist. Recovery therefore includes restoration of functional processes, repair of injury, adjustment of allocation and eventual resetting of stress-responsive pathways. The order and speed of these events depend on what was damaged and what remained functional.

After flooding, the return of oxygen can itself generate oxidative stress. During the low-oxygen period, cells adjust metabolism to maintain essential functions under restricted ATP production. Reoxygenation changes the operation of respiratory and redox systems and can lead to increased ROS production. A plant surviving the low-oxygen phase is not necessarily able to resume normal growth immediately. Antioxidant capacity, membrane integrity and the survival of roots and meristems can influence recovery. A single measurement of survival at the end of the flooding period may therefore underestimate differences in recovery potential. A complete comparison includes the subsequent growth phase.

Source–sink relations are central to recovery. A mature leaf damaged by a pathogen may become less able to export assimilates, while developing leaves and roots continue to require carbon. Some infected tissues can alter local sugar metabolism and become relatively strong sinks. A systemic response in healthy leaves may protect future photosynthetic capacity, benefiting the whole plant even if the original leaf is lost. The balance depends on the amount of healthy source tissue, the ability to mobilize storage compounds and the demand of developing organs. An examination question describing reduced root growth after leaf infection need not imply that roots were directly infected; altered allocation or systemic regulation could contribute.

Growth and defense must finally be understood in terms of developmental goals. During seedling establishment, loss of a small amount of root or leaf tissue may have a large effect on later growth. During flowering, stress can alter reproductive development even if vegetative survival remains high. During seed filling, the allocation of assimilates to seeds influences yield. The same molecular resistance mechanism may have different consequences when activated at different stages. This is one reason crop performance cannot be predicted solely from a resistance marker measured in young leaves. The biological question is how the coordinated response changes the outcome that matters under the relevant environment.

11. Stress memory, resetting and the limits of inheritance claims

A plant that has previously experienced an environmental disturbance can sometimes respond differently to a second exposure after a period of recovery. This phenomenon is often described as stress memory. The term is operational: it refers to a measurable dependence of a later response on a previous stimulus. It does not imply conscious recollection or nervous system activity. A memory-like physiological state can arise from persistent changes in proteins, metabolites, organ structure, gene regulation or chromatin. Identifying which of these mechanisms contributes requires comparing appropriate stages and manipulating candidate factors.

A standard repeated-stress experiment includes an initial stress, a recovery interval and a subsequent challenge. A second group experiences no initial stress but is challenged under otherwise matched conditions. Additional controls are important. Plants exposed to the first stress but not the second reveal residual effects during recovery. Plants receiving neither stress define age-related changes in unstressed physiology. If the previously stressed plants show faster induction of a protective gene or less injury during the later stress, the result is consistent with stress memory. But the interpretation depends on whether the first stress left persistent direct protection, changed the plant's size or selected a subset of surviving individuals.

Heat acclimation illustrates this issue. A mild heat treatment can induce heat-shock proteins and other protective changes, improving tolerance of a later severe heat exposure. If protective proteins remain abundant during the recovery period, they can contribute directly to later tolerance. In some cases transcriptional responsiveness to heat can also be altered for an interval after the initial treatment. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. To distinguish persistent protection from enhanced inducibility, measure protective protein abundance before the second exposure and monitor its change afterward. A plant with a high prechallenge protein pool may be protected even if its second transcriptional response is no larger than that of an unprimed plant.

Repeated drought may affect leaf size, root architecture, stomatal behavior and osmotic properties. A smaller leaf area after the first drought can reduce total water loss during a subsequent drought, even if the molecular sensing and signaling pathways have not acquired increased responsiveness. This represents a persistent physiological consequence of prior stress. A student should not automatically label it a chromatin-based memory. Comparing leaves of similar developmental stage, measuring plant size and applying a comparable water potential trajectory can help separate structural effects from changes in signaling. The distinction becomes especially important when stress itself changes growth substantially.

Some stress-responsive genes show changes in chromatin-associated marks that persist longer than the initial transcriptional response. A modification of histone proteins or chromatin organization can influence how readily transcription factors and transcriptional machinery access a gene. Such persistence is a plausible component of somatic stress memory. However, the presence of a histone mark does not establish that the mark caused the later response. Experiments with mutants affecting the relevant chromatin regulation, combined with expression measurements at multiple times and restoration of gene function, can provide stronger causal evidence. Chromatin changes may also have other effects on growth and gene expression that complicate interpretation.

Somatic stress memory occurs within the individual plant that experienced the original stimulus. Its duration may extend across the lifetime of particular cells or organs, but it need not extend into offspring. Intergenerational effects are observed when offspring of stressed parents differ from offspring of control parents. Such differences may arise through maternal provisioning, seed size, stored metabolites, hormonal content, parental effects on development or epigenetic processes. The observation of a phenotype in the first offspring generation does not by itself establish stable transmission of an epigenetic modification independent of direct exposure or maternal environment.

A stronger claim of transgenerational stress memory requires careful generational design. In plants, developing seeds and reproductive tissues may be exposed to conditions experienced by the maternal individual. To test persistent inheritance, descendants should be grown in a common environment without the original inducing stress, and the effect should be examined in further stress-free generations. Some researchers distinguish intergenerational effects detectable in the first stress-free offspring generation from transgenerational effects maintained into at least a further stress-free generation. The exact exposure history of reproductive cells and developing embryos must be documented. A simple label such as F1 or F2 is not sufficient unless the experimental design explains which tissues were exposed.

Genetic selection is another alternative explanation. If a stress kills susceptible individuals in a genetically diverse population, the survivors may carry alleles conferring greater resistance. Their offspring may also be more resistant because the allele frequencies changed, not because individuals transmitted an acquired epigenetic state. This would be evolutionary selection across a population. Experiments using genetically uniform lines and carefully documented reproduction can reduce this explanation, although mutations and other variation remain possible. Comparing the genomes and relevant phenotypes can help distinguish genetic changes from epigenetic inheritance when that distinction is central to the claim.

Reciprocal crosses can be useful when studying inheritance. If a response is observed only when the stressed parent contributes the maternal side, a maternal effect or a maternally transmitted factor is plausible. This pattern does not automatically establish the precise mechanism, because seed development and cytoplasmic inheritance both differ between reciprocal crosses. If an effect occurs through either parental side, that may support other possibilities but still does not exclude all confounding explanations. Measuring seed mass, germination, developmental rate and the candidate molecular mark can help interpret the offspring phenotype. Demonstrating that a particular mark is causal is a stronger requirement than observing its transmission.

Resetting can be adaptive. A persistent response to an old environment may no longer be appropriate when conditions change. Continued production of costly defensive compounds may restrict growth, while a persistent bias toward a particular hormone pathway could impair a response needed for a different attacker. Protein turnover, changes in hormone metabolism, chromatin regulation and the development of new tissues may contribute to loss of a previous state. The useful length of memory therefore depends on the likelihood that the earlier stimulus predicts a future challenge and on the cost of maintaining altered physiology. There is no general reason to expect every stress memory to persist throughout life or across generations.

The appropriate examination strategy is to match the conclusion to the level of evidence. A repeated-stress phenotype after recovery supports a history-dependent response. Demonstrating a persistent molecular change supports a candidate mechanism. Functional perturbation can provide evidence that the mechanism contributes to the response. Observing effects in offspring establishes an intergenerational phenotype under the stated conditions. Persistence through appropriate stress-free generations, exclusion of direct exposure and genetic selection, and evidence of a transmitted regulatory mechanism are needed for a stronger transgenerational claim. Each step requires additional evidence; none follows automatically from the preceding one.

12. Integrating the physiology of the entire plant

The topics of Unit 6 describe processes that are often taught separately because their molecular details differ. In a stressed plant, however, the processes operate simultaneously. Photosynthetic light reactions provide ATP and reducing power within chloroplasts, carbon fixation supplies organic compounds, respiration supports metabolic activity throughout the plant, roots acquire nitrogen and other mineral nutrients, and vascular tissues connect sources and sinks. Hormone and light signaling regulate the timing and distribution of these activities. Specialized metabolites contribute to defense, while stress-responsive pathways protect cellular structures and alter resource use. The systemic response is an integrated change in this network rather than an additional process that can be understood independently of the preceding topics.

Consider a mature leaf infected by a pathogen on a bright day. Local immune perception can induce ROS formation, changes in ion flux, kinase signaling and transcription. Some protective reactions consume reducing equivalents and require carbon precursors. At the same time, infection may reduce the efficiency of photosynthesis in affected regions. Nearby healthy cells may remain photosynthetically active and provide assimilates. Signals influencing distant leaves can modify their future defensive readiness, potentially protecting those leaves before the pathogen reaches them. The overall outcome depends on whether the plant retains enough functional photosynthetic area and transport capacity to support its responses.

The relationship between photosynthesis and redox protection is especially close. Chloroplasts absorb light energy and transfer electrons through a series of carriers. When carbon assimilation or other electron-consuming processes are limited, the balance between absorbed energy and its utilization can change. Photoprotective mechanisms reduce the risk of damage, while antioxidants help regulate reactive species. An immune response may also deliberately generate ROS through enzymes at the plasma membrane. The same general chemical classes of reactive molecules can thus participate in damage and signaling from different sources. An increase in total ROS measured in a stressed leaf cannot, without spatial or temporal information, identify the source or distinguish a protective signal from damaging accumulation.

Respiration provides energy for cellular maintenance and biosynthesis in both photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic tissues. Under normal oxygen availability, mitochondrial electron transport contributes to ATP production. During flooding or severe tissue oxygen limitation, cells may rely more on glycolysis and fermentation to sustain limited ATP generation and regenerate oxidized cofactors. Active transport of ions and synthesis of protective compounds can become more difficult when energy supply is constrained. A defense gene may be strongly induced at the transcript level, yet its physiological contribution may remain limited by energy or substrate availability. Integrating gene expression with metabolic capacity prevents overinterpretation of molecular markers.

Nitrogen metabolism connects directly to immune and stress metabolites. Amino acids provide building blocks for proteins and substrates for many biosynthetic pathways. Lysine is the precursor of Pip and NHP in the systemic immune pathway, while other amino acids contribute to specialized metabolite synthesis or osmotic adjustment. A shift in nitrogen assimilation can influence the available pools, but the amount of a precursor does not alone determine flux through a pathway. Enzyme abundance, regulation, compartmentation and competing uses influence product formation. A nitrogen-deficient plant may therefore respond differently to infection from a well-nourished plant even when its receptor genes are identical.

Plant hormones coordinate processes across tissues and timescales. ABA contributes to water balance, jasmonates participate prominently in wound and insect responses, ethylene influences defense and development, and SA is important in many immune interactions. Growth regulators influence organ expansion and differentiation. The effects of these molecules depend on the sensitivity and developmental state of the receiving cells as well as concentration. A root signal can influence shoot physiology, but the response differs among guard cells, vascular tissue and meristems because these cells express different regulatory and effector systems. Whole-plant regulation is therefore spatially differentiated, not a uniform change in a single hormone concentration.

Sensory photobiology adds a further layer. The daily light–dark cycle and the circadian clock influence metabolism, stomatal behavior and expression of some defense-related genes. The same challenge administered at different times of day can encounter different basal physiological states. Light quality can affect developmental regulation and interactions with defense pathways. In a controlled experiment, the timing of inoculation, light intensity and photoperiod must be kept consistent when comparing treatments. Otherwise a difference attributed to priming might reflect the time of sampling or different illumination. This is a practical example of why apparently separate topics in plant physiology matter for experimental design.

Transport is necessary for communication and resource distribution, but stress can alter the transport network itself. Water deficit can reduce hydraulic conductance or change transpiration. Damage to vascular tissues may disrupt the delivery of water and assimilates. Infected tissues may alter their sink strength, modifying how carbon is partitioned among organs. A chemical mediator that normally moves efficiently may have a different distribution when the source–sink relationship changes. The effectiveness of systemic defense may therefore depend partly on the physical and physiological condition of vascular tissues. An experiment measuring a mobile compound should consider whether differences in its arrival are due to biosynthesis, transport or altered metabolic removal.

Specialized metabolites connect hormonal signaling to ecological interactions. Jasmonate-associated responses can regulate production of compounds affecting herbivores, while infection can induce antimicrobial compounds such as phytoalexins. Some metabolites contribute to both protective chemistry and signaling, whereas others function predominantly as effectors. Their roles should be distinguished experimentally. Increased expression of a biosynthetic enzyme can suggest that production of a compound may change, but a metabolite assay is required to establish its accumulation. Demonstrating a direct effect on a pathogen or herbivore may require a further functional test. The pathway from signal to ecological outcome includes multiple steps, each potentially regulated.

A comprehensive evaluation of stress adaptation should therefore combine several outcomes. Immediate survival indicates whether essential function was maintained during stress. Subsequent growth reveals recovery. Disease or herbivore measurements indicate resistance, and performance at comparable damage levels informs tolerance. Gas exchange, fluorescence, hydration, ion balance and metabolite measurements reveal physiological mechanisms. Gene expression and mutant comparisons identify regulatory contributions. None alone is a universal index of plant health. A strong interpretation selects measurements appropriate to the particular question and considers whether they agree or reveal a trade-off.

Imagine two cultivars under intermittent drought and infection. One strongly closes stomata, conserves water, but develops high leaf temperatures and restricts carbon assimilation. The other maintains greater conductance while water is still available, supports cooling and growth, but risks more rapid dehydration if the dry period continues. A microbial root treatment might improve disease resistance in either cultivar, yet its benefit could be limited in the one experiencing severe energy shortage. There is no single ranking without defining the environmental trajectory and outcome. The most appropriate cultivar under brief drought may not be the best under prolonged drought and heat. The examination lesson is to interpret stress responses as conditional physiological strategies rather than as lists of universally beneficial traits.

13. Experimental inference and quantitative integration

Consider a hypothetical comparison of wild type, ald1, fmo1 and npr1 plants after local immune induction. Wild type accumulates NHP and displays strong systemic protection. The ald1 mutant has limited production of Pip and NHP, while fmo1 has a block in NHP production despite access to Pip. The npr1 mutant may accumulate immune-associated metabolites but fail to mount a normal transcriptional and resistance response. Supplying NHP could bypass the biosynthetic lesions in ald1 and fmo1, provided the downstream response is functional; it would be unlikely to fully restore an NPR1-dependent output in npr1. This is an example of using pathway position to predict chemical rescue. Actual mutant phenotypes must be interpreted in the relevant pathogen and treatment context.

One can design an analogous comparison for ISR. The beneficial root microbe should colonize the root similarly across the genotypes being compared, or colonization should be measured and included in the interpretation. If a jasmonate-insensitive mutant shows no systemic protection, a simple explanation is that its JA-dependent response is required. But the mutation might alter root development or microbial colonization, preventing the induction process itself. Measuring colonization and using tissue-specific rescue help distinguish a defect in receiving or expressing the systemic response from a defect in establishing the association. It is especially important to compare the treatment effect within each genotype rather than merely compare treated plants across genotypes with different baseline susceptibility.

When microbial counts are expressed as colony-forming units, abbreviated CFU, only organisms capable of producing colonies under the assay conditions are counted. A result of one million CFU per gram is a measure of culturable population density relative to the sampled tissue mass. Log transformation is useful when microbial populations span orders of magnitude and when multiplicative changes are biologically meaningful. If the control contains 10 to the power of 7 CFU per gram and treated tissue contains 10 to the power of 5, the difference is two base-ten log units. Dividing the first value by the second gives a 100-fold difference, and the relative decrease is 99 percent. A two-log decrease is not a two percent decrease.

Changes over time can be used to estimate an apparent population growth rate in an interval. If the logarithm to base two of population size increases by three units in six hours, the population has undergone the equivalent of three doublings over that period. The average doubling time is two hours, assuming the change represents approximately exponential net growth during the interval. In infected plant tissue, this is a net estimate because replication, death and spatial movement may all affect the population recovered. The assumption of constant growth may fail as resources become limited or immune responses intensify. The calculation is useful for comparing the early phase of a challenge, not as an assertion that pathogens grow exponentially throughout the infection.

A reporter of gene expression is often normalized to another signal or a reference transcript. In a real-time quantitative PCR experiment, relative transcript abundance is commonly estimated from cycle threshold differences under specified assumptions. When amplification efficiencies are close to twofold per cycle and comparable, the relative expression ratio can be estimated as two raised to the negative delta-delta Ct. A treated sample whose normalized Ct difference is two cycles smaller than the control has a delta-delta Ct of negative two, giving an estimated fourfold increase. Reliable reference genes and suitable controls are essential because stress may itself alter the abundance of a reference transcript. A fold change in RNA also does not imply an identical fold change in protein concentration or resistance.

Time courses support a distinction between altered baseline, delayed response, increased amplitude and persistence. Suppose a hypothetical primed plant shows a normalized transcript level of two before challenge, ten after two hours and twelve after eight hours. An unprimed plant starts at one, reaches four at two hours and twelve at eight hours. The primed plant shows a larger early increase, but both groups converge at the late time. A late sampling design would miss the early difference. The functional significance depends on whether the transcript corresponds to a protective process that becomes active soon enough to affect the attacker. Measuring bacterial populations at early and late times would connect response kinetics to the disease outcome.

A factorial design can also be used for the priming question itself. Treat initial exposure as factor P and later challenge as factor C. If the response variable is a transcript level, the interaction contrast compares the challenge-associated increment in pretreated plants with the increment in plants lacking pretreatment. For hypothetical values P0C0 equal to 1, P1C0 equal to 2, P0C1 equal to 5 and P1C1 equal to 12, the contrast is 12 minus 2 minus 5 plus 1, giving 6 units. On an additive scale this represents an enhanced increment. If the outcome is measured as a logarithm, the interpretation concerns proportional differences. Reporting the scale is part of the reasoning, not a cosmetic choice.

Replication determines the strength of these conclusions. Measuring three leaves from one plant can improve knowledge of variation within that plant, but these leaves are not three fully independent plant-level biological replicates if the treatment is applied to the whole plant. Treating them as independent could underestimate variability. Independent plants assigned to treatment groups provide replication at the appropriate experimental unit. If plants share pots, trays or growth chamber conditions, additional shared environmental influences may need to be considered. Randomization and suitable blocking can reduce confounding by position, chamber or batch. The experimental unit should be defined before analyzing the data.

The endpoint and statistical question should match the biological claim. A significant difference in PR1 expression supports a difference in that marker, not necessarily in pathogen resistance. A significant interaction between drought and heat for dry mass supports a combined effect on growth, not a specific direct interaction between ABA and a heat-response transcription factor. A mutant that loses a physiological response supports a role for the affected gene, but pleiotropic effects and altered development may complicate attribution. Combining genetic, physiological and biochemical measurements improves the precision of the conclusion.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. SAR is adaptive immunity in the animal sense.

Correct concept: Acquired describes a change in defensive state; plant recognition remains innate and does not use antibody rearrangement. Source of confusion: The word acquired also describes vertebrate adaptive responses. Exam reasoning: Look for distractors invoking B cells, antigen-specific antibodies or clonal selection.

2. Distal PR1 expression alone establishes resistance.

Correct concept: An independent challenge with a suitable disease endpoint is required. Source of confusion: PR1 is an easily measured defense marker. Exam reasoning: Ask whether the data show a regulatory change or actual pathogen restriction.

3. SA must be the transported signal because it is required in the distal leaf.

Correct concept: A receiving tissue may require SA without receiving the initiating message as SA. Source of confusion: Students confuse necessity at the site of response with mobility. Exam reasoning: Use reciprocal grafts, labeled molecules and tissue-specific rescue to distinguish the roles.

4. Pip and NHP are chemically and functionally identical.

Correct concept: FMO1 catalyzes NHP formation from Pip; the products can differ in rescue ability. Source of confusion: Both are associated with systemic resistance. Exam reasoning: Predict the effect of supplying the substrate versus the downstream product to fmo1.

5. Exogenous NHP should fully rescue npr1.

Correct concept: NHP can bypass biosynthetic loss but cannot substitute for missing NPR1-dependent transcription. Source of confusion: Chemical rescue is wrongly generalized to every immune mutant. Exam reasoning: Determine whether the defect is upstream in biosynthesis or downstream in response.

6. All NHP detected distally must have moved from the infected leaf.

Correct concept: Transport and local production can both contribute to distal accumulation. Source of confusion: A measured pool is mistaken for a transported flux. Exam reasoning: Distinguish labeled imported material from newly synthesized material.

7. ISR always depends on JA and ethylene and never SA.

Correct concept: Many classic ISR systems use JA and ET; dependencies vary with the organism and host. Source of confusion: A useful textbook tendency becomes an absolute rule. Exam reasoning: Follow the specific mutant and treatment data.

8. A beneficial microbe directly killing a root pathogen proves ISR.

Correct concept: Direct antagonism is distinct from systemic plant-mediated defense. Source of confusion: Both can reduce disease. Exam reasoning: Require spatially separated induction and challenge and host-response evidence.

9. Priming means that defense is continuously maximally induced.

Correct concept: Priming changes response capacity; direct induction can coexist but is not equivalent. Source of confusion: High postchallenge output is mistaken for high basal output. Exam reasoning: Compare all four pretreatment-by-challenge groups and time courses.

10. A late identical marker level rules out priming.

Correct concept: The primed response may be faster even if both groups reach the same plateau. Source of confusion: Single endpoints conceal response kinetics. Exam reasoning: Inspect early latency, slope and challenge-induced increments.

11. SA always chemically neutralizes JA.

Correct concept: Cross-talk can modify particular regulatory outputs, and interactions are context dependent. Source of confusion: An antagonistic arrow is read as an irreversible chemical reaction. Exam reasoning: Compare hormones and branch-specific markers separately.

12. ABA always increases or always decreases disease resistance.

Correct concept: ABA may reduce stomatal entry but weaken some post-entry defenses. Source of confusion: Different stages of infection are combined into one outcome. Exam reasoning: Compare surface inoculation with infiltration.

13. Combined stresses equal the arithmetic sum of individual responses.

Correct concept: Interactions may produce a distinct outcome; the appropriate null model depends on the scale. Source of confusion: Single-stress experiments are assumed to generalize directly. Exam reasoning: Calculate a factorial contrast before inferring nonadditivity.

14. Higher resistance necessarily means higher tolerance.

Correct concept: Resistance reduces attacker success; tolerance reduces performance loss for a given burden. Source of confusion: Both may improve final biomass. Exam reasoning: Measure burden and performance separately across damage levels.

15. Reduced growth proves that defense consumed all missing resources.

Correct concept: Stress injury, direct hormonal growth regulation and allocation costs may all contribute. Source of confusion: A trade-off is reduced to a simple fixed energy budget. Exam reasoning: Use unstressed defense-activated controls and measures of assimilation.

16. Survival at the end of stress guarantees recovery.

Correct concept: Repair, reoxygenation injury and restoration of function influence later growth. Source of confusion: Survival and recovery are treated as the same endpoint. Exam reasoning: Measure performance after a defined recovery period.

17. A histone modification at a responsive gene proves epigenetic causation.

Correct concept: A modification may be a consequence or correlate; functional evidence is necessary. Source of confusion: Persistent marks are attractive candidate mechanisms. Exam reasoning: Look for perturbation, restoration and controls for baseline expression.

18. An F1 response proves stable transgenerational inheritance.

Correct concept: Direct exposure and maternal provisioning can produce F1 effects without durable transmission. Source of confusion: Any parent–offspring resemblance is labeled epigenetic. Exam reasoning: Examine later stress-free generations, genotype and seed provisioning.

Original CSIR-NET and SET examination practice

All questions in this section are original practice items, not previous-year questions. Numerical and experimental data are illustrative.

Part B — Conceptual and mechanistic questions

1. Which observation best defines SAR?

  1. A distant untreated leaf restricts a later pathogen after local infection.
  2. A lesion becomes surrounded by callose.
  3. A leaf accumulates chlorophyll after light exposure.
  4. A root absorbs nitrate faster.
Answer and explanation

A. SAR is demonstrated by induced protection in separated tissue. Callose can be local, while the other observations do not establish acquired resistance.

2. The principal inducible SA biosynthetic enzyme in Arabidopsis is:

  1. FMO1
  2. ICS1
  3. COI1
  4. SARD4
Answer and explanation

B. ICS1 converts chorismate to isochorismate. FMO1 generates NHP, COI1 participates in jasmonate perception and SARD4 contributes to Pip synthesis.

3. NPR1 is best described as:

  1. A sodium efflux channel
  2. A microbial pattern molecule
  3. An SA-responsive regulator of immune transcription
  4. A chlorophyll biosynthetic enzyme
Answer and explanation

C. NPR1 regulates immune gene expression, including through TGA factors. It is neither a transporter nor an SA-synthesizing enzyme.

4. FMO1 catalyzes the formation of:

  1. NHP from Pip
  2. SA from chorismate
  3. JA-Ile from ABA
  4. Pip from NHP
Answer and explanation

A. FMO1 hydroxylates Pip to yield NHP. The other reactions assign incorrect substrates or reverse the relevant conversion.

5. Which amino acid supplies the carbon and nitrogen framework for Pip in the studied Arabidopsis pathway?

  1. Glycine
  2. Lysine
  3. Tryptophan
  4. Methionine
Answer and explanation

B. ALD1 and SARD4 act in the lysine-derived pathway. The other amino acids are not the starting substrate of this pathway.

6. What is the primary interpretation of strong PR1 transcript induction?

  1. All pathogen cells have died
  2. SA-associated defense transcription is activated
  3. The pathogen is necessarily a necrotroph
  4. All tissues have acquired identical resistance
Answer and explanation

B. PR1 is a marker, not a universal measure of pathogen killing or uniform protection.

7. Which treatment is characteristic of classic microbial ISR experiments?

  1. Localized beneficial root colonization followed by a shoot pathogen challenge
  2. Direct mixing of an antibiotic with a leaf pathogen
  3. Shading an entire leaf
  4. Measuring root pressure alone
Answer and explanation

A. The separated root and shoot treatments help demonstrate host-mediated systemic protection.

8. Which pair often mediates classical rhizobacteria-induced ISR?

  1. Auxin and cytokinin exclusively
  2. Gibberellin and ABA exclusively
  3. Jasmonate and ethylene
  4. Chlorophyll and starch
Answer and explanation

C. Many model ISR responses require JA and ET responsiveness; exceptions exist. The other pairs do not describe the classical route.

9. Which statement distinguishes a primed response from direct activation?

  1. Priming requires permanently elevated defense proteins.
  2. Priming changes responsiveness to later challenge.
  3. Direct activation occurs only in roots.
  4. Priming requires genetic mutation.
Answer and explanation

B. Priming is defined by altered later responsiveness, which can coexist with direct prechallenge activation.

10. What is a relevant physiological consequence of stomatal closure during drought?

  1. Higher potential transpiration at fixed conditions
  2. Lower water loss and potentially lower CO₂ entry
  3. Complete cessation of respiration
  4. Immediate increase in nitrogen fixation in every plant
Answer and explanation

B. Reduced aperture limits diffusion of water vapor and carbon dioxide. It does not imply complete cessation of metabolism.

11. Which group provides the baseline for a pretreatment-by-challenge factorial study?

  1. Primed and challenged
  2. Mock-pretreated and unchallenged
  3. Primed and challenged twice
  4. A different genotype under salt stress
Answer and explanation

B. The mock, unchallenged group controls for neither active exposure and provides a reference for both factors.

12. A biotroph differs from a necrotroph principally because it:

  1. Requires living host tissue during its biotrophic growth
  2. Never produces effectors
  3. Always infects roots
  4. Uses no host nutrients
Answer and explanation

A. Biotrophy depends on living host cells, whereas necrotrophy exploits killed or damaged tissue. Location and effector production do not define the distinction.

13. Which output is often associated with the Arabidopsis JA–ET ERF branch?

  1. PR1 exclusively
  2. PDF1.2 expression
  3. Xylem lignin transport
  4. ATP production by PSI alone
Answer and explanation

B. PDF1.2 is frequently used as a JA–ET-associated marker; PR1 is a common SA-associated marker.

14. In a typical source-to-sink phloem model, transport direction is determined primarily by:

  1. A universal downward flow rule
  2. Source and sink relationships
  3. The direction of gravity alone
  4. Whether the solute is an antibody
Answer and explanation

B. Pressure-driven phloem movement depends on loading and unloading in connected source and sink tissues.

15. Which is an example of tolerance rather than resistance?

  1. Reduced pathogen multiplication
  2. Fewer herbivores settling
  3. Maintained seed yield at the same pathogen burden
  4. Lower pathogen entry into stomata
Answer and explanation

C. Tolerance preserves performance despite a given burden; the other outcomes reduce attacker success.

16. Which statement about NHP mobility is defensible?

  1. All distal NHP is necessarily imported.
  2. Local NHP treatment can produce systemic movement and defense in Arabidopsis.
  3. NHP replaces every other immune signal.
  4. NHP is a transcription factor.
Answer and explanation

B. Experimental evidence supports mobility and systemic activity but not exclusive transport or universality.

17. A somatic stress memory is a history-dependent response:

  1. Within an individual exposed plant
  2. Only in its F4 offspring
  3. Only after a change in allele frequency
  4. Only in the central nervous system
Answer and explanation

A. Somatic memory persists within the individual; it does not require inheritance or a nervous system.

18. Which variable most directly tests pathogen growth in a bacterial challenge?

  1. Leaf color alone
  2. Culturable bacterial population over time
  3. PR1 promoter activity alone
  4. Initial seed size
Answer and explanation

B. Viable population measurements at appropriate times directly address net bacterial multiplication. Markers and color can be indirect.

19. Why can reoxygenation following flooding be damaging?

  1. Oxygen is never used in respiration.
  2. Restored oxygen can be associated with increased ROS production and injury.
  3. All ABA is instantly destroyed.
  4. All phloem is permanently blocked.
Answer and explanation

B. The metabolic transition during reoxygenation can create oxidative stress; survival during hypoxia does not guarantee recovery.

20. Which evidence is needed before labeling a stress-induced chromatin mark causal?

  1. A single association between mark and transcript
  2. Functional perturbation of the relevant regulation with suitable controls
  3. Its presence in one photographed leaf
  4. A larger SVG diagram of the gene
Answer and explanation

B. Perturbation helps distinguish a mechanism from an associated consequence; observation alone is not causal evidence.

21. What does methyl salicylate illustrate in systemic defense?

  1. A modified SA-related compound can have different mobility and conversion properties.
  2. SA signaling always bypasses all metabolites.
  3. A bacterial antigen is rearranged by plant lymphocytes.
  4. All volatile compounds are antibiotics.
Answer and explanation

A. Methylation can alter mobility and signaling properties, with evidence of roles in particular host systems.

22. A negative interaction contrast for dry biomass on an additive scale means:

  1. The observed combined biomass lies below the additive expectation.
  2. The two stress molecules directly bind.
  3. The experiment necessarily lacked controls.
  4. The combined stress increases growth.
Answer and explanation

A. The contrast describes a departure from the selected model at the endpoint; it does not establish a direct molecular mechanism.

Part C — Experimental and analytical reasoning

23. In reciprocal grafts, a normal donor induces protection in a normal recipient but not a mutant recipient; a mutant donor induces protection in a normal recipient. What is most strongly supported?

  1. The gene is required in the recipient response under these conditions.
  2. The gene is required exclusively for signal synthesis in the donor.
  3. The mutant cannot form any vascular tissue.
  4. The signal must be SA.
Answer and explanation

A. The mutant donor can generate effective information for a normal recipient. Failure with a mutant recipient supports a response-side requirement, assuming grafts and challenges are controlled. The signal identity is not determined.

24. Ald1 plants have low Pip and NHP; fmo1 plants have detectable Pip but little NHP. NHP restores resistance in both. What model fits?

  1. FMO1 produces lysine upstream of ALD1.
  2. ALD1 contributes upstream of Pip; FMO1 acts in its conversion to NHP.
  3. NPR1 synthesizes every amino acid.
  4. Pip and NHP are the same substance.
Answer and explanation

B. Different metabolite profiles locate distinct biosynthetic defects; downstream NHP bypasses both. The data do not support reversed or identical reactions.

25. After supplying NHP, a mutant accumulates NHP normally but fails to induce a normally NPR1-dependent PR transcript. What is the best next test?

  1. Measure downstream NPR1/TGA-dependent response and restore the defective signaling gene if identified.
  2. Measure only external leaf length.
  3. Repeat with a higher dose indefinitely.
  4. Assume NHP failed to reach the tissue despite the measurement.
Answer and explanation

A. Normal accumulation and defective output suggest a downstream problem, although the relevant active pool and cell location should be considered. A genetic response test is more informative than simply increasing dose.

26. A root treatment reduces shoot pathogen populations in wild type and sid2 but not coi1 or ein2. Colonization and initial challenge doses are matched. What follows?

  1. The ISR effect requires JA and ET responsiveness in this assay, but is retained in the tested SA biosynthetic mutant.
  2. JA and ET are necessarily the transported molecules.
  3. All forms of SAR are abolished.
  4. The beneficial bacterium directly killed every pathogen in the shoot.
Answer and explanation

A. The genotype-by-treatment comparison supports a signaling requirement. It neither identifies mobile molecules nor establishes universal pathway dependencies.

27. Marker means in four groups (mock/no challenge, primed/no challenge, mock/challenge, primed/challenge) are 2, 4, 7, 16. What is the additive priming interaction?

  1. 3
  2. 7
  3. 9
  4. 12
Answer and explanation

B. The increments are 16−4=12 and 7−2=5; their difference is 7. A simple difference of challenged means (9) ignores the baseline.

28. The same four groups instead yield 2, 9, 7, 14. Which inference is most accurate on an additive scale?

  1. Priming interaction is 7.
  2. The challenge-associated increase is 5 in each group; the final difference reflects the baseline shift.
  3. The pretreatment has no effect of any kind.
  4. Challenge suppresses the marker.
Answer and explanation

B. 14−9 and 7−2 both equal 5. Pretreatment changes baseline, but this endpoint does not show enhanced additive inducibility.

29. At 0, 2 and 8 hours a primed transcript has values 2, 10, 12; control values are 1, 4, 12. Which experiment best tests a useful early advantage?

  1. Measure only the 8-hour transcript again.
  2. Measure early protein activity and early pathogen multiplication with matched inoculum.
  3. Remove both challenge and control groups.
  4. Compare the plants only by seed size.
Answer and explanation

B. Early expression differs despite convergence. Protein activity and pathogen growth can connect kinetics to function, whereas late-only measurements miss the divergence.

30. A control has 10⁷ CFU/g after challenge and induced tissue 10⁵ CFU/g. Starting populations are matched. What is the final difference?

  1. Twofold and 2% reduction
  2. Tenfold and 90% reduction
  3. Hundredfold and 99% reduction
  4. Thousandfold and 100% reduction
Answer and explanation

C. 10⁷/10⁵=100. The fraction remaining is 0.01, so the decrease is 99%; log differences are not percentages.

31. Initial bacterial density is 10⁴ CFU/g and it becomes 8×10⁴ CFU/g after six hours. Assuming exponential net growth, what is the apparent doubling time?

  1. 0.75 h
  2. 2 h
  3. 3 h
  4. 8 h
Answer and explanation

B. The population increased eightfold, or three doublings. Six hours divided by three gives two hours; the estimate is limited to the stated interval.

32. Control, drought, heat and combined dry mass are 12, 9, 8 and 3 g. What is the additive interaction contrast?

  1. −2 g
  2. −1 g
  3. 0 g
  4. +2 g
Answer and explanation

A. 3−9−8+12=−2 g; additive expectation is 5 g. The result identifies a nonadditive endpoint, not a molecular binding event.

33. For the same data, what is expected combined mass if independent proportional effects multiply?

  1. 3 g
  2. 5 g
  3. 6 g
  4. 8 g
Answer and explanation

C. (9/12)×(8/12)×12=6 g. The additive prediction is 5 g, showing why the reference model matters.

34. A plant closes stomata during a combined hot and dry period. Which joint change is most plausible, holding other factors comparable?

  1. Water loss decreases but evaporative cooling can decrease.
  2. Water loss increases and cooling always stops.
  3. CO₂ entry must increase.
  4. Leaf temperature must equal air temperature.
Answer and explanation

A. Closure limits transpiration and can reduce cooling and CO₂ entry; actual leaf temperature also depends on radiation and boundary-layer exchange.

35. A genotype restricts a bacterial pathogen after surface inoculation but not after infiltration. Initial internal loads differ only for surface inoculation. Which interpretation is most plausible?

  1. A pre-entry barrier contributes to protection.
  2. All intracellular bacteria are killed by NHP.
  3. There is proof of heritable resistance.
  4. The pathogen cannot use stomata in any host.
Answer and explanation

A. Infiltration bypasses stomatal entry. The data support an entry-stage effect but do not uniquely establish which barrier or signal causes it.

36. SA pretreatment raises PR1 and lowers PDF1.2 induction during a necrotroph challenge, while JA concentration is unchanged. What is a reasonable inference?

  1. Cross-talk may affect JA–ET-responsive transcription downstream of hormone accumulation.
  2. SA necessarily prevented all JA synthesis.
  3. JA is absent.
  4. PR1 directly binds all PDF1.2 DNA.
Answer and explanation

A. Unchanged JA concentration does not exclude altered signaling output. The markers support a functional interaction, not a particular direct molecular binding mechanism.

37. After local application of labeled NHP, distal tissue contains labeled and unlabeled NHP. What can be concluded?

  1. Some applied label reached distal tissue in NHP form if chemical identity was established; the unlabeled pool may include local synthesis.
  2. Every distal molecule moved intact from the treated leaf.
  3. The unlabeled pool proves absence of transport.
  4. NHP must be the only mobile signal.
Answer and explanation

A. Label detection and chemical identification support movement or label incorporation as controlled in the experiment; unlabeled material cannot be assigned solely to transport.

38. Untreated plants produce 120 seeds without attack and 30 with attack. Primed plants produce 110 and 80. What attack probability makes expected seed yields equal?

  1. 1/6
  2. 1/5
  3. 1/3
  4. 1/2
Answer and explanation

A. Untreated expectation is 120−90p; primed is 110−30p. Equating gives 60p=10, or p=1/6. This assumes constant yields in two environments.

39. A treated and control cultivar carry the same pathogen burden. Treated plants retain 80% and controls 50% of unstressed biomass. What is supported?

  1. Greater tolerance at this burden, with resistance not shown to differ.
  2. Greater resistance proven by pathogen counts.
  3. No physiological difference.
  4. Complete immunity.
Answer and explanation

A. Performance differs despite comparable burden. A fuller tolerance curve across burdens would strengthen inference.

40. A beneficial root bacterium reduces root pathogen abundance and inhibits that pathogen in culture. What additional design best tests ISR?

  1. A distal shoot challenge with checks for absence of the beneficial bacterium in shoot tissue and host signaling controls.
  2. Repeat direct coculture only.
  3. Measure the bacterial colony color.
  4. Apply the beneficial bacterium directly to the shoot challenge site.
Answer and explanation

A. Spatial separation reduces direct antagonism as an explanation; host signaling mutants and colonization measurements add mechanistic evidence.

41. Previously droughted plants have smaller leaves and lose less total water during a second drought. What should be controlled before claiming molecular priming?

  1. Leaf area, developmental stage and comparable stress intensity.
  2. Only seed color.
  3. Only bacterial colony count.
  4. Nothing, because all reduced water loss is epigenetic.
Answer and explanation

A. Persistent structural differences can explain total water loss. Normalizing relevant variables and measuring inducibility help isolate regulatory memory.

42. A stress-associated histone mark persists after recovery. A mutant lacking the mark has impaired baseline transcription even without prior stress. Why is causal interpretation difficult?

  1. The mutant may affect ordinary expression as well as memory.
  2. Persistent histone marks cannot ever influence transcription.
  3. All chromatin is absent.
  4. A molecular mark proves inheritance immediately.
Answer and explanation

A. The perturbation has a baseline phenotype, so failure of the later response may not be specific to memory. Matched controls and complementation are useful.

43. Offspring from stressed mothers show higher survival, but also have larger seeds. Which test most directly strengthens a transgenerational claim?

  1. Raise descendants through further stress-free generations, control provisioning and genotype, and test persistence.
  2. Measure only the first offspring again.
  3. Select the largest seeds from stressed plants only.
  4. Expose every generation to the original stress.
Answer and explanation

A. Maternal provisioning can explain F1 effects; common environments and additional stress-free generations address persistent inheritance more directly.

44. Six leaves are collected from each of three plants per treatment. Treatment was applied to whole plants. How many independent plant-level replicates per treatment are present?

  1. 3
  2. 6
  3. 18
  4. 21
Answer and explanation

A. The plant is the treatment unit. Multiple leaves are subsamples, not independent plant-level replicates.

45. A stress combination increases an antioxidant transcript while photosynthesis declines sharply. Which conclusion is defensible?

  1. A molecular response occurred, but its functional adequacy must be tested.
  2. The transcript guarantees complete oxidative protection.
  3. Photosynthesis cannot decline if antioxidants are expressed.
  4. The result identifies the exact ROS species.
Answer and explanation

A. Expression may reflect stress severity or an insufficient response. Activity, redox state, damage and gas exchange are needed to connect the marker to protection.

46. Relative qPCR expression is calculated by 2^(−ΔΔCt) with comparable doubling efficiencies. ΔCt is 5 in control and 2 in induced tissue. What is the estimated expression ratio?

  1. 1/8
  2. 3
  3. 6
  4. 8
Answer and explanation

D. ΔΔCt=2−5=−3, giving 2³=8. This is a transcript ratio, not automatically a protein or resistance ratio.

State SET and assertion–reason practice

47. Assertion: NHP can restore an FMO1-deficient plant's systemic response in suitable assays. Reason: NHP is downstream of FMO1 in the pathway.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion true; reason false.
  4. Assertion false; reason true.
Answer and explanation

A. Supplying the downstream product bypasses its blocked production, provided response machinery functions.

48. Assertion: All ISR responses require SA accumulation. Reason: Some classical rhizobacterial ISR responses require JA and ethylene responsiveness.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion true; reason false.
  4. Assertion false; reason true.
Answer and explanation

D. The absolute assertion is false; the reason accurately describes many well-characterized cases.

49. Assertion: Strong PR1 transcription alone proves systemic pathogen resistance. Reason: PR1 is a widely used SA-associated marker.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion true; reason false.
  4. Assertion false; reason true.
Answer and explanation

D. A marker does not substitute for a distal challenge phenotype.

50. Which combination of endpoints best distinguishes resistance from tolerance?

  1. Pathogen burden and host performance across burden levels
  2. Only PR1 transcript abundance
  3. Only leaf color
  4. Only plant height before treatment
Answer and explanation

A. Attacker success and performance at comparable damage are the respective concepts; multiple burdens allow tolerance estimation.

51. Which experiment best demonstrates that early heat exposure altered the later challenge response rather than merely leaving high basal HSP abundance?

  1. Measure HSP before and during a second exposure, with both initial-stress and challenge controls.
  2. Measure only the final temperature.
  3. Compare two unrelated species at different temperatures.
  4. Measure only leaf length.
Answer and explanation

A. Baseline and time-course measurements distinguish persistent product from enhanced inducibility, although both may coexist.

52. A stress combination yields 60% survival; individual stresses yield 80% and 75% survival relative to a 100% control. Under independent proportional survival, which conclusion follows?

  1. 60% is exactly the multiplicative expectation.
  2. 60% is a 20% synergistic increase.
  3. Expected survival is 155%.
  4. Independence is proven regardless of sample size.
Answer and explanation

A. 0.8×0.75=0.60. Agreement with the null expectation in mean values does not prove independence without uncertainty and design information.

Revision and mastery

Systemic defense connects initial local perception, transmission of information, distal regulation and the measured outcome of a later challenge. SAR commonly involves NHP and SA signaling; classical microbial ISR often involves JA and ET-dependent priming. These routes may overlap and depend on context. Combined stress outcomes integrate hormone cross-talk with transport, carbon and nitrogen metabolism, redox balance and growth. Stress history can change the capacity for later response, but inheritance requires separate evidence.

Essential terminology and equations

  • SAR: systemic acquired resistance; ISR: induced systemic resistance in the root-microbe context.
  • ALD1 / SARD4 → Pip; FMO1 → NHP; SA-responsive NPR1 and TGA regulation → defense transcription.
  • Priming: altered challenge response, not necessarily a large constitutive output.
  • Factorial additive interaction: Y11 − Y10 − Y01 + Y00.
  • Population reduction (%) = 100 × (1 − treated/control).
  • Relative expression under comparable doubling efficiency = 2−ΔΔCt.
  • Resistance concerns attacker success; tolerance concerns performance at comparable burden.

Active recall

1. How does a two-stage SAR assay distinguish local and distal effects?

Apply the inducer locally, challenge previously untreated tissue later and compare matched mock controls.

2. Why does PR1 induction not itself establish disease resistance?

It is a regulatory marker; pathogen growth or another validated disease endpoint must be measured.

3. What are the major components of the lysine-to-NHP pathway?

ALD1 and SARD4 contribute to Pip production; FMO1 converts Pip into NHP.

4. What distinguishes an fmo1 biosynthetic defect from an npr1 response defect?

NHP may bypass the former; NPR1-dependent output remains compromised in the latter.

5. Why are donor–recipient grafts informative?

They separate requirements for producing/transmitting information from requirements for recipient response.

6. What is the classical distinction between SAR and microbial ISR?

Local pathogen induction versus beneficial root-microbe induction; typical SA/NHP versus JA/ET emphasis, with exceptions.

7. How are direct induction and priming separated experimentally?

Compare all four pretreatment-by-challenge groups and their kinetics.

8. Why may SA accumulation suppress one JA output without reducing JA concentration?

Cross-talk can regulate downstream transcription rather than biosynthesis.

9. How can ABA improve entry resistance yet weaken some post-entry defense?

Stomatal closure can restrict entry, while ABA-associated cross-talk may inhibit other defense outputs.

10. Why do drought and heat create a stomatal conflict?

Water conservation favors closure, while evaporative cooling may favor greater conductance.

11. How is an additive factorial interaction computed?

Combined minus A alone minus B alone plus control, on the stated response scale.

12. What distinguishes tolerance from resistance?

Tolerance preserves fitness at a given burden; resistance reduces attacker success.

13. Why is first-generation stress memory insufficient to establish transgenerational inheritance?

Direct exposure, seed provisioning and parental effects can explain the phenotype.

14. Why is recovery a distinct physiological stage?

Repair, restored transport, reoxygenation effects and growth can differ after initial survival.
