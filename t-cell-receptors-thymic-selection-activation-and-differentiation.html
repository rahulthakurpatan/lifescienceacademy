1. Why adaptive immunity needs T cells

A virus that has entered a living cell presents an unusual problem to the immune system. An antibody in the extracellular fluid may bind a virus particle before infection, but it cannot ordinarily enter the cytoplasm of the infected cell and survey every protein being synthesized there. A bacterium that survives within a macrophage presents a related problem. The macrophage has captured the organism, yet ordinary antimicrobial activity may not be enough to eliminate it. The immune system needs cells capable of recognizing information about what is happening inside another cell and changing that cell's behavior. T lymphocytes provide this form of communication. Some become specialized to direct the activities of other cells, whereas others become capable of eliminating selected abnormal cells. The biological challenge is not merely to recognize an infection; it is to coordinate a suitably targeted response without disrupting the many healthy cells surrounding the infection.

T lymphocytes are a class of white blood cells belonging to the adaptive immune system. Their name refers to the thymus, the specialized organ in which most conventional T cells mature. Like B cells, T cells descend from hematopoietic precursors and carry antigen receptors generated through somatic DNA rearrangement. Unlike a typical B cell, however, a conventional T cell usually recognizes antigen as part of a molecular complex displayed on the surface of another cell. In this complex a peptide fragment occupies a binding groove of a major histocompatibility complex molecule, abbreviated MHC. The receptor recognizes features of the peptide and of the presenting molecule together. A free peptide in extracellular fluid is therefore not generally equivalent to that same peptide displayed by the appropriate MHC allele. This distinction connects the study of T cells directly to antigen processing and presentation.

A T-cell response can be divided into several logically different operations. Receptor generation establishes a repertoire of cells with different binding specificities. Developmental selection reduces the risk that this repertoire will be useless or dangerously self-reactive. Peripheral activation recruits the relatively rare mature cells whose receptors can respond to a particular antigen in the proper context. Clonal expansion increases the number of those cells, while differentiation generates the functional properties needed for the immune response. These operations do not all happen at once or in the same organ. Thymic development precedes conventional naive T-cell activation in secondary lymphoid tissues; peripheral differentiation follows the integration of antigen recognition and other signals. Separating these events prevents a common error: treating the appearance of a mature antigen receptor as proof that a T cell is already an effector.

A naive T cell is a mature lymphocyte that has not yet undergone productive activation by its cognate antigen. The word cognate refers to the antigenic molecular complex recognized by that particular receptor. Naive cells are metabolically active and migrate through the body, but they have not yet acquired the full functional program associated with an immune response. A mature naive CD8 cell is not necessarily an armed cytotoxic T lymphocyte. Likewise, a mature naive CD4 cell is not necessarily committed to producing the cytokines characteristic of a particular helper lineage. In the peripheral lymphoid organs, professional antigen-presenting cells provide opportunities for these mature lymphocytes to encounter antigen. The development of receptor specificity and the subsequent acquisition of effector competence are thus separate processes even though both require signaling through the T-cell receptor.

Consider an infection that generates peptides A and B. Two mature lymphocytes may carry receptors that recognize A and B respectively when presented by suitable host MHC molecules. If an antigen-presenting cell displays A but not B, the first cell has the opportunity to receive a specific antigen signal. The second cell may encounter the same inflammatory environment but lacks the relevant receptor engagement. Inflammation is therefore important without being equivalent to antigen specificity. Conversely, presentation of peptide A by a cell that provides inadequate supporting signals may fail to produce robust activation of the first lymphocyte. This is an important organizational principle of adaptive immunity: the antigen receptor identifies a target, but several additional interactions determine the response to that target.

The need for a T-cell repertoire emerges from the fact that organisms cannot predict the exact sequences of all the foreign proteins they will encounter. Encoding a separate complete receptor gene for every possible antigen would be inefficient and still fail to anticipate new combinations of molecular features. Instead, a limited collection of inherited gene segments is rearranged in developing lymphocytes. Each cell samples a small part of a large space of possible receptors. The population as a whole thereby contains many specificities, and an immune challenge selectively recruits cells with useful ones. Because receptor generation is not directed by a particular future infection, some newly generated receptors will recognize self-derived ligands, others will fail to recognize host MHC, and still others will never encounter an effective ligand. The thymus addresses this consequence of the diversity-generating strategy.

The result is not a perfect division of all molecules into foreign and self. A receptor that responds to a microbial peptide can also respond to a different peptide with sufficiently similar surface chemistry. The repertoire must maintain some cross-reactivity to cover a vast universe of potential antigens with a finite number of cells. Developmental filters and peripheral regulatory signals reduce the consequences of unwanted recognition without eliminating the fundamental capacity to respond to multiple related molecular structures. These ideas help explain why a simple definition of T cells as cells that recognize foreign antigen is incomplete. It is more accurate to understand them as cells whose responses depend on the combination of receptor specificity, developmental history and signaling context.

2. How thymic function and MHC restriction were discovered

The importance of the thymus was not apparent from its anatomical appearance alone. Unlike a gland with an easily measured circulating secretion, its major contribution concerns the production and selection of a population of living cells. Jacques Miller’s landmark 1961 experiments on neonatal thymectomy showed why the timing of intervention mattered. Removal in newborn mice produced substantial deficiencies of immune function, supporting the idea that the organ is needed for development of immunologically competent lymphocytes. Removing an organ after a population of mature lymphocytes has already been established is not necessarily expected to produce the same immediate phenotype. This distinction between production of a cell population and continued operation of its mature descendants is useful when evaluating many experiments in developmental immunology.

The separation of thymus-associated and bone-marrow-associated lymphocyte functions also clarified an apparent paradox. An animal can contain lymphocytes and yet respond poorly to certain antigens. The number of white blood cells is not the same as the functional diversity of that population. B lymphocytes can produce antibody, but many effective antibody responses require assistance from antigen-specific helper T cells. An experimental deficiency in antibody production may consequently originate in the T-cell compartment rather than in the antibody-producing cells themselves. Restoration of a missing cellular population can reveal this dependence, provided that the experiment controls for differences in viability, engraftment, maturation and antigen exposure. A successful rescue is stronger evidence for a causal relationship than an isolated observation of reduced antibody levels.

A second major problem concerned the identity of the structure recognized by T cells. Antibody specificity had provided a natural starting model for adaptive recognition, but experiments with virus-specific cytotoxic lymphocytes revealed that antigen identity alone was insufficient. In 1974 Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty reported that mouse lymphocytes responding to lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus killed infected targets in an MHC-dependent manner. Cells bearing the virus could differ in susceptibility to the same cytotoxic population according to the genetic compatibility of their histocompatibility molecules. The results established an essential relationship between antigen specificity and host histocompatibility. Later studies refined the molecular explanation: conventional T-cell receptors engage the combined peptide–MHC ligand. The 1974 work is historical evidence for the restriction phenomenon, rather than a direct atomic-resolution demonstration of the modern receptor structure.

To reconstruct the reasoning, imagine cytotoxic cells induced in an animal of MHC type X. Test them against four target populations: infected X cells, uninfected X cells, infected Y cells and uninfected Y cells. If recognition depends solely on viral infection, both infected populations should be susceptible. If recognition depends solely on self MHC, both X populations should be susceptible. A high response specifically against infected X targets instead supports a requirement for both infection-associated information and appropriate MHC compatibility. This four-condition framework is an illustrative reconstruction, not the numerical dataset of the original article. The necessary controls are not interchangeable: uninfected matched targets reveal background activity, while infected mismatched targets test the role of histocompatibility.

An additional consideration is the health of target cells. A target population that releases a large amount of an intracellular marker without any immune cells present could falsely appear highly susceptible to cytotoxicity. Measurements of spontaneous release and a maximum-release control allow the contribution of experimental cytotoxicity to be separated from background and assay capacity. Genetic matching also needs to be interpreted carefully. Different inbred strains can differ at many genes besides the MHC. Comparisons involving appropriate genetic combinations or congenic strains help narrow the causal locus. The broader lesson is that identifying a genetically associated phenomenon and identifying the responsible molecular structure require different kinds of evidence.

MHC restriction explains why replacing a presenting allele can affect recognition even when a peptide has the same amino-acid sequence. Different MHC molecules may bind the peptide with different stability, expose different parts of it, or create different contact surfaces for the TCR. Failure to activate a T cell after an allele substitution could therefore arise from reduced peptide loading, altered receptor recognition, or both. Measurements of peptide–MHC abundance and receptor engagement can help discriminate these possibilities. In exam questions, a peptide is sometimes described as having been supplied to two cells as though this guarantees equal presentation. That inference is unjustified unless the problem explicitly controls peptide binding and surface display.

The discovery of the receptor molecules and their associated signaling components added a mechanistic layer to the genetic observations. Researchers could distinguish proteins responsible for binding antigenic complexes from proteins responsible for transmitting a signal. This distinction is analogous to the division of labor seen in other multimeric signaling receptors, but the variable antigen-binding chains of a TCR and the invariant CD3 chains have a particularly important developmental relationship. A cell may synthesize an antigen-binding chain yet fail to assemble a complete surface complex or transmit an adequate intracellular signal. An experiment measuring one protein cannot therefore establish the function of the entire receptor complex.

3. The αβ T-cell receptor and CD3 complex

A conventional αβ T-cell receptor contains one alpha chain and one beta chain. Each chain has an extracellular variable domain and a constant domain, followed by a membrane-spanning region and a short intracellular tail. The variable domains have the immunoglobulin-like fold encountered in the study of antibodies. Their surface loops include complementarity-determining regions, commonly called CDRs, which collectively create the binding interface. Variable does not mean that the chain continuously changes sequence during an ordinary immune response; rather, different lymphocyte clones possess different rearranged variable-region genes. A mature clone transmits its receptor rearrangements to its daughter cells during proliferation.

The alpha and beta chains form a heterodimer, generally stabilized by noncovalent interactions and an interchain disulfide bond in conventional receptors. The arrangement positions the two variable domains close together so that both participate in the recognition surface. This is structurally different from the usual secreted IgG antibody, which carries two equivalent antigen-binding sites in a heavy-chain and light-chain assembly. A conventional TCR is a membrane-associated receptor with a single antigen-binding site per alpha-beta heterodimer, not an antibody molecule that has simply been attached to a T-cell membrane. It is not ordinarily released as a secreted effector and it does not possess an antibody Fc region for recruiting complement or Fc-receptor-bearing cells.

The peptide–MHC surface interacts with CDR loops contributed by both alpha and beta chains. A useful introductory model is that germline-encoded CDR1 and CDR2 loops often contribute substantially to MHC contacts, whereas the more junctionally diverse CDR3 loops often make important peptide contacts. This model explains why gene-segment choice and junctional variation influence recognition differently. It is not an absolute rule for every TCR–ligand structure: individual loops can contact peptide, MHC, or both, and different receptors adopt somewhat different docking arrangements. A student should therefore distinguish a broadly useful tendency from a universal stereochemical assignment. The essential principle is that the receptor recognizes a composite three-dimensional interface.

The short cytoplasmic tails of TCR alpha and beta chains do not contain the extensive signaling machinery needed to convert ligand binding into a cellular response. They assemble with invariant components of the CD3 complex: CD3 gamma-epsilon and delta-epsilon heterodimers, together with a zeta-chain homodimer in the conventional arrangement. Charged residues within their transmembrane regions help stabilize interactions among these proteins in the otherwise hydrophobic membrane environment. These associations allow receptor specificity and intracellular signal transmission to be supplied by distinct polypeptide chains. Disrupting assembly can reduce surface receptor expression even if the genes encoding the antigen-binding chains have rearranged normally.

CD3 and zeta cytoplasmic domains contain immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs, known as ITAMs. An ITAM includes two conserved tyrosine-containing sequence elements with appropriate spacing. In the usual αβ TCR complex there are ten ITAMs in total: one on each of four CD3 gamma, delta and epsilon chains and three on each of the two zeta chains. Phosphorylation of these motifs creates binding sites for intracellular signaling proteins. A TCR-binding event outside the cell can thus produce a change in the phosphorylation state of proteins inside the cell. This process does not require an antigen molecule to pass through the plasma membrane or enter the nucleus.

A co-receptor must not be confused with a component of the antigen receptor itself. CD4 binds a relatively nonpolymorphic region of MHC class II, and CD8 interacts with MHC class I. Their binding helps bring associated Lck, a Src-family tyrosine kinase, near the engaged TCR complex. CD4 and CD8 therefore contribute to signaling as well as to the relationship between T-cell lineages and MHC classes. They are not the molecules that generate the vast peptide-specific repertoire. Many T cells share an identical CD4 or CD8 co-receptor while expressing distinct TCR sequences. An experiment that detects CD4 identifies a phenotypic population, not the precise antigen specificity of its members.

The difference between the TCR and the BCR can be understood through the accessibility of their usual ligands. A BCR can bind a surface of a native protein, a carbohydrate determinant or another accessible molecular structure without first requiring a peptide–MHC display. Antigen-specific membrane immunoglobulin is coupled to Ig alpha and Ig beta signaling chains. A conventional TCR usually samples processed peptide information through an MHC platform and is coupled to CD3 and zeta chains. Both systems therefore separate binding and signaling, but their recognition contexts and effector outcomes differ. The TCR repertoire is selected to operate with host MHC; conventional B-cell receptors do not undergo that same MHC-restriction selection.

This difference has an immediate consequence for interpreting protein denaturation experiments. A conformational B-cell epitope may be lost when a protein unfolds even if the peptide sequence is unchanged. A T-cell epitope derived from the same protein can remain recognizable after processing if the relevant peptide is still generated and displayed. Conversely, a change that alters a protease cleavage site or an MHC anchor residue may prevent T-cell recognition despite preservation of the native surface recognized by a B cell. Thus the terms B-cell and T-cell epitope are not interchangeable descriptions of the same molecular object. They refer to structures that become available to different recognition mechanisms.

Binding energy is distributed across many noncovalent contacts rather than arising from a single irreversible bond. Hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, van der Waals contacts and hydrophobic effects may contribute to receptor–ligand stability. The loss of one contact can weaken binding, alter the preferred orientation or affect a signaling threshold. Binding affinity describes the thermodynamics of a defined interaction under specified conditions, whereas receptor sensitivity in a cell also depends on co-receptors, the density and organization of molecules, biochemical amplification and the cell's state. A low equilibrium dissociation constant is not by itself a complete prediction of T-cell activation. The ligand must participate in productive receptor engagement in a cellular system that is capable of responding.

The membrane requirement also explains why soluble engineered TCRs used as experimental reagents are not counterexamples to the physiological statement that conventional TCRs are membrane receptors. Removing or modifying membrane-anchoring sequences can produce a soluble binding protein for experimental purposes, but the normal T lymphocyte uses its membrane-localized complex to communicate with cells bearing ligands. A soluble reagent may report binding while lacking the ability to initiate a T-cell intracellular signaling cascade. Binding studies and cellular response studies answer distinct questions.

4. Receptor diversity and the limits of the genetic repertoire

The inherited DNA of a developing T cell contains multiple gene segments from which a variable region can be constructed. The conventional beta-chain locus contributes variable, diversity and joining segments, called V, D and J. The alpha-chain locus uses V and J segments but not a D segment. This arrangement resembles the contrast between immunoglobulin heavy- and light-chain genes discussed in antibody diversity. The rearrangements are somatic: they take place in particular developing lymphocytes rather than producing a new inherited receptor sequence in every cell of the organism.

During beta-chain rearrangement a D and J segment are joined, followed by joining of a V segment to the resulting DJ unit. The RAG1 and RAG2 proteins recognize suitable recombination signal sequences and initiate the regulated DNA cleavage associated with antigen receptor assembly. Cellular DNA repair machinery completes joining. Segment combinations provide diversity, but the junctions themselves are also variable because nucleotides can be removed or added during the joining process. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase, or TdT, contributes nontemplated nucleotide addition. The encoded amino-acid sequence of a junction can therefore differ between cells that selected the same V, D and J segments.

A productive rearrangement is one that can generate an appropriately expressed functional polypeptide. Random junctional changes can disrupt the reading frame or introduce a stop codon. It follows that not every rearrangement creates a useful receptor chain, and not every precursor cell will successfully negotiate its developmental checkpoints. The ability to attempt additional permissible rearrangements provides opportunities for successful development, but cells that do not acquire required receptor signals eventually fail to survive. The observed mature repertoire is therefore not a direct census of every theoretical recombination outcome. It reflects molecular generation followed by biological selection.

A productive beta chain initially associates with a surrogate partner called pre-T alpha and with CD3 signaling components. This pre-TCR is a developmental signaling complex, rather than a fully formed antigen receptor with the eventual alpha-chain specificity. Its activity provides evidence that the cell has generated a functional beta chain and supports survival, expansion and progression of the precursor. The resulting daughter cells can subsequently undertake alpha-chain rearrangements. Because a population of descendants may share a beta rearrangement yet acquire different alpha chains, receptor diversity can increase further at this stage. Beta selection is thus both a quality-control checkpoint and an important point of population amplification.

The alpha-chain locus can undergo successive rearrangement attempts during the period when thymocytes express both CD4 and CD8. Signaling associated with successful selection contributes to termination of this rearrangement program. The general principle of one dominant receptor specificity per lymphocyte clone is useful for understanding clonal selection, but it should not be treated as an exceptionless law of alpha-chain expression. Beta-chain allelic exclusion is relatively stringent; expression of two alpha chains can occur in some T cells. This is a biologically meaningful caveat, not evidence that receptor assembly is completely unregulated. In a basic counting question, assume one productive alpha and one productive beta chain per cell only when the model states that assumption.

A simple combinatorial model illustrates the origin of repertoire size. Suppose an imaginary locus contains 20 usable alpha V segments and 10 alpha J segments; independently, the beta locus has 15 usable V segments, 2 D segments and 8 J segments. If every permitted combination is equally available, alpha has 200 choices and beta has 240 choices. Pairing these independently gives 48,000 possible combinations before junctional diversity. These are deliberately hypothetical segment counts, not a claim about a human or mouse germline locus. The product principle counts ways of choosing components, whereas a real repertoire also depends on gene usage biases, productive joining, chain compatibility, selection and the number of cells produced.

A real immune system does not need to contain one lymphocyte for every mathematically possible receptor. The number of theoretical sequences is vastly larger than the number of T cells in an individual. Cross-reactivity allows a given receptor to respond to more than one related ligand, and different receptors may recognize the same microbial peptide–MHC combination. Repertoire breadth should therefore be described in terms of coverage, overlap and functional recognition rather than a simplistic one-sequence–one-antigen mapping. In infection, the number of responding cells reflects both initial precursor frequency and the subsequent survival and expansion of the responding clones.

Unlike activated B cells undergoing somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination, conventional T cells do not ordinarily diversify their TCRs through antigen-driven somatic hypermutation or change an antibody-like constant-region isotype. Their response generally amplifies the existing receptor rearrangement. Selection among clones with different responsiveness can change the composition of the population, which is distinct from mutating the receptor sequence within a clone. This distinction matters in lineage-tracing experiments: sharing a rearranged receptor sequence can provide evidence of common ancestry, although convergent recombination can occasionally generate similar or identical sequences independently. Sequence identity alone should be interpreted with the resolution and assumptions of the method used.

A loss of RAG activity affects both B-cell and T-cell receptor generation because both lineages require rearrangement. In contrast, a defect in a T-cell-specific developmental signal may primarily affect the T-cell compartment. These perturbations can be separated by examining B-cell development, T-cell precursor stages, receptor rearrangement status and surface protein expression. Counting only mature T cells would identify a phenotype but not the location of the developmental failure. A strong mechanistic explanation identifies the earliest stage at which the mutant diverges from its matched control and tests whether downstream failure follows from the proposed defect.

5. The thymus as an organized developmental environment

The thymus is a primary lymphoid organ because it supports development of lymphocytes before their ordinary antigen-driven responses in peripheral tissues. Bone-marrow-derived progenitors migrate into the organ, where local stromal cells provide contact-dependent signals and soluble factors that shape the T-cell developmental program. This is different from a lymph node, whose principal immunological role is to bring mature lymphocytes together with antigen and antigen-presenting cells. The two organs both contain T cells and antigen-presenting structures, yet their biological objectives are different. One builds and selects a repertoire; the other recruits mature members of that repertoire for a response.

The thymic cortex and medulla provide distinguishable microenvironments. Immature thymocytes are particularly abundant in the cortex. Cortical thymic epithelial cells supply essential signals and peptide–MHC ligands involved in positive selection of conventional alpha-beta thymocytes. The medulla contains medullary thymic epithelial cells and other antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells, which help shape self-tolerance and late stages of maturation. This is a useful spatial model rather than a strict statement that every selection event occurs exclusively in one compartment. Some deletion of strongly self-reactive cells occurs in the cortex, and cellular migration and antigen distribution influence which selecting interactions are encountered.

Early conventional thymocytes are termed double negative because they lack the surface co-receptors CD4 and CD8. This designation describes a phenotype rather than a complete definition of a molecular stage. During early thymic development, lineage commitment and receptor-gene rearrangement occur. Notch signaling from the thymic environment, particularly interactions involving Notch1 and its ligand DLL4, is important for supporting T-lineage specification. Cytokines such as IL-7 contribute to survival and proliferation of appropriate precursor populations. Neither a generic cytokine signal nor the mere presence of a thymic epithelial cell establishes that the developing lymphocyte has made a productive receptor rearrangement.

A major early checkpoint is beta selection. A developing cell that generates an expressible beta chain can form a pre-TCR complex and receive signals permitting progression. Cells that fail to generate a functional beta chain lack this usual route of developmental support. After beta selection, progenitors proliferate and progress through stages that culminate in expression of both CD4 and CD8. These double-positive cells rearrange the alpha-chain locus and express complete alpha-beta TCR complexes. They are now able to test the interaction of their newly generated receptor with peptide–MHC ligands in the thymus. A cell at this stage is still undergoing developmental selection, not responding as a fully differentiated effector.

Conventional T cells that successfully complete selection generally reduce expression of one of the two co-receptors and become single positive. Recognition of MHC class I is associated with development toward the CD8 lineage, whereas recognition of MHC class II is associated with the CD4 lineage. These associations are maintained by developmental gene-regulatory programs and are not simply determined by an extracellular molecule physically removing the unwanted co-receptor. Thymocytes integrate signals, change expression of transcription factors and stabilize a lineage-appropriate program. The mature single-positive phenotype reflects an accumulated developmental outcome rather than an instantaneous biochemical switch.

Flow cytometry offers a way to investigate this progression. If fluorescent antibodies recognize CD4 and CD8 on individual cells, plotting the two fluorescence intensities can distinguish double-negative, double-positive and single-positive populations. A high proportion of double-positive cells is expected in a normal thymus compared with the mature circulating T-cell compartment. A genetic defect preventing beta selection may reduce the production of double-positive cells, whereas a defect affecting positive selection may leave a substantial double-positive compartment but greatly reduce mature single-positive cells. Interpretation depends on the age of the animal, the genetic background, the definition of viable cells and the gating strategy used to exclude non-T-cell precursors. A plot does not directly show the molecular defect without these additional considerations.

The thymus also supports gamma-delta T-cell development. Gamma-delta receptors contain gamma and delta chains rather than alpha and beta chains, and many gamma-delta T-cell populations recognize ligands without conventional peptide–MHC restriction. They can contribute to tissue surveillance and rapid responses, especially at epithelial barriers. Their developmental routes and ligand recognition are heterogeneous, so the conventional double-positive selection framework should not be extended to all gamma-delta cells without qualification. The present chapter focuses on the dominant textbook model of conventional alpha-beta T-cell selection, while recognizing that the T-cell compartment contains additional populations with specialized developmental and recognition strategies.

6. Positive selection: learning to recognize host MHC

Imagine a developing thymocyte that has just assembled a complete alpha-beta receptor. The cell has a receptor, but that accomplishment says nothing about whether the receptor can engage an antigenic complex displayed by its own organism. Every mature conventional T cell depends on the presenting molecules available in the host. A receptor that can recognize only an unrelated MHC allele, or that cannot engage any of the host's peptide–MHC complexes sufficiently to generate a survival signal, will ordinarily contribute little to that host's defensive repertoire. Positive selection addresses this problem by testing newly formed receptors against peptide–MHC complexes supplied by the thymic environment. The term positive describes selection for survival and continued differentiation, not selection for cells that respond to a specific foreign antigen.

The key participants in the usual cortical selection process are the double-positive thymocyte and the cortical thymic epithelial cell. The latter expresses class I and class II MHC and presents a variety of self-derived peptides. Interactions between these molecules and the newly expressed thymocyte TCR generate signals whose character depends on the ligand and receptor combination. A weak or moderate productive interaction can support survival and developmental progression. Without an adequate signal, a large fraction of thymocytes die by apoptosis, a process often called death by neglect. Death by neglect differs mechanistically from deliberate deletion after a strong self-reactive signal, although both reduce the number of cells that reach the mature compartment.

A conventional textbook diagram portrays the range of recognition as a horizontal scale with no effective recognition at one end and excessively strong self-recognition at the other. A region between these extremes represents signals compatible with positive selection. The usefulness of this diagram is its explanation of the apparent contradiction that a T cell needs some ability to recognize self-derived molecular complexes while excessive self-reactivity must be constrained. The diagram becomes misleading if its axis is labeled with a single universal affinity value. Selection is influenced by the amount of displayed peptide, dwell time of receptor interactions, receptor and co-receptor availability, the developmental stage of the cell and the type of presenting cell. A threshold drawn in an exam problem should therefore be interpreted according to the model specified in that problem.

One consequence of positive selection is the relationship between class of MHC and co-receptor expression. At the double-positive stage a thymocyte can use either co-receptor. Engagement involving class II MHC and CD4 generally supports development into a CD4 single-positive T cell, whereas class I recognition and CD8 generally support the CD8 lineage. The interactions are not simply a final test performed after the cell has irrevocably decided its phenotype. Signals help establish the lineage-specific regulatory program. Once the cell differentiates, its co-receptor contributes to signaling in the context of the same MHC class encountered during selection. This relationship explains why a mature conventional CD4 helper cell responds chiefly to class II peptide complexes and why a mature conventional CD8 cell recognizes chiefly class I complexes.

A useful experimental perturbation is selective loss of class II expression on cortical thymic epithelial cells. In the simplest model this removes the normal positively selecting ligands for conventional class II-restricted thymocytes. The predicted result is reduced production of conventional CD4 single-positive cells, not a general abolition of all lymphocytes. If class I expression and other essential developmental support remain intact, conventional CD8 selection can continue. The reciprocal prediction follows for selective loss of class I on the appropriate selecting cells. A whole-animal knockout is more difficult to interpret, because the missing molecule may affect selection in the thymus and antigen recognition in peripheral tissues simultaneously. A cell-type-specific perturbation narrows the question to the developmental environment.

It is also useful to distinguish the cell that supplies the selecting ligand from the cell whose fate is selected. If class II MHC is expressed on a cortical epithelial cell, the receptor on the neighboring thymocyte responds to it. The epithelial cell does not thereby become a CD4 helper cell; its contribution is presentation. In a chimera, host and donor components can have different genotypes. The prediction about the repertoire depends on which cellular compartment supplies the relevant selecting ligand, not just which genotype is assigned to the entire experimental animal. This distinction between lymphocyte-intrinsic genotype and stromal genotype is a recurring pattern in developmental biology.

Positive selection should not be confused with clonal selection after immunization. In both cases receptor-dependent signals affect the fate of lymphocytes, but the biological objectives and settings differ. Positive selection shapes immature thymocytes before exposure to the particular foreign antigen that may eventually trigger them. Peripheral clonal selection recruits mature lymphocytes because a cognate antigen appears in an appropriate activation context. A thymocyte need not have seen a viral peptide during development in order for its mature descendant to respond to that virus later. Weak recognition of a selecting self peptide–MHC complex can be compatible with stronger recognition of a different peptide displayed by a suitable host MHC molecule.

The importance of positive selection persists after thymic exit in a more subtle form. Mature naive T cells receive homeostatic signals that contribute to survival, including interactions with self peptide–MHC and cytokines such as IL-7. These signals should not be interpreted as evidence that every naive cell is continuously mounting a full immune response against self. A maintenance signal in a mature lymphocyte and an activating signal delivered during infection have different contexts and consequences. The selected repertoire is thus sustained as a population ready to respond, rather than as a permanently activated army of effectors.

7. Negative selection and the origin of central tolerance

Producing cells capable of recognizing the host's MHC solves only half the developmental problem. The same gene rearrangement process that generates potentially protective receptors also creates receptors capable of reacting strongly to self-derived ligands. If these cells were allowed to mature without constraint, ordinary host proteins could become targets of destructive or inflammatory responses. Negative selection is one mechanism that reduces this risk during lymphocyte development. In the thymus, recognition of some self peptide–MHC complexes with sufficiently strong or appropriate signaling induces programmed cell death of the developing T cell. The clone is thereby removed before it joins the conventional mature repertoire.

A central question is how a thymus can test receptors against proteins normally expressed in distant organs. The thymic medulla is important because its epithelial cells can express a range of tissue-associated antigens that would otherwise be encountered mainly in specialized peripheral tissues. The transcriptional regulator AIRE helps promote expression of many such antigens in medullary thymic epithelial cells. Other mechanisms, including the transcriptional regulator FEZF2, contribute to broader self-antigen representation. The thymus does not literally contain a complete replica of every cell and physiological state in the body. Rather, the expression and presentation of diverse self-derived molecules improves the likelihood that potentially harmful recognition will be detected during development.

Thymic dendritic cells can participate in this process by presenting self antigens, including antigens acquired from other sources. Negative selection is thus not a single interaction between one thymocyte type and one exclusively responsible presenting cell. The spatial division of the cortex and medulla provides a helpful introductory map, but deletion can take place at multiple developmental stages and locations. The identity of the presenting cell influences what antigens are displayed and how the signal is interpreted. For an examination question comparing a cortical epithelial cell and a medullary epithelial cell, the dominant associations are positive selection and broad self-antigen display, respectively. The statement that negative selection never occurs in the cortex would be too absolute.

Strong self-recognition does not inevitably cause deletion in every developmental context. Some self-reactive CD4-lineage thymocytes instead enter the regulatory T-cell lineage, acquiring a program associated with the transcription factor Foxp3. This is a form of developmental diversion in which the cell's eventual function contributes to immune regulation rather than conventional effector activity. Both deletion and regulatory lineage differentiation help manage self-reactivity, but they do so by different cellular mechanisms. Deletion reduces the number of a potentially reactive clone; regulatory differentiation retains cells with a specialized functional program. It would therefore be incorrect to say that any measurable affinity for self makes thymic survival impossible.

The distinction between positive and negative selection can be clarified through three hypothetical receptors. Receptor A fails to engage available host peptide–MHC ligands sufficiently to support development. Its thymocyte is expected to die by neglect. Receptor B engages a selecting self complex within an appropriate range and receives a survival signal without a deletion-inducing encounter. Its thymocyte can progress. Receptor C engages a strongly self-reactive ligand in a context that induces deletion. Its thymocyte is expected to undergo apoptosis, although alternative outcomes can occur in special contexts. All three receptors were generated by similar molecular processes, but their cellular fates differ because of the ligand interactions and signaling environment. The difference is not that one cell's DNA rearrangement is necessarily structurally defective.

Central tolerance refers to processes that establish tolerance during development in primary lymphoid organs. Negative selection is an important part of central T-cell tolerance, but it is not equivalent to all tolerance. Some self-reactive cells escape thymic selection, whether because an antigen was not adequately represented, its level was too low, or the interactions fell outside a deletion-promoting context. The repertoire also needs to preserve cells capable of responding to foreign antigens that may resemble self ligands. Peripheral mechanisms therefore remain necessary, including regulation of costimulation, functional unresponsiveness and suppression by regulatory populations. The next chapter considers these peripheral controls in greater detail. Here the crucial point is that the thymic checkpoint lowers risk rather than certifying that every exported T cell is incapable of self-reactivity.

A genetic loss of AIRE supplies a useful way to reason about the specificity of a developmental failure. The simplest prediction is that display of a subset of tissue-associated self antigens is reduced and that deletion or diversion of particular reactive thymocytes becomes less efficient. AIRE deficiency should not be equated with complete loss of all class I or class II MHC. Many self peptides can still be presented, and positive selection can still operate. The phenotype is therefore different from a mutation that abolishes peptide–MHC recognition throughout the thymus. This reasoning illustrates how molecular specificity determines the scope of an immunological defect. A broad immune phenotype does not necessarily imply that the most general antigen-recognition machinery has been eliminated.

An experiment comparing the frequency of a defined self-reactive clonotype before and after selection can help assess deletion. Suppose a receptor-transgenic population develops in two otherwise comparable thymic environments, one displaying a relevant self antigen and one lacking it. A reduction of viable cells expressing that clonotype in the antigen-expressing environment is consistent with deletion. To distinguish deletion from failure of positive selection or altered migration, measurements at successive stages and assays for cell death are helpful. If the same clonotype instead appears among Foxp3-positive cells, the result suggests diversion into a regulatory program. The data must be interpreted with the knowledge that transgenic expression can change precursor frequency and developmental timing compared with a normal diverse repertoire.

Selection does not measure whether a molecule is foreign by examining its origin directly. A TCR cannot read a chemical label that identifies a peptide as self or microbial. It responds to molecular features and associated signals. Self versus nonself discrimination is an emergent property of receptor generation, antigen presentation, developmental selection and contextual regulation. This is why a structurally similar microbial peptide and host peptide can sometimes be recognized by overlapping populations of T cells. Such overlap does not mean that every infection will produce autoimmunity. It does show why developmental deletion and peripheral controls must be considered together when explaining immune protection and its failures.

8. From double-positive thymocytes to distinct mature lineages

The relationship between CD4 and CD8 expression and MHC restriction is one of the most frequently tested principles of T-cell development. An immature double-positive cell expresses both proteins, but a conventional mature T cell usually maintains one dominant co-receptor phenotype. The two co-receptors help couple recognition to intracellular signaling by interacting with distinct MHC classes and bringing Lck into the relevant molecular neighborhood. During development, the nature and persistence of these signals influence transcriptional programs that stabilize the lineage choice. The simple rule that class II selects CD4 cells and class I selects CD8 cells is a summary of the usual conventional outcome; it is not an explanation of the entire decision mechanism.

At an introductory mechanistic level, transcription factors are proteins that influence gene expression by interacting with regulatory DNA or associated molecular machinery. The acquisition of a lineage-specific transcriptional program makes the difference between transient changes in marker expression and a relatively stable developmental identity. ThPOK is associated with the conventional CD4-lineage program, whereas Runx3 contributes to the CD8-lineage program. These regulators participate in networks rather than acting as isolated on–off switches. A mutation that changes one such network may yield an unexpected correspondence between receptor restriction and co-receptor phenotype. Detailed lineage-regulatory circuitry is not needed to apply the central NET-level distinction between the two conventional populations.

A flow cytometry experiment illustrates why surface phenotype and biological function must be separated. Consider four populations measured after staining for CD4 and CD8: a low-low population, a high-high population, a CD4-high CD8-low population, and a CD4-low CD8-high population. In a thymic sample these patterns can correspond to double-negative, double-positive and single-positive stages. In a sample from a peripheral lymph node, the same staining patterns need not represent exactly the same developmental stages, because mature populations and non-T cells may occupy some of the regions. Gating on viable cells and relevant T-lineage markers is necessary. Surface marker staining identifies the phenotype being measured; additional evidence is required to infer the developmental path by which the cells reached it.

The distribution of thymocytes reflects both production and loss of cells. A large double-positive compartment does not mean that most of its cells will survive. A compartment may contain many cells because it receives a large influx from an earlier proliferating stage, because cells remain in it for a long period, or because exit is blocked. Similarly, a small compartment can represent rapid transit rather than a failure to generate cells. In a developmental mutant, compare absolute numbers as well as percentages. If the double-positive population collapses, the percentage of remaining single-positive cells could increase even though their absolute number falls. Percentages are conditional on the denominator and must not be equated automatically with production rates.

Suppose a control thymus contains 100 million viable cells, 80 percent double positive and 10 percent CD4 single positive. A mutant thymus contains 20 million viable cells, 40 percent double positive and 20 percent CD4 single positive. The CD4 single-positive percentage has doubled, but the absolute number has fallen from 10 million to 4 million. The double-positive population has fallen from 80 million to 8 million. These hypothetical counts suggest a severe change in total thymic cellularity and in the developmental distribution, but they do not on their own prove a specific molecular lesion. Proliferation, apoptosis, immigration and export might all influence the measured populations. A good Part C answer must distinguish the arithmetic conclusion from the mechanistic hypothesis.

Another useful comparison is between thymic maturation and peripheral effector differentiation. A single-positive thymocyte is progressing toward a mature T-cell identity. A naive CD4 cell that later becomes Th1 or Th2 has undergone a different type of differentiation in response to antigen and contextual signals. A developing CD8 single-positive cell is not identical to a CD8 cytotoxic effector induced during infection. Both use gene-regulatory programs, but the first establishes the receptor-restriction lineage and the second equips a mature lymphocyte for a particular immune task. The presence of CD4 alone does not distinguish an unactivated helper precursor from a mature helper effector or a regulatory T cell. Additional markers and functional assays are needed.

Exported conventional naive T cells circulate through blood and secondary lymphoid organs. Homing molecules and chemokine receptors such as CD62L and CCR7 contribute to entry and localization in lymph nodes. The importance of migration can be understood through the rarity of antigen-specific precursors. If each naive lymphocyte remained fixed at a random peripheral location, an antigen-bearing dendritic cell would have limited probability of encountering the appropriate clone. Organized lymphoid tissues bring potentially responding cells and relevant presenters into proximity. A mutation affecting homing can therefore reduce immune activation even if the TCR, CD3 and intracellular signaling machinery are normal. This is an example of an immune defect caused by failure of cellular organization rather than failure of molecular recognition.

The thymus decreases in functional tissue with age, a process commonly called thymic involution. This does not mean mature T cells disappear immediately in adults. The peripheral compartment is maintained by survival and homeostatic proliferation as well as ongoing thymic output, and memory populations reflect previous antigen encounters. A reduction in generation of new naive cells can affect repertoire renewal differently from an acute loss of mature circulating cells. In an experimental comparison of young and older animals, thymic size, naive cell abundance and responses to previously encountered antigens therefore measure different aspects of T-cell biology. Age is a relevant environmental and physiological variable when interpreting selection and output.

9. Signal 1: converting peptide–MHC recognition into intracellular information

A mature naive T lymphocyte in a lymph node repeatedly contacts other cells. Most encounters do not result in productive engagement by its receptor because the molecular features of the displayed peptide–MHC complexes are not suitable for that particular clone. An antigen-presenting cell that carries the cognate peptide–MHC combination creates the possibility of a specific signal. Adhesion molecules help maintain contact, and the plasma membranes establish a structured interface often called an immunological synapse. This interface permits interactions involving antigen receptors, co-receptors, costimulatory molecules and adhesion molecules. Its organization is dynamic; it should not be pictured as a permanent physical junction comparable to a desmosome.

Productive engagement of the TCR initiates a sequence of changes in protein phosphorylation. Lck can phosphorylate tyrosine residues in CD3 and zeta ITAMs, allowing the kinase ZAP-70 to bind phosphorylated motifs through its SH2 domains. Activation of ZAP-70 promotes phosphorylation of adaptor proteins such as LAT and assembly of downstream signaling machinery. An adaptor is a protein that helps organize interacting molecules without necessarily catalyzing a reaction itself. In this setting, adaptor-dependent assembly places enzymes and other partners near the membrane receptor complex and increases the efficiency with which the signal can be transmitted. The TCR is therefore not a receptor tyrosine kinase in the same sense as an insulin receptor. Its antigen-binding chains depend on associated signaling chains and cytoplasmic kinases.

Downstream signaling connects receptor engagement to pathways already familiar from general cell signaling. Phospholipase C gamma 1 can hydrolyze phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate to produce inositol trisphosphate and diacylglycerol. Inositol trisphosphate promotes release of calcium from intracellular stores, and sustained calcium entry can help activate the calcium-dependent phosphatase calcineurin. Calcineurin contributes to activation of NFAT, a transcriptional regulator important for T-cell responses. Diacylglycerol contributes to signaling through other pathways, including protein kinase C theta-associated signaling and pathways leading to AP-1 and NF-kappa B activity. The important principle is convergence: different branches of a receptor-generated signal cooperate to alter the transcription of genes involved in activation, survival and proliferation.

Calcium does not carry information about the exact amino-acid sequence of the peptide. It serves as an intracellular second messenger whose temporal and spatial behavior contributes to the interpretation of receptor engagement. Two different TCR clones can use similar calcium signaling machinery while recognizing different antigens. The specificity resides principally in the upstream receptor interaction, whereas downstream pathways provide common molecular processes through which that information changes cell behavior. Consequently, experimentally raising intracellular calcium can bypass part of the receptor pathway without reproducing the complete biological specificity of the initial interaction. A rescue by a downstream activator can help position a defect in a pathway, but it is not evidence that the original receptor binding has been restored.

Phosphorylation reactions use ATP as a phosphate donor, and phosphatases reverse the modifications by hydrolysis. These coupled reactions permit regulated information flow rather than a one-time structural change. If every phosphorylated signaling protein remained phosphorylated indefinitely, a transient receptor encounter could create a persistent response unrelated to the current extracellular conditions. Conversely, if phosphatases removed phosphate groups faster than kinases could produce them under all conditions, signaling would fail. The balance between addition and removal of phosphate therefore helps determine signal magnitude and duration. This is a useful connection between immune signaling and the principles of enzyme kinetics and nonequilibrium biological regulation.

An isolated defect in CD3 signaling can be distinguished conceptually from loss of antigen recognition. Suppose a mutant TCR retains normal peptide–MHC binding but its associated zeta chain has alterations that prevent phosphorylation of its ITAM tyrosines. If the rest of the complex remains assembled, the antigen can still be physically recognized while one major part of signal transmission is impaired. Other CD3 ITAMs may retain some signaling capacity, so a mutation in zeta should not automatically be described as eliminating every possible signal. The magnitude of the defect depends on which motifs are altered and the functional redundancy remaining in the complex. A binding assay alone could miss the signaling defect, whereas an early phosphorylation assay and a later proliferation measurement might reveal its consequences.

The strength and duration of TCR signaling also depend on ligand density, interaction lifetimes and the cell's responsiveness. A simplified kinetic proofreading model proposes that productive signaling requires completion of intermediate steps while receptor engagement persists. Very brief interactions may terminate before these steps are completed; longer or repeated productive interactions can increase the probability of reaching downstream signaling states. This model provides an intuitive reason why small differences in ligand interaction kinetics can produce larger differences in cellular output. It is not a claim that every T-cell response is determined by a fixed number of steps or by dwell time alone. Co-receptors, feedback and receptor organization complicate the real system.

A researcher can compare early and late responses to distinguish mechanisms. An assay of calcium mobilization or proximal protein phosphorylation examines events that occur relatively soon after receptor stimulation. Expression of activation-associated surface molecules and production of IL-2 reflect later cellular processes. Cell division requires subsequent metabolic and cell-cycle changes and occurs over a longer timescale. A mutation that preserves early calcium responses but prevents division could affect a downstream transcriptional pathway, costimulation, cytokine responsiveness or metabolic adaptation. It would be premature to conclude that the TCR cannot bind its antigen. Conversely, a failure to detect one early signal does not prove that no other branch of signaling is operating.

Signal 1 is therefore best understood as the antigen-specific component of activation. It supplies information about the peptide–MHC ligand but is interpreted in the context of additional inputs. During thymic selection, receptor signaling operates in a developmental environment that can induce survival, deletion or lineage differentiation. In a mature naive cell, the same general machinery participates in an activation decision. Differences in developmental state, gene expression and available supporting signals explain why receptor engagement can lead to very different outcomes in these two situations. The receptor is part of a decision system, not a universal command that always causes proliferation.

10. Signal 2 and the context of antigen recognition

A mature naive T cell faces a decision that cannot be made from antigen specificity alone. Many cells display self-derived peptides, and some foreign proteins are harmless. A receptor capable of recognizing a molecular complex has no inherent information about whether the presenting cell has encountered microbial products or tissue damage. Additional signals supplied by antigen-presenting cells help convey this context. The best-known activating costimulatory interaction involves CD28 on the T cell and B7-family ligands, CD80 and CD86, on the presenting cell. In standard descriptions of naive conventional T-cell activation this interaction is called signal 2, whereas engagement of the TCR by cognate peptide–MHC is called signal 1.

Costimulation is not a second antigen receptor with the same fine specificity as the TCR. Many lymphocytes express CD28, and its ligand does not contain the antigenic peptide that distinguishes one responding clone from another. Instead, it helps a cell that has received an appropriate receptor signal establish a productive activation program. It supports survival, metabolic responses, transcriptional regulation and effective IL-2 production. The biological value of this arrangement is that an antigen-specific receptor signal and a context-sensitive supporting signal can be combined. A dendritic cell presenting a relevant peptide after innate activation is more likely to supply this combination than a resting nonprofessional cell displaying ordinary self peptide.

Dendritic cells are particularly effective at priming naive T cells because they combine antigen presentation, costimulatory capacity and appropriate localization in lymphoid tissues. Recognition of infection-associated signals can promote dendritic cell maturation, change the expression of costimulatory molecules and alter cytokine production. Antigen can then be transported or presented in lymphoid tissues where naive T cells circulate. This is an important bridge from innate recognition to adaptive specificity. A pattern-recognition receptor on the dendritic cell can detect microbial features shared by many organisms, while the TCR on the lymphocyte responds to a particular displayed peptide–MHC complex. Their distinct recognition strategies cooperate rather than duplicate one another.

Consider two antigen-presenting cell populations displaying equal amounts of a cognate peptide–MHC ligand. Population A expresses abundant accessible CD80 and CD86, whereas population B lacks these molecules. A naive T-cell clone can encounter the same signal 1 in both cultures, but the probability and magnitude of productive activation may differ. If the cells in population B fail to induce IL-2 production and proliferation, the result is consistent with a costimulation defect. To attribute that difference specifically to B7–CD28 interactions, controls must establish similar antigen display, viable presenter numbers, appropriate T-cell conditions and comparable access to other relevant signals. A rescue by restoring B7 expression or supplying an appropriate substitute supporting signal would strengthen the interpretation.

The term anergy describes a state of functional unresponsiveness rather than simply absence of receptor binding. In a classic model, TCR stimulation without sufficient costimulation can induce a state in which a T cell responds poorly to subsequent stimulation. However, the response to incomplete signaling is not invariably anergy. Depending on the cell type, antigen exposure, cytokines and developmental state, the cell may fail to activate, undergo deletion, become anergic or follow another fate. A single measurement of low proliferation in the first culture cannot distinguish these outcomes. A useful experiment washes the cells, measures viability and then tests their ability to respond to a complete subsequent stimulus. Persistent poor responsiveness among viable antigen-specific cells would support a functional unresponsiveness interpretation.

A lack of initial activation must also be distinguished from suppression of an established effector response. The amount and type of costimulation required can differ between naive cells and previously activated cells. An effector CD8 cell can recognize and kill an appropriate target that does not express the costimulatory molecules normally needed to prime a naive CD8 cell. This does not contradict the two-signal model. Priming generates a cell with a different functional program, allowing it to act in a different context. A tissue cell infected by a virus may present class I peptide–MHC but be poorly equipped to activate naive lymphocytes. A dendritic cell may provide priming, and the resulting effector cells can later recognize infected targets. The requirement for professional priming and the ability to execute an effector function must not be conflated.

The costimulatory system also has inhibitory counterparts. CTLA-4 can bind the same B7 ligands as CD28 and contributes to down-regulation of T-cell responses. The inhibitory receptor PD-1 interacts with its own ligands and can attenuate signaling. Their physiological importance is that the decision to respond must remain adjustable rather than proceeding indefinitely once activated. These inhibitory systems are not the same molecule, and PD-1 does not replace B7 as the activating ligand for CD28. A detailed examination of inhibitory checkpoints and peripheral tolerance belongs to the following chapter; their mention here explains why the amount of positive costimulation is not the only contextual influence.

An experimentally useful distinction is between binding of antigen and acquisition of functional competence. A fluorescent peptide–MHC reagent may identify antigen-specific T cells even if those cells are unable to proliferate following stimulation. Conversely, broad activation by an experimental reagent may cause many lymphocytes to divide without reproducing the physiological peptide-specific selection process. In Part C questions, identify the level at which each assay operates. Measurements of CD28 expression alone do not establish its engagement, just as measurements of B7 expression alone do not establish the display of the cognate peptide. A causal explanation must connect the molecules present, the interactions occurring and the cellular outcomes being measured.

The three-signal representation extends this discussion by adding the cytokine environment as another major determinant of response. Its value is conceptual organization: signal 1 establishes antigen-specific recognition, signal 2 conveys supporting or regulatory context, and signal 3 contributes to differentiation and survival programs. Real cells integrate many more interactions than three discrete wires, and the boundaries between the categories are not perfectly sharp. A cytokine can influence proliferation as well as fate; costimulation can affect cytokine expression; antigen dose can influence the differentiation outcome. Nonetheless, the model allows the student to predict why identical antigen recognition can result in different immune responses when the surrounding molecular signals differ.

11. IL-2, metabolic adaptation and clonal expansion

A successful antigen encounter solves a problem of identification, but the responding population may initially be very small. To influence an infection affecting thousands or millions of cells, rare antigen-specific lymphocytes must generate many descendants. Clonal expansion is the increase in the number of lymphocytes descended from activated precursors. The descendants inherit the rearranged receptor genes of their ancestors, so proliferation preserves the central specificity of a recruited clone. Differentiation accompanies this process but must be distinguished from it. A cell can undergo division without acquiring a particular effector phenotype, and two populations that have undergone similar numbers of divisions can have different functions.

Interleukin-2, abbreviated IL-2, is a central cytokine in classical descriptions of activated T-cell proliferation. A cytokine is a secreted signaling protein capable of altering the behavior of cells that express suitable receptors. Activated T cells can produce IL-2, and IL-2 can act on the cell that produced it or on nearby receptive cells. These modes are called autocrine and paracrine signaling, respectively. The same secreted cytokine need not act exclusively on one clone; the pattern of receptor expression and local availability determines which cells can respond. This arrangement couples activation to population growth without requiring the antigen receptor itself to function as a growth factor receptor.

The IL-2 receptor exists in assemblies with different binding and signaling characteristics. Its alpha chain, CD25, contributes importantly to the high-affinity trimeric receptor but is not by itself the main signal-transducing component. The beta chain, CD122, and common gamma chain, CD132, participate in downstream signaling. Activated conventional T cells often upregulate CD25, whereas regulatory T cells commonly maintain high CD25 expression and depend strongly on IL-2 supplied by other cells. This explains why an increase in CD25 staining is useful evidence in certain activation experiments but is not by itself sufficient to classify a cell as a newly activated conventional effector.

Signaling through the beta and gamma chains involves associated Janus kinases and downstream transcriptional regulation, notably STAT5. These signals help regulate survival, proliferation and differentiation according to the cellular context. The receptor is not simply another copy of the TCR: it recognizes a cytokine rather than a peptide–MHC complex and transmits information about the supporting environment. A T cell with a normal TCR can respond to antigen while failing to expand efficiently if cytokine signaling is impaired. The reverse experimental situation is also informative: exogenous IL-2 may support proliferation of suitably primed cells, but it cannot in general replace the antigen-specific selection of a naive repertoire.

Cell division imposes material and energetic requirements. A resting naive lymphocyte is relatively small and must increase the production of proteins, membrane components and nucleotides before generating daughter cells. TCR and costimulatory signals promote metabolic changes that support this growth. Activated populations often increase nutrient uptake and glycolytic flux while retaining mitochondrial functions. The increase in glycolysis does not imply that mitochondria cease to contribute ATP or biosynthetic intermediates. A proliferating cell needs both energy and building material, and pathway usage changes with activation state and availability of nutrients. The connection to metabolism is therefore functional rather than merely descriptive: growth cannot be sustained by transcriptional instructions alone when essential resources are absent.

A simplified model of clonal expansion follows from binary cell division. If N0 viable responding cells each divide exactly n times and every daughter survives, their number is N = N0 × 2^n. The exponent is dimensionless because it counts divisions. Starting with 25 cells and allowing eight complete rounds of division gives 25 × 256 = 6,400 cells. The model assumes that all cells enter the cycle, that divisions occur without loss, and that all descendants are counted. In biological experiments, activation may be asynchronous, daughter cells may die and some cells may stop dividing. The model nevertheless provides a useful reference against which observed expansion can be compared.

A time-dependent version assumes a constant division interval T and synchronized division or an approximate continuous exponential growth description. Under these assumptions the number after time t is N0 × 2^(t/T). For 100 cells with a model doubling interval of 12 hours, 48 hours corresponds to four doublings and produces 1,600 cells. A culture counted at the same time with only 800 cells has a smaller net expansion. It cannot be concluded from the final count alone that the division interval is longer. The same outcome could arise from delayed entry into the cell cycle, loss of half the potential descendants, or a mixture of proliferating and nonproliferating cells. Growth equations describe observable outcomes; they do not automatically identify cellular mechanisms.

A fluorescent division-tracking dye can help discriminate division history from net abundance. Initially labeled cells contain a quantity of dye that is approximately partitioned between their daughters. Under the idealization of equal partitioning and negligible dye loss unrelated to division, the intensity after n divisions is F0/2^n. A population initially at 1,600 arbitrary fluorescence units would be expected near 200 units after three divisions. Multiple intensity peaks can represent distinct division histories within the same culture. A dye profile still requires viability measurements and absolute counts, because a population that divided many times but lost most of its descendants may yield a small number of surviving cells. Fluorescence intensity is a relative reporter, not a direct count of surviving lymphocytes.

Expansion must eventually be limited. When antigen and inflammatory signals diminish, many effector cells die during the contraction phase. A smaller population persists as memory cells under suitable conditions. Memory is not the survival of every activated cell; it is a change in the composition and functional properties of the long-lived repertoire. The presence of antigen-specific memory cells can increase the speed or magnitude of a subsequent response, but the details depend on memory subset, tissue localization and the nature of the challenge. The broad logic of expansion, contraction and persistence parallels the population dynamics of adaptive immunity studied in B-cell responses, without implying that T cells secrete antibodies or undergo antibody affinity maturation.

12. Signal 3 and the acquisition of specialized functions

Activation does not produce a single universal T-cell effector. Different pathogens and tissue conditions require different biological strategies. A microorganism inside a macrophage may be controlled more effectively if the macrophage receives signals that increase its antimicrobial capacity. A large extracellular parasite may require a response emphasizing barrier tissues and other leukocyte populations. Antibody responses to protein antigens can require direct assistance from a specialized helper cell that encounters the responding B cell. The same general antigen-recognition machinery must therefore be coupled to different downstream functional programs.

Cytokines contribute information about the local biological context. A dendritic cell responding to one category of microbial stimulus can produce a different cytokine mixture from a cell responding to another. Cytokines may also originate from innate lymphoid populations, other leukocytes, stromal cells or previously activated lymphocytes. Their availability influences which transcriptional programs become established during the activation of naive T cells. The term polarization is often used to describe differentiation toward a particular functional phenotype, especially in the CD4 compartment. It does not mean that the plasma membrane acquires a simple electrical polarity; it refers to a biased developmental and functional outcome.

It is important to distinguish an inducing cytokine from a characteristic effector cytokine. An inducing cytokine contributes to formation or stabilization of a lineage program. An effector cytokine is a product that helps the differentiated cell act on its environment. In the Th1 model, IL-12 is an important inducing signal and interferon gamma is a characteristic effector product; interferon gamma can also participate in reinforcing differentiation. In the Th17 model, a collection of cytokines contributes to differentiation and stabilization, whereas IL-17 is a characteristic effector product. A question may ask for either the cytokine that promotes the lineage or the cytokine that the lineage typically produces. Reading the direction of the relationship is essential.

Many cytokine receptors act through kinase and transcription factor systems. Binding of a cytokine can induce phosphorylation and activation of STAT proteins, which help alter transcription. In a particular cellular context, these pathways support expression of transcription factors associated with a helper lineage. The lineage-associated factors then regulate sets of genes involved in cytokine production, receptor expression, migration and other functions. A transcription factor does not act by converting a cytokine into another cytokine. It influences the expression of genes, changing the cell's capacity to make and respond to proteins. This provides a mechanistic bridge between cell signaling and differentiation.

The differentiation outcome reflects the integration of multiple inputs, not an isolated cytokine concentration in a vacuum. The activation state of the T cell, magnitude and duration of receptor stimulation, availability of costimulation, tissue environment and other cytokines can influence the result. In experimental cultures, defined cytokine combinations can favor particular phenotypes, but the conditions that work in one organism or model do not necessarily reproduce every in vivo environment. The same cytokine can exert different effects on cells expressing different receptors or transcriptional machinery. A useful exam-level model assigns characteristic associations without treating them as immutable laws.

To determine whether a cytokine is necessary for differentiation, an experiment can compare matched activation conditions with and without that cytokine or its receptor signal. If the expected lineage markers decrease after removal, the result supports a contribution under the tested conditions. To test sufficiency, supply the cytokine in an otherwise defined context and examine whether the lineage program develops. Necessity and sufficiency are different logical properties. A signal may be necessary in one model but insufficient by itself, because antigen recognition and additional inputs are also required. Conversely, a high experimental concentration may induce a phenotype under artificial conditions even if that signal is not the sole physiological determinant.

Measuring a single secreted cytokine is often inadequate to identify a population precisely. A culture supernatant combines products from all cells present, and several types of immune cells can produce overlapping cytokines. Intracellular staining, appropriately controlled transcriptional measurements and functional assays can help determine which cells express particular products and whether these products are associated with a stable lineage program. Even a well-established marker can vary with activation and time. The aim is not to memorize a limitless list of markers, but to reason from the stimulus, the responding cell, the regulator and the biological effect. This approach remains useful when a question presents unfamiliar experimental conditions.

13. Th1, Th2 and Th17: matching the response to the biological problem

Th1 cells are conventional CD4 helper cells whose characteristic program includes production of interferon gamma. The lineage-associated transcription factor T-bet and signals including IL-12 and interferon gamma are central to standard descriptions of Th1 differentiation. The biological task commonly associated with Th1 immunity is assistance in control of intracellular microorganisms. When a macrophage contains an organism that resists its initial antimicrobial activity, interferon gamma can enhance components of macrophage antimicrobial function. The helper cell does not need to engulf that organism itself. It recognizes appropriate antigenic information through its receptor and provides signals that alter the behavior of the cell carrying out the antimicrobial response.

The relationship between Th1 cells and macrophages illustrates why the term cell-mediated immunity includes more than direct cytotoxicity. A helper lymphocyte can contribute to clearance of an intracellular pathogen by activating another cell, whereas a cytotoxic lymphocyte may directly trigger death of an infected target. Both routes involve cellular effectors, but they are not equivalent. A macrophage activation assay and a target-cell lysis assay measure different mechanisms. Loss of interferon gamma signaling in the macrophage could impair its enhanced antimicrobial response despite normal T-cell receptor recognition and cytokine production by the helper cell. The molecular defect would reside in the recipient of the helper signal rather than in the antigen-specific lymphocyte.

Th1 differentiation provides an example of positive feedback. Cytokines that favor the lineage can promote expression of factors that increase the capacity to produce or respond to related signals. This reinforcement can stabilize a functional program during an immune response. However, feedback does not make a differentiated cell independent of its environment, nor does it guarantee that all products of a Th1 population are exclusive to that population. NK cells and other lymphocytes can produce interferon gamma. Detecting interferon gamma in a tissue is therefore consistent with a type 1 immune response but does not alone demonstrate that conventional CD4 Th1 cells are the only source.

Th2 cells are associated with a different set of functions. The transcription factor GATA3 and the cytokine IL-4 are prominent in the classical Th2 differentiation model. Typical effector products include IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13. These cytokines contribute to responses against helminths and to changes in barrier tissues, recruitment or support of particular leukocytes, and aspects of antibody class switching. The biological context differs from a bacterium replicating within a macrophage. A multicellular parasite can be much larger than a phagocyte, so a strategy based exclusively on phagocytosis is inadequate. Coordination of multiple extracellular and tissue-associated defense processes becomes especially relevant.

The characteristic Th2 cytokines have distinguishable roles. IL-5 is strongly associated with eosinophil biology, including support of eosinophil development and activity. IL-4 contributes to type 2 differentiation and, with other required signals, can favor particular antibody class-switch outcomes, including IgE responses. IL-13 contributes to several type 2 tissue responses, including changes in mucus-producing epithelia. These are representative associations, not a claim that each cytokine has only one target cell. The same protective program can contribute to allergic pathology when activated in an inappropriate context. The mechanisms of hypersensitivity are addressed later; here allergy illustrates why the functional value of a response depends on what initiated it.

An experimental culture containing antigen-activated CD4 cells can reveal differences between Th1 and Th2 differentiation. If matched cultures are exposed to conditions favoring IL-12-dependent or IL-4-dependent differentiation, they may differ in expression of T-bet or GATA3 and in their cytokine profiles. Measurements should compare similarly viable activated populations and account for the number of cells in each culture. A culture producing twice as much total cytokine may simply contain twice as many viable cells; output per cell or the fraction of producing cells can give additional information. Changing the differentiation environment while maintaining the same receptor specificity demonstrates that antigen recognition and effector identity are partly separable dimensions.

Th17 cells are especially relevant to defense at barrier surfaces and against certain extracellular bacteria and fungi. Their characteristic program includes expression of the transcription factor ROR gamma t and production of IL-17-family cytokines, particularly IL-17A, with other products such as IL-22 in appropriate contexts. IL-17 acts on responsive tissue cells and can promote production of chemokines and other factors that support recruitment of neutrophils. The helper cell is thus linked indirectly to the cellular populations that carry out antimicrobial defense at the affected site. The function is not explained simply by saying that IL-17 kills extracellular bacteria directly. Much of its biological significance arises from regulation of other cells.

The cytokine conditions promoting Th17 differentiation are context dependent. In familiar mouse teaching models, TGF-beta together with inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 can favor early Th17 development, while IL-23 supports expansion or stabilization of relevant effector programs. IL-1 and additional factors can contribute, particularly in other settings, and human differentiation cannot always be reduced to the same simple recipe. For exam purposes, the key is to recognize the association with inflammatory differentiation cues, ROR gamma t and IL-17-associated barrier and neutrophil responses, without treating one culture formulation as universally necessary and sufficient for all Th17 populations.

The distinction between Th17 and Th1 becomes particularly useful in interpreting a perturbation affecting tissue recruitment. Suppose an animal mounts a measurable interferon gamma response to an extracellular fungal challenge but fails to recruit neutrophils efficiently at a mucosal site. A defect in an IL-17-dependent tissue response is one plausible explanation, but it is not proven by the description alone. Chemokine production, neutrophil abundance, adhesion molecules and responsiveness of the tissue cells must also be considered. The same recruitment phenotype could arise from a downstream defect even when Th17 differentiation is normal. An analytical question may provide additional measurements that locate the failure at the helper cell or at the responding tissue.

Th1, Th2 and Th17 populations are often shown as separate branches of a naive CD4 precursor. This is a powerful framework for learning their dominant functional associations, but real immune responses frequently contain mixed populations and context-dependent phenotypes. A pathogen may present multiple stages with different locations or biological characteristics. A response may change over time as the cytokine environment changes. Some helper populations also display plasticity, altering aspects of their cytokine and transcriptional programs. Therefore, a well-reasoned prediction should identify the predominant response expected under the stated conditions rather than assume that all other helper functions must disappear.

14. Follicular helper cells and cooperation with antigen-specific B cells

A T follicular helper cell, usually abbreviated Tfh, specializes in providing assistance to B cells in lymphoid follicles and germinal centers. Its differentiation program is associated with the transcription factor Bcl6 and expression of CXCR5, a chemokine receptor that contributes to localization toward follicular regions. Tfh cells can provide signals through CD40 ligand and cytokines such as IL-21, helping regulate B-cell proliferation, selection and differentiation. These signals operate in a spatially organized setting: the helper lymphocyte must encounter the B cells that are participating in the relevant adaptive response.

The relationship between a BCR and a TCR during this interaction is particularly important. A B cell can bind a native antigen using its membrane immunoglobulin, internalize the antigen, process its proteins and display derived peptides on MHC class II. A suitable helper cell recognizes a peptide–MHC complex on the B cell rather than necessarily recognizing the native epitope bound by the BCR. Both cells can therefore participate in a response to the same antigenic material while recognizing different molecular determinants. This is often called linked recognition because the B-cell-binding determinant and the T-cell-recognized peptide can be physically associated within the antigen captured by the B cell.

Linked recognition explains the immunological logic of carrier-dependent antibody responses. Consider a small hapten attached to a protein carrier. A B cell specific for the hapten can bind and internalize the conjugate. It may then present peptides derived from the carrier protein to a carrier-specific CD4 helper cell. The helper cell need not recognize the hapten itself through its TCR. Instead, the B cell receives assistance because it presents a peptide that the helper cell recognizes. The physical association between the hapten and carrier helps direct T-cell assistance toward B cells that have captured the relevant conjugate. Simply providing unrelated antigen molecules in the same environment does not guarantee this linked presentation.

CD40 on the B cell and CD40 ligand, also called CD154, on an activated helper cell contribute a contact-dependent signal. This interaction should not be confused with CD28–B7 costimulation in naive T-cell priming. Both involve cell-surface proteins and help coordinate adaptive immunity, but the identities, orientations and major functions differ. A defect in CD40 ligand can impair T-dependent B-cell responses even when the helper lymphocyte recognizes peptide–MHC and the B cell binds its native antigen. The consequences illustrate how a missing intercellular signal can prevent a downstream functional outcome without eliminating the initial recognition event.

Within a germinal center, B cells compete for antigen and signals that support selection and differentiation. Helper-cell assistance is one of the influences shaping this process. The Tfh cell is not itself undergoing antibody somatic hypermutation or class-switch recombination to produce a higher-affinity secreted TCR. Its role is to influence the B-cell population carrying out those processes. Germinal-center selection involves multiple interactions, and the availability of T-cell help can influence which B-cell clones expand or persist. The preceding chapter addresses the detailed humoral outcomes; here the focus is the differentiation and function of the helper lymphocyte that makes such cooperation possible.

Loss of follicular localization can impair B-cell help even if an activated T cell still produces cytokines in a culture dish. This is a consequence of tissue organization: signals that can operate at a short distance or require cell contact must be delivered near the appropriate responder. A CXCR5-associated homing defect may therefore alter the ability of helper cells to participate effectively in follicular responses. To investigate this possibility, one could compare the presence of helper cells in follicles with their total abundance and functional activity. Normal total cell counts combined with abnormal localization would point toward a different mechanism from complete failure to generate the helper lineage.

Tfh identity also illustrates the limitations of assigning cells by a single secreted product. IL-21 is important in follicular helper biology, but other activated populations can produce it. Bcl6 expression and CXCR5 help identify relevant cells, while their location and capacity to assist B cells provide additional functional evidence. A population can show partial features of a follicular helper program before reaching a mature functional state. An examination question that asks for the best identifying combination should therefore favor a coherent set of lineage, localization and function markers rather than an assumption that one cytokine occurs exclusively in one cell type.

15. Regulatory T cells and the decision to restrain immunity

An effective immune system must be able to prevent or restrain responses as well as initiate them. Regulatory T cells, often abbreviated Treg, represent a major specialized CD4 population associated with this function. The transcription factor Foxp3 is central to the identity and function of conventional regulatory T cells, and many express high levels of CD25. The existence of this lineage helps explain why thymic selection is not simply a process of deleting every cell that recognizes self. Some cells develop a program that contributes to maintenance of tolerance and regulation of other immune populations.

Regulatory T cells can arise during thymic development, and additional regulatory populations can develop from mature conventional CD4 cells in peripheral contexts. In standard teaching models, TGF-beta and IL-2 can contribute to development of induced Foxp3-positive regulatory cells under appropriate conditions. This should not be reduced to a rule that exposure to TGF-beta always produces a regulatory lymphocyte. In inflammatory contexts, combinations of signals involving TGF-beta may favor other outcomes, including Th17 differentiation in some models. The identity of the responding cell and the other signals present help determine which transcriptional program becomes established.

IL-2 dependence provides an important contrast with the idea that all cytokine-responsive cells are necessarily effector cells. Regulatory T cells often express high-affinity IL-2 receptors and rely on access to IL-2 for survival and function. Many do not produce substantial amounts of IL-2 themselves. The cytokine made by activated conventional T cells can therefore support populations that limit or shape the response. This relationship creates a form of feedback within the immune system. It also explains why indiscriminately blocking a cytokine can have complex outcomes if the cytokine supports both responding and regulatory populations. Predicting the net result requires information about which cells are affected.

Regulatory T-cell suppression can involve contact-dependent mechanisms and changes in the cytokine environment. CTLA-4, IL-10 and TGF-beta are representative participants, although their relative importance depends on the biological setting. A regulatory cell can influence antigen-presenting cells or other immune cells rather than necessarily destroying the cells it regulates. It is therefore incorrect to treat a regulatory T cell as a cytotoxic T cell with a different surface marker. Both can reduce aspects of an immune response, but the cellular mechanisms and biological consequences differ. The next node examines regulation, checkpoints and peripheral tolerance in more detail.

A loss-of-function perturbation of Foxp3 provides a useful connection between a transcriptional program and organism-level homeostasis. Failure to establish or maintain a functional regulatory population can allow inappropriate activation of other immune cells, producing broad inflammatory or autoimmune consequences. The phenotype is not necessarily an inability of conventional T cells to recognize foreign peptides. Rather, normal or excessive responses may occur without adequate regulation. This illustrates a general principle of homeostatic systems: removal of an inhibitory component can produce disease through excessive activity of otherwise functional pathways.

Functional suppression should be tested rather than inferred from CD25 alone. Activated conventional T cells can also upregulate CD25. A carefully designed comparison can assess whether a population reduces proliferation or cytokine output of appropriately stimulated responder cells under matched conditions. Controls should account for initial responder number, viability, competition for nutrients and the possibility that observed changes arise from nonspecific loss of cells. Marker expression supports classification, while functional results provide evidence about the activity of the population. These complementary measurements are more informative than an unqualified claim that every CD4-positive CD25-positive cell is regulatory.

16. CD8 differentiation, effector competence and memory

A mature naive CD8 T cell recognizes cognate peptide displayed by MHC class I and can differentiate into an effector cytotoxic T lymphocyte after appropriate priming. Class I presentation allows the immune system to sample peptides derived from proteins synthesized within nucleated cells. Professional antigen-presenting cells, particularly dendritic cells, can also present acquired antigen through cross-presentation, linking material from other cells to priming of class I-restricted lymphocytes. This exception is important because the cell in which an infection originated is not necessarily the cell most capable of activating naive T cells. Antigen presentation and the location of the eventual target need not coincide.

Priming a CD8 cell involves antigen recognition and supporting signals that promote proliferation and functional differentiation. Cytokines such as IL-2, IL-12 and type I interferons can contribute depending on the infection and context. CD4 helper cells may support effective CD8 responses, for example by enhancing the capacity of antigen-presenting cells through interactions involving CD40. The importance of particular helper signals can differ among responses. A highly inflammatory context may provide substantial innate support, whereas other contexts require more assistance. It is therefore inappropriate to claim that every CD8 response is absolutely dependent on one identical cytokine or one identical source of helper activity.

A differentiated cytotoxic T lymphocyte acquires molecular machinery that enables it to induce death of appropriately recognized targets. Perforin and granzymes are characteristic components of one effector pathway, and another route involves death receptor interactions such as Fas and Fas ligand. Their detailed mechanism is reserved for the next chapter. Here the important developmental distinction is that the cell's antigen receptor does not itself cut or destroy a target. The receptor identifies the appropriate contact, and the differentiation program supplies the machinery that executes an effector response. Normal recognition without functional effector machinery can therefore yield target binding without effective killing.

Cytotoxic T-cell function differs from NK-cell recognition. Both can use related cytotoxic machinery, but conventional CD8 T cells use clonally rearranged TCRs to recognize appropriate peptide–MHC class I complexes. NK cells integrate signals from germline-encoded activating and inhibitory receptors, including recognition mechanisms sensitive to alterations in self MHC expression. A reduction of class I display may make a cell less recognizable by some antigen-specific CD8 populations while altering its susceptibility to NK cells. These predictions are not necessarily opposites in every context because multiple other ligands and signals affect both populations. The comparison demonstrates that sharing an effector mechanism does not imply identical recognition.

Expansion of an antigen-specific CD8 population can be accompanied by divergence into cells with different survival and effector properties. After the primary response, many effectors disappear while a smaller memory population persists. The fate of a given cell depends on the intensity and duration of stimulation, inflammatory environment, cytokine signals and metabolic state. A population with strong immediate effector function is not necessarily the population with the greatest long-term persistence. An experiment comparing two activation conditions should therefore measure immediate functional responses and subsequent survival separately if it is intended to address memory formation. A large early clone is not a sufficient measurement of durable protection.

Memory T cells are heterogeneous. Some recirculate through lymphoid organs, others patrol nonlymphoid sites and some persist in particular tissues. These patterns help explain why a subsequent response can be faster or occur closer to a site of repeated exposure. The antigen-receptor specificity of memory descendants reflects their clonal history, whereas their responsiveness and localization reflect differentiation. The memory principle connects this node to the later discussion of vaccination, but memory T cells do not protect by secreting immunoglobulins. Their protective effects depend on their capacity to coordinate cellular responses or rapidly acquire or deploy effector functions.

A complete understanding of T-cell differentiation thus requires three separate axes of reasoning: receptor specificity determines which molecular complexes can be recognized; developmental and activation history determines which functional programs a cell has acquired; tissue localization determines where its interactions can occur. A cell with an intact TCR may still fail to protect if its proliferation, cytokine responsiveness, differentiation or migration is disrupted. Conversely, a population with abundant effector molecules may fail to act on a particular target if the appropriate peptide–MHC ligand is absent. These distinctions provide the foundation for studying cell-mediated immunity, immune modulation and self-tolerance in the next node.
