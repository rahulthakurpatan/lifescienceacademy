1. How skin reveals the physical world

Close your eyes and hold a coin between two fingertips. Even without vision, you can determine whether it is round, whether its surface is rough, and whether it is beginning to slip. The skin does not receive an image of the coin. It is deformed by contact forces, and the nervous system must infer properties of an external object from that deformation. This is the central problem of tactile physiology. The answer involves a sequence of mechanical, electrical and neural transformations, rather than a single receptor responsible for the whole sensation.

A tactile stimulus is a mechanical event at or near the body surface. An object pressing perpendicular to the skin creates indentation and compression; a moving object creates shear and changing patterns of displacement; stretching pulls adjacent regions apart. A vibration repeatedly reverses or varies deformation over time. These mechanical events may occur together. The edge of a sliding coin produces local pressure gradients, the metal surface produces tiny oscillations during movement, and tangential force stretches the skin. Different afferents report different aspects of the same event. The brain combines their activity rather than waiting for a labelled message that says coin.

Somatosensation is broader than touch. It includes information concerning mechanical deformation of the body, limb position, temperature, tissue damage and certain internal bodily states. This chapter concerns non-painful cutaneous mechanosensation: touch, pressure, skin stretch and vibration. Proprioceptors in muscles and tendons also detect mechanical changes, but their main function is to describe the condition of the locomotor system and is treated in the nervous-system subunit. Nociceptors and thermoreceptors may lie in the same piece of skin as tactile endings, yet differ in their stimulus sensitivity and central routing. A mechanical stimulus can become painful at sufficiently damaging intensities; this does not imply that every receptor sensitive to gentle touch is a nociceptor.

Skin is itself part of the measuring apparatus. Imagine applying the same indentation to a thin membrane and to a thick resilient cushion. At the outer surface the indentation is similar, but deformation at a buried sensor is different. The epidermis, dermis, connective tissue, capsule of a receptor and attachment to a hair follicle all affect transmission of mechanical energy. A receptor's physiological sensitivity therefore depends both on the intrinsic excitability of its cells and on the structures that transmit force to their membranes.

A mechanoreceptor is a sensory ending or specialised cell associated with a sensory neuron that responds preferentially to mechanical deformation. An adequate stimulus is the form of energy to which a receptor is especially sensitive under normal conditions. It is not the only possible way of exciting its axon; an experimental electrical pulse can activate a nerve without touching the skin. This distinction matters because electrical stimulation bypasses mechanical transduction and can be used to test whether an abnormal response is caused by failure of the receptor ending or by failure of the axon.

A tactile experience also includes central interpretation. A single action potential is not intrinsically large or small according to how hard a surface presses. Its amplitude remains approximately fixed for a given healthy axon under constant recording conditions. Stimulus information is carried by the identity of active afferents, the timing and rate of their spikes, and the distribution of activity over many neurons. These complementary codes allow the nervous system to distinguish a stationary pressure from a rapid vibration and a broad contact from a narrow edge. The first task in learning tactile physiology is to keep the levels distinct: stimulus, tissue deformation, receptor current, generator potential, afferent spike train, central representation and perception.

Distinguishing detection, discrimination and identification

Detection is the simplest of several distinct sensory achievements. It asks whether a stimulus is present rather than absent. Discrimination asks whether two stimuli differ in some dimension, such as spatial position, roughness or vibration frequency. Identification asks which object or event caused the sensation. An animal may detect a contact without being able to determine its exact location, and may discriminate two textures without identifying the materials that produce them. These distinctions are essential when interpreting sensory loss. If an experiment reports a failure of discrimination, it has not necessarily demonstrated a failure of detection.

Consider a blindfolded participant whose palm is contacted by an object. One question is whether contact occurred. Another is whether the object is circular or rectangular. Answering the second question requires information about edges at different positions, whereas the first can be answered from activity in a much broader tactile population. If the participant can feel pressure yet cannot identify the shape, some peripheral or central information remains available. The investigator should determine which dimension of the sensory signal is missing before selecting a physiological explanation.

The information in a tactile response may also be described as exafferent or reafferent according to whether the mechanical event arises from the outside environment or from movement of the animal itself. The skin experiences mechanical forces in both cases, and peripheral receptors need not distinguish their origin. A finger moving over a stationary surface and a surface moving beneath a stationary finger can create similar local patterns of deformation. The nervous system can combine tactile information with motor-related and proprioceptive information to distinguish the situations. This shows why an intact receptor is not sufficient for all aspects of perception: the sensory system must interpret its activity in relation to the behaviour of the whole animal.

The physiological meaning of pressure is force per area, not a unique perceptual category. A force of 0.2 N applied over an area of 1 cm² creates an average pressure of 2,000 Pa. Applying the same force over 0.1 cm² creates an average pressure of 20,000 Pa. The conversion uses 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m². These average pressures do not specify the local pressure distribution around the edge of the probe or the deformation at the depth of a receptor. A narrow rigid tip may produce a high local pressure but deform only a small area superficially, whereas a broad contact at the same total force can recruit different spatial patterns. Questions comparing touch stimuli should specify both the force and the contact geometry, or otherwise explain why those factors can be neglected.

Tactile perception is thus built from partial information. A receptor can be especially sensitive to one aspect of mechanical stimulation without being an exclusive detector of it. The same physical event can stimulate multiple receptor classes and produce several simultaneous perceptual qualities. Identifying a dominant receptor contribution is valuable, but it is more powerful to reason from what the physical stimulus does to the tissue and what each class of afferent actually encodes.

2. Skin mechanics and the organisation of tactile afferents

The outer epidermis forms the interface with the world. It is supported by the dermis, which contains connective tissue, blood vessels and sensory structures. Deeper connective tissue and subcutaneous structures transmit forces over wider regions. In glabrous skin, such as the palmar surfaces of the fingers, a thick epidermis and characteristic epidermal ridges provide contact surfaces used during manipulation. Hairy skin has a further means of detecting weak disturbances: displacement of a hair shaft can transmit force to the sensory endings associated with its follicle before a large object reaches the skin itself.

The distinction between superficial and deep tactile receptors is a mechanical as well as an anatomical distinction. A small point near the surface produces relatively local deformation there. The same force generates a spatially broader distribution of stress at greater depth. Superficially positioned receptors can consequently help preserve fine spatial information. Deep receptors are often well placed to detect mechanical events transmitted across larger areas. These are general tendencies, not laws that allow receptor depth alone to determine an exact receptive-field diameter. The pattern of terminal branching, skin stiffness and the intensity of stimulation also matter.

The sensory neuron carrying information from the trunk and limbs usually has its cell body in a dorsal root ganglion. In a pseudounipolar sensory neuron, one process leaves the soma and divides into a peripheral branch and a central branch. The peripheral process receives receptor-generated activity; the central process enters the spinal cord through a dorsal root. This arrangement means that the cell body is not the site of a chemical synapse between two neurons. A spike generated in the peripheral process propagates along the axon toward the central process. The first synaptic relay of a particular ascending pathway occurs centrally.

The tactile nerve ending is often a specialised terminal rather than a separate sensory epithelial cell. However, the Merkel cell–neurite complex is a notable case in which an epithelial-derived Merkel cell and a sensory neurite contribute together to the response. A physiological unit therefore need not be equivalent to a single receptor cell. A single afferent may branch to several terminal structures, and one sensitive area can contain several overlapping innervation patterns. It is useful to call the combination of a sensory neuron and the peripheral structures that shape its response a mechanoreceptive unit.

A low-threshold mechanoreceptor responds to innocuous deformation, in contrast to high-threshold mechanoreceptors that typically participate in signalling potentially damaging events. Many rapidly conducting discriminative-touch afferents are myelinated Aβ fibres. Some low-threshold tactile information in hairy skin is also carried by thinner myelinated or unmyelinated afferents. The distinction is therefore not simply that all touch is carried by one fibre diameter and all other senses by another. Fibre class predicts conduction properties and provides clues to physiological role, but the actual stimulus-response relation must be measured.

The organisation of cutaneous afferents has an important consequence for experimental interpretation. If two stimuli applied to different parts of a finger evoke spikes recorded from a single axon, the observations need not mean the axon moves. The neuron's terminal arbor or the spread of deformation can account for both responses. Conversely, a nerve recording containing several spike amplitudes may represent multiple units, not one unit changing spike size with stimulus intensity. Single-unit discrimination requires evidence that recorded impulses can be assigned to the same neuron.

Force transmission, strain and the importance of stimulus geometry

Stress describes the force per unit area within a material. Strain describes a relative change in geometry, such as the change in length divided by the original length. If a 10 mm region of skin is stretched to 10.5 mm, the engineering strain along that direction is 0.5/10 = 0.05, or five percent. This calculation does not imply that every point within the region undergoes the same deformation; in heterogeneous living tissue, strains can vary substantially with position. The relevant quantity for the sensory ending is the deformation actually transmitted to its membrane and associated structures.

A normal force acts perpendicular to the surface, while a tangential force acts along it. In a grip task, both are important. The normal force presses the object into the fingers, and tangential loading tends to cause slip. The surface may not slip everywhere at once: some areas can remain attached through friction while others begin moving. Localised changes in skin deformation are informative about the onset of slip. A sensory system that represented only total normal force would miss these changing spatial patterns, which is why the complement of cutaneous receptors is useful.

Mechanical coupling is affected by the way that tissue responds over time. An ideal elastic material recovers its original configuration immediately after removal of load and has a fixed relation between stress and strain. A viscous element resists the rate of deformation, and a viscoelastic material combines time-dependent and elastic behaviour. Skin and receptor capsules are viscoelastic biological structures. If an indentation is held constant, the force required to maintain it may decline through stress relaxation. If a constant force is applied, deformation may gradually increase through creep. These are distinct conditions. A decrease in receptor firing in a fixed-displacement experiment might arise partly because the local stress itself changes over time, even if the probe remains at the same position.

A simple spring model F = kx can illustrate the influence of stiffness. F is force in newtons, k is stiffness in newtons per metre and x is displacement in metres. If a spring-like tissue region has an effective stiffness of 100 N/m, a 0.001 m indentation would require 0.1 N in the linear model. If stiffness doubles, the same indentation requires twice the force. Conversely, holding force constant while increasing stiffness reduces displacement. Skin is not a perfect linear spring, and the effective stiffness depends on geometry, load and time. The model is valuable for designing comparisons rather than calculating a universal skin constant.

This mechanical dependence provides a testable prediction. Suppose two groups have similar neural receptor populations but one group has stiffer tissue surrounding the receptors. At a fixed external force, their internal tissue strains may differ; at a fixed external displacement, the force may differ. Either design can reveal an altered sensory response, but neither proves a change in channel abundance or excitability. A direct recording of mechanically activated current from isolated cells under matched stimuli would help address the molecular question. An intact tissue recording preserves physiological force transmission but makes it harder to identify the precise origin of a change.

The anatomy of a receptor organ is therefore not an ornament around the sensory membrane. Its geometry contributes to what that membrane experiences. For the Pacinian corpuscle, concentric lamellae contribute a temporal filter; for a superficial tactile unit, the distribution of terminal sites allows spatial sampling of local features; for a hair-follicle unit, the hair provides a lever coupling environmental movement to the ending. These examples show the same causal principle at different scales: structure determines the transformation between external stimulus and receptor deformation.

3. Four classical mechanoreceptor classes and hair-follicle endings

Merkel cell–neurite complexes are associated with slowly adapting type I (SA I) units. Merkel cells lie close to the basal layer of the epidermis, where surface geometry can generate local stress patterns. A typical SA I afferent has a relatively well-defined receptive field with several sensitive spots. When an edge presses against the skin, the discharge depends on where the edge lies relative to the branching terminals. When the deformation remains constant, the unit can retain a substantial sustained discharge. These properties make SA I activity particularly valuable for estimating spatial structure, local curvature and sustained indentation. They do not imply that a Merkel-associated afferent is silent whenever the object moves: the onset of indentation may generate a pronounced dynamic response too.

Meissner corpuscles, associated with rapidly adapting type I (RA I or FA I) units, lie in dermal papillae of glabrous skin. Their superficial position and compact receptive fields allow them to signal local changes in contact. They respond strongly to movement, transient indentation and relatively low-frequency vibration or flutter. When the force is held, firing falls sharply. During grip, local slip can move the skin at the object–finger interface, producing activity that can contribute to rapid correction of grip force. A sensory afferent does not itself command the muscle; spinal and supraspinal circuitry converts the information into an appropriate motor response.

Pacinian corpuscles are associated with rapidly adapting type II (RA II or FA II) units. A characteristic Pacinian corpuscle contains an axon terminal surrounded by concentric lamellae. This organisation transmits rapid changes in mechanical force efficiently but redistributes sustained force so that the terminal receives a much smaller persistent deformation. A brief vibration can therefore evoke repeated neural activity even when a stationary pressure of similar amplitude fails to sustain discharge. Pacinian afferents often have large, poorly bounded receptive fields and exquisite sensitivity to vibration at relatively high frequencies. A small oscillation transmitted through an object can excite a Pacinian unit even when the vibration source is not directly over the anatomical corpuscle. That is one reason a person can sense a vibrating tool through a handle.

Classical Ruffini endings are elongated connective-tissue-associated structures conventionally linked with slowly adapting type II (SA II) afferents. Physiologically identified SA II units respond strongly to skin stretch, often with directional preferences, and generally have more diffuse receptive fields than SA I units. Consider grasping a large object: its shape changes the stretch distribution over the hand, which can supplement information from muscles about hand configuration. The classical anatomical correspondence between every human SA II unit and a specific Ruffini corpuscle is less firmly established than the other three textbook pairings. In an examination, retain the conventional functional association, while recognising that direct physiology is not identical to an anatomical identification.

Hair-follicle receptors contribute especially to the sensitivity of hairy skin. A hair shaft provides a mechanical lever: displacement of the shaft can transfer force to tissue around its root. Lanceolate endings are among the structures associated with hair follicles. Physiological responses vary with the specific hair and afferent type. Some afferents respond preferentially to the direction of hair movement; others signal deflection more broadly. A moving air current or very light contact may consequently be detected without a large indentation of the skin surface. It would be misleading to assign all hair-follicle units one adaptation class and one conduction velocity.

These receptor classes can be organised along two useful dimensions: the persistence of the response to a sustained indentation and the spatial extent of the sensitive field. Type I units are conventionally associated with smaller and better-defined fields; type II units with larger, less distinct ones. Slowly adapting units retain activity during static deformation, whereas rapidly adapting units are more sensitive to changes. This classification is based on measured physiological responses, not on how fast a nerve impulse travels. A rapidly adapting afferent and a slowly adapting afferent can both conduct rapidly if they are myelinated Aβ fibres.

The usefulness of the four classes becomes clearer when considering a single manipulation task. Pressing a fingertip on a patterned surface generates slowly adapting activity encoding the deformation pattern. Moving over the surface creates low-frequency changes and fine vibrations that excite rapidly adapting populations. Stretch caused by the changing posture of the skin activates stretch-sensitive units. The system does not isolate one stimulus into one anatomical detector; it represents a complex contact through multiple partially overlapping channels. An experiment that excites a single unit strongly with one stimulus does not establish that the unit is exclusively sensitive to that stimulus.

Reading the physiological classification from experimental records

The distinction between type I and type II is easiest to understand through an experiment rather than a memorised table. Imagine a grid marked on glabrous skin. At each position, a probe delivers the same calibrated, non-damaging indentation while the investigator records from one afferent. A type I response tends to be associated with a relatively compact collection of sensitive points that can be located reproducibly. A type II response tends to extend over a broader region without as sharply defined a boundary. The map can be represented by plotting firing rate or detection threshold at each grid position. If stimulus intensity is increased, the mapped field may expand because more distant deformations reach a sufficient level.

Now consider the same recording during a step indentation at the most sensitive site. A unit that responds strongly while the probe is moving but little while it is held would be classified as rapidly adapting. A unit that continues discharging during a substantial maintained indentation would be classified as slowly adapting. Together these observations generate four possibilities. The investigator can then use further properties, including vibration tuning, stretch sensitivity and the spatial distribution of the most sensitive spots, to test the classification. No single feature alone gives all the information contained in the full response profile.

It is useful to distinguish a receptor's anatomical identity from its physiological identity. A nerve recording from an adult human cannot usually be followed directly to every terminal of the recorded neuron and examined histologically during the same measurement. Researchers therefore classify units from response properties and relate those classes to anatomical evidence from complementary investigations. The strongest associations are supported by convergent evidence: characteristic response properties, position in the skin, anatomical morphology and studies of identified structures. The conventional Ruffini–SA II association illustrates why this distinction is not merely philosophical; a useful examination classification can coexist with uncertainty about the detailed anatomical substrate of every physiological unit.

The temporal response of SA I units contributes to perception of a static spatial pattern, but their dynamic response contains additional information about changes in indentation. Suppose a moving edge first contacts one sensitive spot and then another within the same field. The firing pattern may reveal both the movement and the local pressure distribution. To establish whether a particular temporal pattern actually encodes an edge feature, compare matched stimuli across repeated trials and record populations of units, not just one response. A single unit's stronger firing at one location does not uniquely identify which physical attribute caused the difference.

Meissner-associated afferents often respond to movement at the skin surface. A familiar functional example is the moment a held object begins to slide. If a smooth object is nearly slipping, the skin–object contact contains small transient mechanical changes even before gross motion is visible. These changes can activate transient-sensitive afferents. A motor circuit can use this feedback to adjust grip. An organism does not require a conscious judgement of slip for every corrective response, and peripheral activity can contribute to both perception and automatic control. The same principle explains why a person may maintain grip on an object while simultaneously attending to an unrelated task.

The Pacinian contribution to texture becomes especially important when the surface contains fine features that produce vibrations during scanning. A coarse raised pattern can produce spatially distinct deformation at the skin surface; much finer features may instead be represented strongly through temporal vibrations. If a periodically ridged surface has spatial wavelength λ and the finger moves at speed u, the encounter frequency is approximately f = u/λ. For u = 40 mm/s and λ = 0.2 mm, the encounter frequency is 200 Hz. Doubling scan speed doubles this frequency without changing the surface's physical wavelength. This relationship shows how active movement changes the sensory input. It is a simplified model assuming constant scan speed and regularly spaced features; natural textures produce more complex vibration spectra.

The functional contrast between Meissner and Pacinian responses is not a rigid frequency boundary. Both populations can respond to a range of mechanical waveforms, and their relative contributions depend on amplitude and contact area. For a low-amplitude stimulus, a receptor class with a low threshold at that frequency may be recruited while another remains below threshold. At higher amplitudes, both may respond. In a graph of threshold versus frequency, the preferred frequency is the region where less displacement is needed, not necessarily the frequency producing the highest firing rate at every possible amplitude.

Skin stretch presents a different coding problem. A tangential force may deform the skin directionally, so an afferent can show different responses when the same amount of force is applied along different axes. Direction-sensitive stretch responses provide information about the changing shape of the skin during grasping and motion. Such information can supplement, rather than replace, proprioceptive signals from muscle and tendon. An experiment investigating directional tuning should rotate the direction of stretch while controlling its amplitude; otherwise a stronger response in one direction could arise from a larger deformation rather than directional selectivity.

In hairy skin, receptor responses can be differentiated by the mechanical role of different hair types and terminal arrangements. A long shaft can amplify the effect of a small distal displacement at the follicle, depending on its stiffness and anchoring. An experiment moving a hair without indenting neighbouring skin can help establish whether a recorded unit responds to hair movement itself. When interpreting such experiments across species, avoid assuming that a receptor classification established in a human fingertip applies unchanged to every rodent hair follicle. The physiological principle of selective mechanical coupling is general; the detailed anatomy and distributions can differ.

4. Piezo2 and the conversion of deformation into current

A sensory ending cannot transmit a mechanical indentation down the axon as a mechanical wave. It must convert the deformation into an electrical change in a living membrane. Mechanically activated ion channels provide a molecular route for this conversion. The probability that a channel is open changes when force is transmitted to its membrane environment. A change in the population of open channels changes the membrane conductance. Ion movement then changes the electrical potential of the cell.

Piezo2 is a major mechanically activated cation channel in mammalian touch sensation. It is expressed in many low-threshold sensory neurons and in Merkel cells. At the negative resting potential typical of an excitable cell, opening a cation-permeable channel with a reversal potential near zero usually produces an inward net current. Cations moving through the channel change the charge distribution across the membrane, causing depolarisation. The current does not arise because mechanical force synthesises ATP or pumps positive ions actively across the membrane. The ion gradients already exist, maintained indirectly by active transport, and channel opening allows passive movement down electrochemical gradients.

A simplified relation is I = g(Vm − Erev). Here I is ionic current in amperes, g is conductance in siemens, Vm is membrane potential in volts and Erev is the channel's reversal potential in volts. In the usual electrophysiological convention, positive current is outward. If g is 2 nS, Vm is −60 mV and Erev is 0 mV, I equals −120 pA. The negative value represents net inward current. If the membrane is clamped at the reversal potential, there is no net current through those channels despite their being open. Individual ions may still move in opposite directions. A zero-current measurement at Erev is therefore not by itself evidence that the mechanically activated channels failed to open.

A receptor potential is a graded change in membrane potential that reflects sensory stimulation. In a primary afferent, the term generator potential often describes the local graded depolarisation that brings an excitable region of the axon toward its spike threshold. A stronger deformation may open more channels or increase their probability of opening, yielding a larger depolarisation, within the mechanical and electrical limits of the receptor. Once voltage-gated sodium channels at the impulse-initiation region are sufficiently recruited, regenerative action potentials propagate toward the central nervous system. The local graded potential is not the same electrical phenomenon as a propagating spike.

The Merkel cell–neurite complex demonstrates that a channel expressed in a non-neuronal cell can shape an afferent's tactile response. A mechanical indentation can activate both sensory neurites and Merkel cells. Merkel cells can depolarise and communicate with the associated neurite, contributing particularly to the maintained response to deformation. Evidence from several experimental systems supports a model with more than one transduction site, although the relative contribution depends on the preparation, stimulus and time phase examined. Do not assume that every rapidly adapting ion-channel current must produce a rapidly adapting whole afferent. A receptor organ includes several components whose interaction can maintain neural activity after an individual channel current has declined.

The molecular evidence for Piezo2 was strengthened by studies in which the channel was removed genetically from selected cell populations. Comparing mechanically activated currents and touch responses in genetically modified and control animals helps distinguish the presence of a protein from its physiological necessity. Woo and colleagues (2014) tested Merkel-cell mechanotransduction, while Ranade and colleagues (2014) examined the broader contribution of Piezo2 to touch in mice. Reduced mechanical sensitivity following a targeted deletion supports an essential contribution in the targeted system. It does not establish that Piezo2 is the only mechanosensitive molecule in every species, every cutaneous receptor or every kind of mechanical sensation.

The correct experimental logic is particularly important. Suppose a modified sensory neuron no longer fires when the skin is indented. If an electrical stimulus applied to the axon still generates a normally propagating action potential, the experiment argues against complete loss of axonal excitability. If the isolated sensory cell lacks a mechanically activated current at a controlled membrane potential, the defect is more directly localised to mechanotransduction. Even then, an investigator must distinguish loss of the channel itself from changes in membrane mechanics, cell development or coupling between receptor structures. A rescue experiment restoring channel expression can strengthen the interpretation. These are different levels of evidence rather than interchangeable ways of naming the same observation.

From channel conductance to membrane potential: a quantitative bridge

Opening a mechanosensitive channel changes both the current and the effective electrical resistance of the membrane. The resulting voltage change is not necessarily proportional to stimulus strength over the entire range. At very small stimuli, only a small fraction of channels may open. At sufficiently strong stimuli, channel opening may approach saturation, and increasing the deformation further cannot recruit the same conductance indefinitely. The membrane potential also approaches the reversal potential of the open channel population, decreasing the electrical driving force. These two factors can limit further depolarisation.

To illustrate the interaction between a resting leak and a mechanically activated conductance, consider an isopotential membrane containing a leak conductance gL with reversal potential EL and a mechanically activated conductance gM with reversal potential EM. At steady state, neglecting other currents and externally injected current, the sum of currents is zero: gL(V − EL) + gM(V − EM) = 0. Rearranging gives V = (gLEL + gMEM)/(gL + gM). Each conductance must be expressed in the same unit, and each voltage in the same unit. The expression is a conductance-weighted average of reversal potentials. It does not describe the regenerative action potential, where several voltage-dependent conductances change dynamically.

For an illustrative membrane with gL = 4 nS and EL = −65 mV, the resting voltage is −65 mV when gM is zero. If mechanical stimulation adds gM = 1 nS with EM = 0 mV, the steady voltage becomes (4 × −65 + 1 × 0)/5 = −52 mV. Increasing gM to 4 nS gives −32.5 mV. The second example demonstrates a substantial depolarisation, which in an actual excitable cell could recruit voltage-gated channels before the simple steady state was reached. The relationship is nonlinear because the added conductance changes both numerator and denominator. Doubling a mechanosensitive conductance need not double the depolarisation.

Membrane capacitance means that voltage cannot change instantaneously in response to a finite current. In a passive model, the time constant is τm = C/(gL + gM), where capacitance C is measured in farads and total conductance in siemens. With C = 20 pF and total conductance of 5 nS, τm = 0.004 s, or 4 ms. An increased conductance shortens this idealised electrical time constant. Mechanical stimulus duration, channel opening kinetics and membrane capacitance can consequently influence the speed of a receptor potential. It would be incorrect to identify τm directly with the adaptation time constant of an entire receptor organ, because the latter can additionally involve time-dependent tissue deformation and changes in excitability.

The spatial separation of transduction and spike initiation also matters. A current generated at a terminal spreads passively through the local membrane and can depolarise an adjacent excitable region. The voltage change diminishes with distance according to electrical properties of the neurite. The site with sufficient density of voltage-gated channels can initiate spikes once threshold is reached. A change that reduces generator potential amplitude might prevent spiking without altering the action-potential machinery itself. Conversely, a change in threshold can alter spike output even when the generator current is unchanged.

In a Merkel cell–neurite complex, communication between the specialised cell and its associated afferent adds another level of regulation. Activation of a mechanosensitive conductance in the Merkel cell changes its electrical state; subsequent communication contributes to activity of the neurite. This is why identifying Piezo2 expression in the Merkel cell is not the same as demonstrating that all touch responses are produced exclusively there. Different phases of a stimulus can engage direct neuronal transduction and contributions from the Merkel cell. In experimental reasoning, preserve the distinction between a molecular conductance, a cellular depolarisation, intercellular signalling and the final afferent spike response.

Evidence from human loss-of-function variants provides a complementary approach to animal experiments. Chesler and colleagues (2016) described individuals with damaging PIEZO2 variants whose discriminative touch and proprioceptive abilities were selectively impaired, while certain gentle mechanical responses in hairy skin remained. The observation supports a major contribution of PIEZO2 to human mechanosensation, not a claim that every form of touch in every tissue depends absolutely on that one channel. Human phenotypes also integrate development, compensation, receptor diversity and behaviour. Their interpretation is strongest when combined with direct cellular and animal studies.

A common multiple-statement question combines facts drawn from different levels. For example, it may correctly state that Piezo2 is mechanically activated, then incorrectly claim that opening it generates an action potential independently of the membrane potential and the availability of voltage-gated channels. The first statement is a description of gating; the second is a claim about the whole excitable cell. Evaluate them separately. Even at a large mechanically activated conductance, net ionic current depends on the electrochemical driving force, and spike production depends on the current state of the excitable membrane.

5. Adaptation, temporal coding and vibration

Adaptation describes how a receptor's response evolves while the stimulus is maintained. Apply an indentation quickly, hold the probe stationary, and remove it quickly. A slowly adapting unit may fire rapidly at onset and then maintain a lower discharge during the hold. A rapidly adapting unit may fire a brief burst at onset, become almost silent during the hold and respond again when the probe is removed. The physical stimulus was present throughout the interval. The absence of spikes during the hold reflects how the unit encodes the stimulus, not necessarily a disappearance of the stimulus.

One reason for adaptation is mechanical filtering. In a lamellar Pacinian corpuscle, relative movement between layers and redistribution of stress reduce sustained deformation of the central ending. Early physiological studies by Loewenstein and Mendelson examined the components of this adaptation. The mechanical contribution can be explored by comparing responses of an intact corpuscle and an ending after alteration of surrounding structures. If a more sustained response appears when the capsule is altered, the surrounding tissue contributed to the original transient behaviour. However, the ending has its own electrical properties, so the demonstration does not prove that adaptation is entirely mechanical.

Channel gating kinetics, voltage-dependent conductances and other features of the receptor cell can also shape the decline in generator potential or spike discharge. For example, a mechanically activated current may decrease during sustained force even if the applied force remains fixed. An axon can show spike-frequency adaptation during sustained depolarisation. These mechanisms are conceptually separable. They may act together, and a neural response observed at the nerve cannot be assigned to one mechanism solely from the appearance of its trace. Mechanical controls, current recordings and direct electrical activation help to distinguish these possibilities.

Consider a simple phenomenological adaptation model for firing rate after a step stimulus: r(t) = r∞ + (r0 − r∞)exp(−t/τ). Here r0 is initial firing rate in spikes per second, r∞ is the maintained rate in spikes per second, t is time in seconds, and τ is an adaptation time constant in seconds. This is not a universal receptor law. It approximates an onset response whose excess above the maintained component decays exponentially. For r0 = 80 spikes/s, r∞ = 20 spikes/s and τ = 0.5 s, the rate at 0.5 s is 20 + 60/e, approximately 42.1 spikes/s. After 1 s it is approximately 28.1 spikes/s. If r∞ is instead near zero, the same mathematical form represents an idealised transient response. The exponential model describes the measured behaviour without identifying its molecular cause.

Vibration is a repeated change rather than a simple step. A sinusoidal displacement can be represented as x(t) = A sin(2πft), where A is amplitude in metres, f is frequency in hertz and t is time in seconds. Its peak speed is 2πfA. For a fixed displacement amplitude, doubling frequency doubles peak speed, while the period halves. This explains why a comparison of responses at different frequencies must specify whether displacement, velocity, acceleration or force is held constant. Receptor and tissue mechanics depend on these variables, so an apparent frequency preference can change with the stimulus calibration.

Meissner-associated RA I afferents are commonly especially sensitive to lower-frequency flutter, whereas Pacinian RA II units have very low displacement thresholds for higher-frequency vibration, often around a few hundred hertz under suitable experimental conditions. These ranges overlap and depend on contact area and stimulus geometry. The strongest response at a particular frequency does not imply complete insensitivity at every other frequency. A threshold curve plots the minimum amplitude needed to reach a specified response criterion as frequency changes. A lower curve indicates greater sensitivity, the reverse of an ordinary firing-rate graph at fixed stimulus amplitude.

Afferent spike timing can carry temporal information. When a receptor responds at a consistent phase of a periodic vibration, the spike train is phase-locked to that vibration. It need not produce exactly one spike per cycle. A unit may skip some cycles but preserve the preferred phase when it does discharge. At high frequency, refractory properties constrain how frequently an individual axon can fire. Combining timing information from a population can provide information even when individual firing rates do not equal the stimulus frequency. The response to vibration therefore cannot be interpreted by counting spikes alone without considering their temporal arrangement.

Stimulus waveform and the separation of mechanical from neural adaptation

A ramp-and-hold stimulus contains a dynamic phase and a static phase. During the ramp, the probe's position changes continuously. During the hold, the displacement is maintained. If two ramps reach the same final position but differ in speed, the dynamic input differs while the final static indentation is equal. This is a useful way of separating sensitivity to rate of change from sensitivity to maintained displacement. A unit with pronounced velocity sensitivity may fire more rapidly during the faster ramp even though the final displacement is unchanged.

Suppose a probe moves 0.5 mm in 50 ms in the first trial and 0.5 mm in 250 ms in the second. For constant-speed ramps, the speeds are 10 mm/s and 2 mm/s respectively. If the first trial produces more onset spikes, this is consistent with dynamic sensitivity. However, faster indentation can also create a different transient force because the tissue is viscoelastic. The conclusion should therefore specify that the response is related to the dynamic mechanical stimulus rather than claiming the sensory membrane measures velocity directly and exclusively.

A step stimulus can be considered as an ideal limiting case of a ramp with very rapid onset, but real probes cannot change displacement instantaneously. Their finite acceleration and mechanical ringing may evoke additional vibration-sensitive responses. When comparing receptors with high sensitivity to rapid changes, apparatus calibration is particularly important. A transient response might partly reflect overshoot of the stimulating device rather than only the imposed ideal waveform. Recording the actual stimulus trace alongside the neural response allows the student to see whether a large burst coincides with an unexpected mechanical oscillation.

The offset response of a rapidly adapting unit deserves careful attention. During release, forces in the surrounding tissue redistribute and the ending undergoes a transient deformation. A mechanically sensitive channel may be activated as the membrane environment changes even if the direction of macroscopic displacement is opposite to the onset. The existence of an offset burst is not evidence that action potentials themselves have reversed direction or sign. Both onset and offset may produce depolarising events at the sensory ending that trigger normally oriented propagated impulses.

Adaptation is sometimes described as making the nervous system insensitive to constant stimuli, but this is only partly true. Different receptor classes maintain different amounts of activity. Rapidly adapting units can respond again if a new transient occurs on top of a maintained background. Slowly adapting units can continue reporting the background deformation. The combination allows the nervous system to preserve information about static contact while remaining sensitive to changes. A sensory system containing only perfectly adapting units would have difficulty maintaining direct information about a stationary indentation, while a system of purely sustained detectors might represent rapid changes less effectively.

A difference between sensory adaptation and habituation should also be maintained. Adaptation at the receptor concerns changes in peripheral responsiveness during a stimulus or stimulus sequence. Habituation is a reduction of a behavioural or central response during repeated stimulation and can involve changes in neural processing beyond the receptor. The distinction can be investigated by recording peripheral input and behavioural output together. If the afferent response remains stable while the behavioural response declines, a purely peripheral explanation is inadequate. If both decline, peripheral changes may contribute, but central changes are not thereby excluded.

For periodic stimuli, displacement amplitude thresholds are one useful measure of sensitivity. Imagine an illustrative frequency-response experiment. At 20 Hz, receptor X reaches the chosen firing criterion at 4 μm and receptor Y at 40 μm. At 250 Hz, X requires 30 μm and Y requires 0.5 μm. At 20 Hz X is ten times as sensitive by this amplitude-threshold metric, whereas at 250 Hz Y is sixty times as sensitive. A larger amplitude requirement represents lower sensitivity. These values are hypothetical, and the relative comparison applies only to the specified recording criterion and mechanical conditions. If one measured firing rate at a very large amplitude instead, the comparison might differ because both receptors could be recruited or saturate.

This analysis links temporal filtering to the practical interpretation of experimental graphs. Always identify the vertical variable before saying a peak means greater sensitivity. If the graph shows minimum threshold amplitude, a trough represents the preferred region. If the graph shows firing rate at fixed amplitude, a peak represents a stronger response. If it shows probability of detection, the meaning depends on the behavioural criterion and stimulus values. Confusing these three graph types is a common source of avoidable examination errors.

6. Receptive fields, spatial sampling and lateral inhibition

A receptive field of a cutaneous afferent is the region of skin within which an appropriately defined stimulus changes that unit's activity. The definition is operational: it depends on the stimulus intensity, direction and the criterion used to decide whether the response is significant. Mapping the field with a very weak probe may reveal only a small central region, while a stronger stimulus recruits a larger zone. Therefore receptive-field size is not a perfectly fixed anatomical circle.

A single afferent can be sensitive at several spots because its peripheral branch divides into several endings. Adjacent afferents also have overlapping receptive fields. This overlap enables the nervous system to estimate stimulus location through a pattern of responses. Suppose an indentation is between the most sensitive sites of two afferents. Both may respond at intermediate rates, and a small shift in position may increase one response while decreasing the other. Population comparison can then provide more precise positional information than a simple assumption that every spike corresponds to a single point.

The work of Johansson and Vallbo on human glabrous hand skin used recordings from individual mechanoreceptive units to characterise receptive fields and adaptation patterns. Their investigations related the distribution of afferent classes to tactile sensitivity. An important inference is that the density of peripheral sampling is not uniform across the hand. Fingers used for fine manipulation have relatively dense spatial sampling. The study illustrates why anatomical images alone do not reveal the full functional organisation: identification of physiological classes requires controlled mechanical stimulation and neural recording.

Two-point discrimination is the ability to perceive two simultaneous spatially separated contacts as distinct. When the separation is extremely small, the afferent population patterns generated by the two points may overlap so extensively that they are difficult to distinguish from a single contact. At greater separation, the two activation peaks become more separable. Fingertips generally support finer spatial discrimination than regions such as the back. The difference arises partly from receptor density and field size and partly from central representation and processing.

A simple geometric model illustrates one factor. If receptors were arranged on a uniform square grid at density ρ receptors/cm², the approximate interreceptor spacing would be d = 1/√ρ centimetres. For ρ = 100 receptors/cm², d = 0.1 cm = 1 mm. For ρ = 25 receptors/cm², d = 0.2 cm = 2 mm. Increasing density fourfold halves grid spacing. This does not predict the exact two-point threshold, because true receptor positions are not a square grid and central circuits integrate overlapping responses. It gives a useful scale for understanding why greater sampling density can support finer spatial discrimination.

Lateral inhibition is a circuit operation in which activity associated with one location suppresses activity in neighbouring channels. It can increase spatial contrast: a response representing the strongest input remains prominent, whereas surrounding weaker responses are reduced. Consider five neighbouring channels receiving excitation of 2, 6, 10, 6 and 2 arbitrary units. In an illustrative circuit subtracting 0.25 of each immediate neighbour, the central response is 10 − 0.25(6 + 6) = 7, whereas its immediate neighbour becomes 6 − 0.25(2 + 10) = 3. The ratio of central to adjacent activity increases from 10/6 to 7/3. This is a pedagogical model, not a measurement of the exact synaptic weights in the tactile system.

Inhibition does not create information absent from the sensory inputs. If two contacts produce exactly the same population pattern as one contact, a deterministic contrast-enhancing operation cannot reconstruct their original distinction from that pattern alone. Spatial discrimination depends on mechanical transmission, receptor distribution, population coding, inhibitory circuits and the behavioural decision criterion. Very strong stimulation may spread deformation widely and can make a simple two-point task harder to interpret. Unequal pressure, asynchronous contact, visual cues or differences in total force can allow a participant to report two contacts without actually resolving two spatial locations. Well-controlled comparisons must address these factors.

Population codes, receptive-field overlap and psychometric interpretation

A spatial population code can be explained with a simple numerical example. Imagine three adjacent sensory units with receptive-field centres at x = 0, 2 and 4 mm. A probe at x = 1 mm evokes 30, 30 and 5 spikes/s. Moving the probe to x = 2 mm evokes 15, 50 and 15 spikes/s. Neither pattern is a literal image of the contact, but the relative distribution of activity changes systematically with position. If the brain has learned the relationship between afferent activity and contact location, it can estimate position from the population response.

One elementary estimate is the activity-weighted centre, x̂ = Σrixi/Σri, where ri is the response of unit i and xi is its receptive-field centre. In the first illustrative pattern, x̂ = (30 × 0 + 30 × 2 + 5 × 4)/(30 + 30 + 5) = 80/65 ≈ 1.23 mm. In the second, x̂ = (15 × 0 + 50 × 2 + 15 × 4)/80 = 2 mm. These estimates are not claims that the brain uses this exact formula. They show how overlapping fields can support an estimate more detailed than simply selecting the centre of the most active unit. A real population contains noise, nonuniform receptor positions and diverse response functions.

This model also reveals a limitation. A weighted average can locate the centre of activity but cannot necessarily distinguish one central stimulus from two equally strong stimuli at neighbouring locations. Both patterns may have the same centre of mass while differing in spatial spread or number of peaks. Different perceptual tasks therefore require different features of the population response. A useful answer to a question about two-point discrimination should mention the spatial pattern, not merely the total rate or mean position.

Two-point performance is typically represented by a psychometric relationship: probability of a correct response as a function of stimulus separation. When two contacts are close together, a participant may perform near chance in a properly designed forced-choice task. At larger separations the probability increases. A threshold is assigned according to a specified criterion on that curve. In an illustrative two-alternative task, a criterion of 75 percent correct might yield 2 mm for a fingertip and 20 mm for a forearm. The tenfold difference is a statement about performance under those particular conditions, not a direct count of sensory endings. Values depend on stimulus protocol, site and participant.

Consider a dataset at separations of 1, 2, 3 and 4 mm, with correct response rates of 55, 68, 82 and 92 percent. If the criterion is 75 percent and simple linear interpolation is used between 2 and 3 mm, the estimate is 2 + (75 − 68)/(82 − 68) = 2.5 mm. Interpolation assumes that performance changes approximately linearly across this narrow interval. A fuller psychometric analysis would fit a suitable curve and quantify uncertainty, but the simple calculation illustrates how a threshold can be obtained without treating the first correct trial as the definitive limit.

The number of trials affects confidence in the measured proportion. If a participant answers correctly in three of four trials at a particular separation, the observed proportion is 75 percent, but the uncertainty is substantial. Seventy-five correct responses in one hundred trials provide stronger evidence about performance under the specified conditions. This is why repeated trials matter. A comparison based on only one or two presentations can be influenced strongly by chance, criterion shifts or momentary attention.

Lateral inhibition can also be explored with two spatial peaks. If excitation has two maxima separated by a shallow valley, suppressive interactions among neighbouring channels may reduce responses in the valley relative to the maxima. The resulting contrast can help preserve the representation of two locations. If a pharmacological manipulation reduces inhibition while peripheral inputs remain unchanged, the two peaks may become less distinct centrally. An observed reduction in discrimination would be consistent with a role for inhibition, but a drug may affect multiple neural processes. Controls measuring general excitability, attention and peripheral sensory response would strengthen the inference.

Receptive-field overlap, convergence and inhibition are therefore not competing explanations in a simple either-or sense. Overlap can provide graded information about stimulus position; convergence can combine related inputs; inhibition can enhance spatial differences. Excessive pooling can blur spatial structure, whereas insufficient overlap may produce gaps in sampling. The useful arrangement depends on the resolution and sensitivity demanded by the sensory task. High acuity and high sensitivity are related but not identical objectives.

7. Afferent classes, conduction and sensory latency

A receptor's adaptation rate and its axon's conduction velocity are different properties. Adaptation describes the time course of neural responsiveness during a continuing stimulus. Conduction velocity describes the speed at which an already initiated action potential travels along the axon. Myelination and larger axonal diameter generally allow faster propagation. Most classical glabrous-skin tactile units belong to the myelinated Aβ class. Thinly myelinated Aδ and unmyelinated C fibres generally conduct more slowly, although some of these fibres participate in low-threshold touch rather than only pain or temperature.

The importance of fast conduction can be seen during manipulation. If a glass begins to slip, sensory information must arrive soon enough for corrective motor output to limit the movement. A long conduction delay would allow the glass to travel farther before correction. Nonetheless, the total latency of correction includes receptor transduction, central conduction, synaptic integration, motor-axon conduction and muscle activation. A measurement of behaviour alone cannot identify the exact velocity of a cutaneous afferent.

For a path of length L metres traversed at speed v metres per second, conduction time is t = L/v seconds. An axon with a conduction distance of 0.8 m and velocity of 50 m/s has an idealised propagation delay of 0.016 s, or 16 ms. If the same path is traversed at 2 m/s, the delay is 0.4 s. The calculation assumes constant effective propagation speed along the specified path. Real sensory pathways consist of segments with different diameters and synapses between neurons, so this relation applies to a particular measured segment or a specified average.

When conduction is assessed experimentally using two stimulation sites along a nerve, comparing their response latencies can cancel delays common to both trials. If the sites are 0.3 m apart and the difference in arrival times is 6 ms, velocity is 0.3/0.006 = 50 m/s. The use of latency difference rather than absolute response latency helps isolate the segment under study. The experiment still assumes that the same population of fibres is recruited in both trials. If stimulus intensity changes recruitment or if different responses are identified as the onset, the estimate may be misleading.

A lesion that interrupts large myelinated sensory axons can reduce vibration sense and discriminative touch while leaving some other sensations relatively intact. This selectivity illustrates why peripheral fibres are functionally specialised, but does not establish that every individual tactile quality is conveyed exclusively through one axon class. Aβ axons transmit multiple types of low-threshold mechanoreceptive information; the sensory quality depends additionally on receptor properties and central connections. When predicting a lesion phenotype, first ask whether it affects a particular receptor, an axon population, a central pathway or a region of cortex. These levels yield different patterns of deficit.

8. The dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway

Information required for precise touch discrimination and vibration from the body ascends primarily through the dorsal column–medial lemniscus system, often abbreviated DCML. The pathway is also important for conscious proprioception. To understand its organisation, trace a signal from the skin of the right foot to the cerebral cortex. The first-order afferent has its cell body in a right dorsal root ganglion. Its peripheral branch receives information from the receptor, and its central process enters the spinal cord through a right dorsal root.

For the principal ascending route, the central branch enters the ipsilateral dorsal column. Axons representing the lower body occupy the fasciculus gracilis, a medial bundle. Axons from the upper body enter the fasciculus cuneatus, situated more laterally in spinal regions where these fibres are present. Lower-limb and upper-limb information is therefore organised spatially even at the level of the spinal cord. Some primary sensory afferents also send collaterals into spinal grey matter, allowing participation in local circuits; the presence of these collaterals does not change where the main DCML ascending branch travels.

The dorsal-column axons reach the caudal medulla, where lower-body information relays in the nucleus gracilis and upper-body information in the nucleus cuneatus. The axons of second-order neurons cross the midline as internal arcuate fibres and contribute to the medial lemniscus on the opposite side. The right-foot signal, which ascended in the right spinal dorsal column, now travels in the left medial lemniscus. This crossing in the medulla is the key to determining the laterality of sensory losses from lesions at different levels.

The medial lemniscus carries information toward the ventral posterolateral nucleus of the thalamus (VPL). Thalamic neurons send third-order projections through the internal capsule to the primary somatosensory cortex in the postcentral gyrus. Tactile information from the right foot is consequently represented mainly in the left cerebral hemisphere. The thalamus is an organised relay participating in modulation and transmission of sensory information, not an unstructured junction at which all sensory pathways lose their spatial identity.

The face follows a related but distinct route. Most discriminative tactile information from the face travels through the trigeminal nerve to the principal sensory trigeminal nucleus in the brainstem. Many second-order fibres cross and ascend to the ventral posteromedial thalamic nucleus (VPM), with details and exceptions depending on the territory. Thalamic projections reach the face representation of somatosensory cortex. For standard examination problems, distinguish body touch via VPL from facial touch via VPM, rather than forcing the face into the spinal dorsal-column scheme.

Crude touch has additional ascending routes, including pathways within the anterolateral system. That system is especially important for pain and temperature, which are outside the main scope here. Some tactile information can therefore remain accessible when the DCML pathway is impaired. Statements that a dorsal-column lesion abolishes all possible touch on the affected body surface are too broad. A more defensible prediction is impairment of discriminative touch, vibration and conscious position information carried by the damaged fibres, with possible preservation of some simpler touch detection.

A lesion of the right dorsal column in the spinal cord can produce ipsilateral deficits below the affected level because the principal fibres have not yet crossed. A lesion of the left medial lemniscus in the brainstem can impair similar sensations on the right side of the body, because the fibres crossed in the medulla. A lesion of the left primary somatosensory cortex can disrupt discrimination on the right body regions corresponding to the damaged cortical territory. These predictions refer to isolated idealised lesions. Real lesions can extend into adjacent pathways and produce mixed signs, so reasoning must be tied to the stipulated lesion rather than treating all spinal or cerebral injury as identical.

Reasoning from lesion patterns and the distinction between pathway and modality

A pathway diagram becomes useful when it permits predictions about a new situation. Consider an isolated lesion of the right fasciculus gracilis at a thoracic spinal level. The principal ascending fibres carrying lower-body information on that side are interrupted. The deficit occurs below the lesion because the ascending axons from receptors entering at lower segments pass through the affected level. Fibres entering above it do not traverse the same damaged segment. If the lesion is confined to gracilis, upper-limb inputs travelling in cuneatus at higher levels are not necessarily affected.

Now move the lesion to the left medial lemniscus after the medullary crossing. The affected information originates mainly from the right side of the body, rather than the left. The same sensory modality can thus show different laterality depending on the position of damage. The crossing should be identified from the pathway before reasoning about side. Memorising that sensory cortex is contralateral without considering the spinal pathway leads to the incorrect prediction for lesions below the decussation.

A lesion of a peripheral sensory nerve produces a different pattern because it interrupts fibres according to the distribution of that nerve. It may impair multiple modalities within that territory, depending on which fibre populations are damaged. A dorsal root lesion can affect the distribution of the corresponding root, with possible overlap from adjacent roots. By contrast, a small lesion in a cortical somatotopic region may affect a particular representation without damaging the corresponding peripheral receptor or axon. These distinctions help separate peripheral, segmental, tract and cortical abnormalities in idealised experimental problems.

The fact that a person can feel a touch without resolving its location provides further information about the pathway. Different ascending systems convey overlapping but nonidentical sensory information. An isolated deficit in vibration and discriminative touch is more consistent with disruption of large-fibre or DCML-related processing than an equal loss of every sensory quality. Preserved crude touch does not prove that the dorsal columns are intact. Conversely, impaired two-point performance does not prove a dorsal-column lesion because peripheral receptor organisation and cortical processing also influence the task.

A complete sensory pathway is a network, not a wire with only three isolated cells. The classical first-, second- and third-order scheme describes the major route from receptor to cortex, which is appropriate for anatomical reasoning. However, branching, local spinal circuits, brainstem modulation and thalamocortical interactions affect information transmission. In particular, the first-order axon can issue collaterals while its main ascending branch remains in the dorsal column. A synapse made by a collateral does not imply that the entire principal ascending signal must now be described as a second-order spinal fibre.

A useful experimental prediction follows from this branching. If a manipulation interrupts ascending dorsal-column fibres but spares local spinal circuitry and the entering peripheral branches, some local sensorimotor responses could remain possible even when conscious fine discrimination is impaired. The exact retained behaviour depends on the experimental preparation and the circuitry involved. The prediction is about separation of local and ascending information routes, not a guarantee that every reflex is preserved in any real injury.

The thalamic division into VPL and VPM also provides a way to test anatomical interpretation. A question may describe a unit receiving input from tactile receptors of the left cheek. The principal facial mechanosensory pathway is trigeminal, so its thalamic relay is generally VPM in the contralateral hemisphere, not VPL simply because the modality is touch. If the stimulus is applied to the hand instead, body somatosensory input normally reaches VPL. The modality is the same, but anatomical territory determines the route. Knowing both modality and origin is necessary to predict the relay.

When interpreting a diagram of the brainstem, use the terms dorsal, ventral, medial and lateral with respect to the actual anatomical orientation. The medial lemniscus is named for its position and organisation within the brainstem; the layout of fibres can change along its course. For examination-level questions, the critical facts are the medullary synapse, the sensory decussation and subsequent contralateral ascent. Detailed rotations of the tract are unnecessary unless explicitly supplied in a diagram or question.

9. Cortical somatotopy and perceptual integration

Somatotopy is the orderly mapping of body regions onto neural structures. In primary somatosensory cortex, different areas respond preferentially to information from different parts of the contralateral body. The familiar sensory homunculus is a schematic representation of this organisation. The cortical territory assigned to a region is not proportional simply to the surface area or physical mass of that region. Hands and lips, for example, require fine sensory discrimination and occupy disproportionately large cortical territories.

The primary somatosensory cortex includes multiple interconnected subdivisions, conventionally associated with Brodmann areas 3a, 3b, 1 and 2. Area 3b is especially associated with cutaneous input; area 3a is strongly associated with proprioceptive information. Other areas participate in processing more complex features such as texture and shape through integration of inputs. These subdivisions are not isolated boxes that each perform one exclusively defined perceptual act. Processing takes place through interconnected neuronal populations and extends beyond S1.

Imagine recognising an embossed shape by moving a finger across it. Each moment samples a different piece of surface. Spatial patterns from slowly adapting units convey local deformation and edges, transient signals convey movement and fine surface events, and proprioceptive information reports the changing configuration of the hand. Central processing must relate these inputs over time. The capacity to identify an object by touch without vision, called stereognosis, therefore requires intact elementary sensation as well as higher-order integration. A failure to identify an object is not sufficient evidence that a particular cutaneous receptor has been lost.

The size of a cortical receptive field can differ from the field of one peripheral afferent because central neurons receive convergent inputs. Convergence can improve sensitivity when small signals sum, but it may reduce spatial resolution when the inputs are pooled indiscriminately. Circuits combining excitatory and inhibitory inputs can preserve or sharpen differences. Cortical response patterns depend on the preceding sensory and behavioural context, and cortical maps are capable of experience-dependent changes. Somatotopy is organised but not an immutable photograph of the body.

These ideas also explain why a psychophysical discrimination threshold need not predict a single receptor property. Attention, prior experience, stimulus history, task design and central neural processing can influence whether a person reports a sensation. The threshold is a behavioural measurement under specific conditions. It is distinct from the minimum displacement that evokes an action potential in one afferent. Relating the two requires measuring the relevant neural population and the behaviour with controlled stimuli.

Cortical representation and the integration of active touch

A cortical map describes the spatial organisation of input, not the size of the perceptual image. In an electrophysiological mapping experiment, the investigator records activity from cortical neurons while stimulating different body locations. Moving the recording electrode across the cortical surface changes the most effective skin region in a systematic fashion. Such results provide evidence for somatotopy. An analogous map can be explored with modern imaging methods, but a change in aggregate activity is not identical to a direct recording of individual receptive fields.

The disproportionate cortical representation of sensitive body regions is called cortical magnification. Suppose two regions occupy equal areas of skin but one is represented by a much larger cortical area. More cortical tissue may permit finer processing or integration of the information from that region. However, this relationship is not a simple formula that lets the student calculate perceptual acuity solely from cortical surface area. The peripheral density of receptors, their receptive-field properties, the number of convergent inputs and the architecture of cortical circuits also affect discrimination.

Central neurons often receive converging signals from multiple peripheral units. This convergence can produce sensitivity to combinations of features. For example, an oriented edge may activate an arrangement of peripheral fields whose combined activity differs from that produced by an edge rotated through a different angle. A central neuron receiving appropriately organised excitatory and inhibitory inputs could respond more strongly to one edge orientation. The response would represent a relationship among inputs rather than the location of a single receptor. This conceptual mechanism illustrates how elementary spatial measurements can contribute to more complex tactile representations.

The integration of information over time is equally important. A finger exploring an object does not receive all information about the object at once. Contact changes as the finger moves, and the neural system must relate the present response to earlier ones. A static contact may provide local curvature; a sequence of contacts reveals a larger contour. If the finger changes direction, the movement-related information helps establish how successive tactile samples fit together. Touch therefore shares a general principle with other senses: neural representations combine information across multiple sampling positions and times.

A distinction between active and passive tactile exploration can be demonstrated by comparing a stationary finger over which a stimulus is moved with a moving finger exploring a stationary surface. Both conditions can be designed to generate similar local surface velocities, but the active condition additionally includes motor commands and proprioceptive feedback. Differences in perception can therefore reflect more than peripheral receptor sensitivity. Experimental comparisons should control local stimulus mechanics before attributing the result solely to central motor-related processing.

Loss of higher-order tactile recognition can coexist with elementary detection of contact. For example, if a participant can accurately report that a stimulus is present and locate a simple touch but cannot identify a familiar object by palpation, the task requires investigation of integrative processing rather than an automatic conclusion that every receptor class is defective. Recognition also requires memory, attention and adequate movement during exploration. To localise a defect, the experimental question must specify which basic sensory functions and motor abilities remain intact.

The sensory pathway is not simply a passive upward transmission chain. Descending and recurrent influences can modify responsiveness at multiple central levels. Attention to a particular finger can alter the processing of tactile information from that region. A change in cortical response after a change in task instructions does not necessarily imply that the mechanical stimulus or the receptor current has changed. When interpreting neural recording studies, distinguish altered peripheral input from altered processing of an equivalent input. This distinction is especially useful for questions about the roles of sensory maps, attention and behavioural relevance.

10. Comparative tactile adaptations and functional consequences

Different animals encounter different mechanical problems. For an animal moving through darkness, contact with nearby surfaces can provide information unavailable from vision. In many mammals, vibrissae are specialised hairs anchored in richly innervated follicles. Deflecting a whisker transfers force and torque to sensory endings associated with the follicle. The direction and timing of deflection can therefore report contact with an obstacle. Active whisking extends the animal's sampling volume beyond the immediate skin surface, making tactile sensing a behaviour rather than simply a passive response.

The rodent whisker system provides a useful example of somatotopy. Discrete cortical regions in the barrel cortex are related to particular whiskers, reflecting the organisation of sensory pathways. The representation is useful for studying how a spatial pattern at the body surface is transformed by neural circuits. It should not be equated with a single cortical neuron per whisker or with an assumption that no information is shared between adjacent whisker representations.

In insects, mechanosensory bristles and other cuticular sensilla provide information about contact and deformation. The external skeletal cuticle can act as a structure transmitting force to an associated sensory neuron. The broad principle resembles the mammalian hair-follicle system: a physical structure couples an environmental event to an electrically excitable cell. The structures need not be homologous, and the molecular transduction machinery should not be assumed identical simply because the functional problem is similar.

The animal's ecology determines which mechanical signals are most useful. Fine spatial sampling is advantageous for surfaces used in manipulation or detailed exploration. A sensitive vibration detector is advantageous when mechanical waves travel through a substrate. A large receptive field can therefore be biologically beneficial for detecting distant vibration even though a small receptive field is advantageous for identifying the precise location of a point. There is no universal ranking in which all large receptive fields are inferior to all small fields.

The recurring physiological design is selective transformation of deformation into patterns of electrical activity. Specialised peripheral structures filter physical inputs, channels transform deformation into current, axons deliver the encoded signal, and central circuits compare patterns. Each stage can be modified during evolution without requiring the entire sensory architecture to be replaced.

Structure–function reasoning across species

A comparison of tactile systems should begin with the physical problem faced by each organism rather than with a list of structures. A mammal using its fingertips to manipulate an object requires high local spatial resolution and rapid information about slip. A rodent moving through a narrow passage can use whisker deflection to obtain information about boundaries before the body touches them. An insect walking on a substrate can use cuticular mechanoreceptors to detect contact or vibration. All these animals require a mechanism linking environmental mechanical events to neural activity, but they sample different spatial and temporal dimensions of those events.

The hair or bristle can be viewed as a mechanical lever in a simplified model. Torque about the base is τ = rF sinθ, where r is the distance from the base to the point of force application, F is the magnitude of force and θ is the angle between the position vector and the applied force. Torque is measured in newton metres. A force perpendicular to a rigid lever at twice the distance from its base produces twice the torque. Thus contact near the tip of a long whisker can transmit a detectable mechanical effect to its follicle even if the force is small. The model does not predict a precise biological threshold because a real whisker bends, its cross-section varies and the follicle tissues have complex mechanical properties.

If the direction of deflection changes, the stress distribution around the follicle can change. Sensory endings in different positions may then respond differently, providing a basis for directional information. During active whisking, the timing of contact relative to movement can supply additional information about object location. An experiment recording from a whisker-associated neuron while moving the whisker through different angles can reveal directional preferences. To establish whether the animal uses those preferences for behaviour, one would also need to relate the neural responses to performance under controlled conditions.

A general lesson from these examples is that the same physical variable can be transformed differently by different sensory structures. Force applied to the skin, a hair shaft and a cuticular bristle may create distinct patterns of local strain. A difference in receptor response need not reflect different ion channels alone. Conversely, similarity of physiological response does not establish that receptor structures in distant species evolved from the same ancestral organ. Functional analogy and evolutionary homology are different claims requiring different evidence.

For an examination problem comparing receptor systems, examine four levels: the mechanical input, the structure transmitting it, the electrophysiological signal and the behavioural use of that signal. If two organisms detect vibration but one samples through a specialised cuticular structure and the other through a deep lamellar corpuscle, the shared function does not imply identical anatomical organisation. If a receptor with a wide field is associated with detection of substrate vibration, its poor point localisation may be an acceptable trade-off for detecting an event over a large area. Structure and neural coding should be interpreted in the context of function, not ranked by a single measure of acuity.

11. Experimental reasoning and worked interpretation

Three different experimental questions are often confused. The first is whether an identified unit detects a particular mechanical stimulus. The second is whether a molecular component is required for the transduction of that stimulus. The third is whether the unit's activity contributes to a behavioural perception. An extracellular neural recording can answer the first question, an appropriately controlled molecular perturbation can address the second, and a behavioural comparison can address the third. None alone completely establishes all three.

In a single-unit recording from a peripheral nerve, a controlled probe applies a defined indentation to the skin while the investigator records spikes. By varying location, amplitude, duration and velocity independently as far as possible, the receptive field and dynamic response of the unit can be mapped. A useful control is a trial in which the probe approaches without contacting the skin, to distinguish mechanical responses from electrical noise, movement of the recording electrode or cues produced by the apparatus. Repeated trials establish whether the firing pattern is reproducible.

Consider an illustrative experiment with four units. Unit A fires at 85 spikes/s near onset and at 26 spikes/s during the hold, and has a small field. Unit B fires at 100 spikes/s near onset and at zero during the hold, with a small field. Unit C has no sustained activity but responds strongly to a 250 Hz vibration over a broad area. Unit D maintains activity during stretch and has a broad field. The best conventional classifications are SA I, RA I, RA II and SA II respectively. These are physiological classifications; histological identification or molecular labelling would be needed for a direct claim about each terminal's exact anatomy.

Suppose a researcher compares two vibration frequencies using a probe that has the same maximum displacement at both. At 25 Hz and 250 Hz, with amplitude 10 μm, peak speeds are 2π × 25 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, approximately 0.00157 m/s, and 2π × 250 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, approximately 0.0157 m/s. Thus a tenfold change in frequency creates a tenfold change in peak speed. If the research question is whether frequency alone affects the receptor under constant peak speed, the amplitude at 250 Hz must instead be one tenth as large. Real mechanoreceptors can be sensitive to several mechanical variables, so the choice of control condition determines the meaning of the result.

For a simple behavioural experiment on spatial acuity, present one or two points in random order with controlled timing, probe force and body location. Obtain repeated reports at multiple separations rather than relying on a single trial. Define a performance criterion before comparing sites, for example a chosen percent correct under a forced-choice design. The threshold is the separation corresponding to that criterion. If two-point trials always exert twice the total force of one-point trials, the participant may discriminate the conditions by overall intensity rather than by spatial resolution. Matching non-spatial cues helps isolate the desired perceptual function.

Another experiment tests receptor current in isolated sensory cells using a controlled mechanical perturbation and voltage clamp. By holding voltage constant, the recording can reveal the ionic current evoked by deformation. In a modified cell lacking a candidate channel, loss of the mechanically evoked current combined with preserved other membrane currents supports selective loss of mechanical transduction. Comparison with unmodified cells, confirmation of cell viability and appropriate verification of the molecular alteration are necessary. Behavioural consequences still need to be measured at organism level because sensory pathways may compensate or use alternative receptor classes.

When reasoning from lesion data, compare modalities and sides. If a unilateral spinal lesion selectively interrupts the dorsal column, expect a deficit in discriminative touch and vibration on the same side below the lesion. If a lesion affects the medial lemniscus after decussation, expect the deficit mainly on the opposite side. If a peripheral nerve is interrupted, the deficit follows the territory of that nerve and may affect several modalities carried in it. These distinctions make sensory observations useful for localising the level of a defect, but an examination problem should state enough information to support one interpretation rather than assume that one sensory complaint uniquely identifies one lesion.

A structured approach to data, controls and causal conclusions

The strongest examination responses separate the observation from the conclusion. Imagine that a mutant mouse shows poorer performance on a task requiring discrimination of two textured surfaces. Several hypotheses remain possible: an impairment of mechanical transduction, altered skin mechanics, reduced axonal conduction, a central processing change, or a motor or motivational difference affecting task performance. The behaviour alone does not determine which hypothesis is correct. The design of follow-up measurements should isolate the proposed stage.

First, examine whether the animal can perform a control task that requires similar movement but does not depend on the same tactile information. Normal performance would reduce concern about a broad motor impairment or inability to engage in the task, although it would not eliminate all such possibilities. Second, record afferent responses to calibrated skin deformation. Reduced responses would localise at least part of the difference to the peripheral sensory system. Third, assess whether the axons can conduct spikes when electrically activated. If conduction is preserved but mechanically triggered responses are diminished, the transduction or mechanical coupling stages become stronger candidates.

Fourth, measure mechanically activated currents in identified cells at controlled voltage. A reduced current under comparable mechanical stimulation would provide more direct evidence for altered transduction. Fifth, use molecular verification of the targeted change and, where feasible, a rescue comparison. If restored expression also restores the cellular response, the causal interpretation is strengthened. It remains important to ensure that the manipulation has not altered cell identity or survival. The logic is not to perform every possible modern experiment but to identify which measurement addresses which alternative explanation.

A similarly careful approach applies to a test of the Pacinian capsule. The experimental prediction is that removing or altering a structure that filters static deformation will change the receptor's response to a sustained stimulus. An appropriate control examines whether the nerve ending remains viable and can still generate impulses. The mechanical stimulus reaching the ending may change after removal of the capsule, which is precisely the mechanism under investigation, but its magnitude must be considered when comparing the response. If the terminal becomes more sustained after the capsule is altered, this supports a contribution of the mechanical filter. It does not show that the ion channel has changed its intrinsic gating properties.

Single-unit recording can also be combined with psychophysical measurement. Suppose a unit responds reliably to a vibration amplitude below the amplitude a participant reports detecting. The observation shows that a neural response can occur without a reliable behavioural report under those conditions. It does not imply that the unit's spikes are irrelevant to perception. Other factors include the number of recruited afferents, noise, attention and the participant's decision criterion. Conversely, perception at a lower amplitude than the threshold of one chosen unit does not mean perception occurs without sensory neural activity; other units may be more sensitive to the stimulus.

A common teaching dataset can be designed to distinguish rate and temporal codes. At 20 Hz, an afferent fires 20 spikes/s, one at a consistent phase of each vibration cycle. At 40 Hz it still fires 20 spikes/s, but responds on every second cycle at a consistent phase. A mean firing-rate measurement cannot distinguish the two conditions. A time-resolved analysis of spike timing relative to the stimulus can reveal the difference. If the same afferent fires at random phases in another condition, the mean rate might remain unchanged even though phase information is lost. This example is hypothetical and does not claim that a real afferent follows these exact patterns.

A useful calculation relates latency and phase. A periodic stimulus at 100 Hz has a period of 10 ms. If a sensory response occurs 2 ms after each cycle begins, the phase delay relative to that reference is (2/10) × 360° = 72°. At 200 Hz the period is 5 ms, so the same 2 ms delay corresponds to 144°. This does not establish whether the observed delay is caused by mechanical transmission, transduction, conduction or all three. It demonstrates why a fixed time delay has a different phase meaning at different frequencies. A test of phase locking should compare spikes against the actual stimulus waveform.

When estimating receptor adaptation from data, avoid confusing the maintained rate with the rate at a single arbitrary time point. In the exponential example introduced earlier, the maintained component is an asymptote. A short stimulus may end before that value is approached. Suppose one unit declines from 60 spikes/s to 30 spikes/s over half a second and a second unit declines from 60 to 5 spikes/s in the same interval. The second shows a larger reduction over the measured period, but the full adaptation parameters cannot be determined from these two points alone unless a model and the maintained rates are known. More time points and controlled stimulus histories permit a stronger comparison.

Two-point discrimination can be interpreted through the difference between sensitivity and response bias. If a participant always answers that there were two contacts, they will be correct on all two-point trials but wrong on one-point trials. The proportion correct within only one category would misleadingly suggest excellent performance. A balanced design that records both types of response, including false reports, allows a fairer assessment. For exam reasoning, recognise that the criterion for a response influences measured performance independently of the underlying receptor sensitivity.

Finally, the distinction between direct evidence and inferred mechanism should guide the evaluation of every experiment. Histological labelling establishes where a protein is detected; it does not alone establish what current it carries. A mechanical-current recording establishes a functional response under the recording conditions; it does not alone establish the behavioural role of that current. A behavioural deficit establishes impaired performance in the task; it does not alone identify the responsible cell. Converging experiments at the anatomical, cellular, neural and behavioural levels provide the most informative physiological explanation.

13. Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Rapid adaptation means high conduction speed.

Adaptation concerns response persistence; conduction concerns spike propagation.

Why it is tempting: Students confuse the word rapid with propagation speed. Exam reasoning: When asked about a trace, classify the hold response first; use distance/latency only to infer velocity.

2. Pacinian corpuscles report static pressure continuously.

Their mechanical filtering and intrinsic properties favour changes and vibration.

Why it is tempting: They equate sensitivity to pressure changes with persistent pressure coding. Exam reasoning: Compare onset, hold and offset, then decide whether the capsule acts as a temporal filter.

3. A stronger stimulus makes every spike larger.

Stronger stimuli generally change firing rates and recruitment, not ordinary propagated spike amplitude.

Why it is tempting: Graded receptor potentials are mistaken for regenerative spikes. Exam reasoning: Separate receptor-potential amplitude from action-potential amplitude; count rate and recruitment instead.

4. The dorsal columns cross immediately on entering the spinal cord.

The principal DCML crossing occurs in the caudal medulla.

Why it is tempting: They apply the rule for the anterolateral system to every sensory tract. Exam reasoning: Trace first-order axons to the dorsal-column nuclei before choosing the side.

5. VPM relays touch from the entire body.

VPL is the principal body relay; VPM serves the face.

Why it is tempting: They remember a thalamic label without specifying the body region. Exam reasoning: Select VPL for trunk/limbs and VPM for facial mechanosensation.

6. A receptive field is exactly the diameter of a corpuscle.

Afferent branching and mechanical spread affect the measured field.

Why it is tempting: A receptor drawing makes its terminal appear to be a fixed sampling circle. Exam reasoning: Use the experimentally mapped skin region and stated stimulus intensity.

7. Two-point thresholds directly equal receptor spacing.

Behavioural thresholds additionally depend on overlapping fields, central processing and task design.

Why it is tempting: A simple receptor-density explanation is treated as an equality. Exam reasoning: Use spacing only as a model and include overlap, inhibition and behavioural criterion.

8. Piezo2 is an ATP-driven pump.

It is a mechanically activated ion channel permitting passive movement down electrochemical gradients.

Why it is tempting: The energy required for gating is confused with active transport. Exam reasoning: Identify passive current through a conductance, not transport against gradients powered by ATP.

9. A zero current at reversal potential proves channel closure.

Net current can be zero through open channels when driving force is zero.

Why it is tempting: Zero net current is confused with zero permeability. Exam reasoning: Use I=g(V−Erev) and distinguish conductance from driving force.

10. All touch disappears after isolated dorsal-column damage.

Some crude touch information can use additional ascending routes.

Why it is tempting: One major pathway is mistakenly treated as the sole route for every touch quality. Exam reasoning: Specify discriminative touch and vibration; check whether crude detection remains.

11. All low-threshold touch fibres are Aβ.

Other fibre classes can also contribute, particularly in hairy skin.

Why it is tempting: Aβ dominance in glabrous skin is generalised to all skin and all afferents. Exam reasoning: Retain exceptions for low-threshold afferents in hairy skin.

12. SA II always proves a histologically identified Ruffini ending.

The classical association is useful but physiological and anatomical identification are not identical.

Why it is tempting: A physiological classification is mistaken for direct anatomical observation. Exam reasoning: Separate an identified SA II response from histological proof of an ending.

13. Phase locking means exactly one spike each cycle.

Afferents can skip cycles while maintaining preferred timing.

Why it is tempting: Frequency and firing rate are assumed to be identical whenever timing is informative. Exam reasoning: Check spike phase and cycle skipping, not only the mean rate.

14. Lateral inhibition recreates information that was never sampled.

Inhibition can enhance contrast in existing information, not uniquely recover identical input patterns.

Why it is tempting: Contrast enhancement is assumed to generate missing spatial samples. Exam reasoning: Identify what information was present in the inputs before inhibition.

15. Cortical area is proportional to skin surface area.

Cortical allocation varies with sensory representation and functional demands.

Why it is tempting: The homunculus is read as a drawing to physical scale. Exam reasoning: Interpret cortical magnification in terms of representation and processing demands.
