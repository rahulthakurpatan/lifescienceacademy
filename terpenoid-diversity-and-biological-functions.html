1. The biological problem of chemical diversity

Rub a citrus peel gently and a characteristic scent appears; look at a ripe tomato and a red pigment is visible; inspect the resin exuding from an injured conifer and a sticky, water-resistant material coats the wound. These observations concern different organs, different timescales and different physical properties, yet the molecules responsible for important parts of all three phenomena are related through isoprenoid metabolism. Plant cells must not only build the molecules needed for division and energy capture but also solve problems involving interactions with light, microorganisms, insects and changing physical conditions. The diversity of terpenoids illustrates how a relatively small set of biochemical precursors can be transformed into compounds with very different biological roles.

A terpene, in the narrow chemical sense, is a hydrocarbon constructed from isoprenoid building units. Terpenoids include oxygenated, rearranged or otherwise modified derivatives of such carbon skeletons; the literature also uses the two terms in a broader overlapping sense. Isoprenoid is a useful umbrella term because it connects the specialized molecules described in this chapter with sterols, photosynthetic pigments, prenyl side chains of electron carriers and several plant hormones. Not all of these compounds are dispensable products. Sterols and photosynthetic carotenoids contribute to fundamental cellular functions, whereas many species-specific scent compounds are useful only in particular ecological contexts. The term specialized metabolism therefore describes a pattern of biosynthetic and functional specialization rather than a strict boundary between necessary and unnecessary chemistry.

The five-carbon building unit is represented conceptually by isoprene, CH2=C(CH3)–CH=CH2. This notation is a carbon-accounting device, not a statement that free isoprene is routinely joined together to build all terpenes. The actual activated substrates are isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and its allylic isomer dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP). The diphosphate group enables controlled enzymatic reactions in aqueous cells. Counting the five-carbon contributions explains why many familiar terpenes occur as C10, C15 or C20 molecules. It does not predict the precise location of every double bond, methyl group or ring after rearrangement. To make that prediction we must consider the reaction catalyzed by the relevant enzyme.

Chemists first recognized structural relationships among compounds isolated from essential oils and resins. The empirical isoprene rule organized otherwise bewildering structural information, and the modern biochemical explanation emerged with the identification of activated isoprenoid intermediates and the discovery of alternative routes supplying them. Structural classification remains valuable, but isotope tracing and characterization of enzymes are required to establish the actual biosynthetic history of a compound. A C15 hydrocarbon could have several different arrangements of its carbon atoms and several possible stereochemical configurations. A chemical formula therefore identifies a set of possible structures rather than a single natural product.

A useful first distinction is between the chemical properties of a compound and the biological consequences of those properties. A neutral hydrocarbon has few opportunities for hydrogen bonding with water, so it tends to partition into nonpolar environments. A long hydrocarbon chain embedded in a membrane, a volatile hydrocarbon stored in an oil cavity and a polymer accumulated in a latex particle all exploit this tendency in different ways. Hydrophobicity does not guarantee volatility, however. A very large nonpolar molecule can have an extremely low vapor pressure because many weak intermolecular interactions must be disrupted before it enters the gas phase. Conversely, a small molecule bearing a hydroxyl group can still be readily detected in plant headspace if its concentration and vapor pressure are sufficient. These distinctions explain why the carbon-count classification is helpful but not enough to explain function.

The same distinction applies to biological activity. A compound may participate in a chemical defense because it interferes with a pathogen's membrane, yet another molecule with almost the same carbon skeleton may be ineffective because a stereochemical change alters its interaction with a binding site. Enantiomers have the same molecular formula and most bulk physical properties in an achiral environment but can interact differently with proteins. A receptor or enzyme is itself a chiral structure. Two mirror-image forms of a terpene therefore need not evoke identical responses in an insect. This is why descriptions such as scent, toxicity or antimicrobial activity should be attached to the actual compound and context rather than transferred without evidence to its entire chemical family.

The notion of a biosynthetic family also helps in interpreting mutant phenotypes. If a mutation prevents a plant from constructing a whole group of essential molecules, the mutant may be weak, developmentally abnormal or unable to survive. If a different mutation prevents formation of a single scent compound, the plant may grow normally in a controlled environment but perform differently in the presence of a particular pollinator or herbivore. Neither outcome is surprising. The term essential must be qualified by environment, developmental stage and the level of organization being considered. A trait can be dispensable for an isolated seedling in the laboratory but strongly affect reproductive success in a natural habitat.

A plant does not benefit merely by producing a greater number of compounds. Each compound requires some portion of the photosynthetically acquired carbon, enzyme synthesis, cofactors and cellular space. Selection can favor particular combinations of compounds when those combinations improve survival or reproduction relative to their costs. Species-specific differences in terpenoid composition therefore reflect both biochemical opportunities and historical selection. Similar ecological pressures may favor similar properties in distantly related lineages, without implying that the exact biosynthetic enzymes evolved independently in the same way. Conversely, closely related plants may use very different terpene blends if the selective conditions or available genes differ. The molecular pathways described below provide a mechanistic basis for this evolutionary interpretation.

A structural classification becomes more meaningful when the learner considers how natural products were originally distinguished. Isolation of an oil, followed by physical and chemical tests on its components, made it possible to recognize that many products had related hydrocarbon frameworks. The recognition of structural regularity preceded direct identification of the enzymes that generated it. Modern biochemical evidence explains why the regularity exists, and also why departures occur. The isoprene rule is useful in interpreting a molecule's carbon skeleton, but actual precursor incorporation provides stronger evidence about biosynthetic origin. A molecule that appears to contain repeated five-carbon patterns might have undergone extensive rearrangement, and a molecule lacking obvious regular repetition may nevertheless arise entirely from activated isoprenoid precursors.

To understand the relationship between terpenes and other metabolic classes, compare a fatty acid with a prenyl chain. Both contain hydrocarbon segments and can be hydrophobic, but fatty-acid biosynthesis generally extends a chain with two-carbon contributions from malonyl-derived intermediates. Ordinary isoprenoid chain assembly uses five-carbon units and produces characteristic methyl branching. Chemical behavior alone therefore cannot identify the biosynthetic pathway. Structural features, precursor-labeling evidence and enzyme function must be considered together. This distinction will be especially important when examining metabolites such as phenolics carrying a prenyl side chain, because a molecule can combine products of otherwise distinct metabolic routes.

2. Prenyl precursors and carbon accounting

The preceding node establishes the mevalonate pathway, located chiefly outside plastids, and the methylerythritol phosphate pathway (MEP), operating in plastids. Both produce IPP and DMAPP. The distinction matters because different cellular compartments contain different pools of substrates, enzymes and products. A chloroplast synthesizing carotenoids must supply the long-chain precursor within the appropriate plastid environment; a cytosolic sesquiterpene synthase will not automatically have access to every IPP molecule synthesized elsewhere. Exchange of isoprenoid intermediates can occur, but its extent is not constant among organs, species and experimental conditions.

In a common head-to-tail prenyltransferase reaction, DMAPP supplies an allylic starter while IPP acts as the extending five-carbon unit. One IPP addition produces geranyl diphosphate (GPP), containing ten carbons. A second addition gives farnesyl diphosphate (FPP), containing fifteen, and a third gives geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP), containing twenty. These descriptions represent net carbon accounting rather than an obligation for a single enzyme to perform all three steps. Distinct prenyltransferases differ in product-length specificity. The physical dimensions and chemical environment of an active site help determine how long a product can become before being released.

Longer products require different modes of assembly. Squalene synthase joins two FPP-derived units to produce the C30 hydrocarbon squalene, with NADPH participating in the reductive reaction. Phytoene synthase couples two GGPP units to produce the C40 carotenoid precursor phytoene. Unlike the ordinary chain-extension reactions, these are commonly described as head-to-head coupling reactions. Thus the classification of triterpenoids and tetraterpenoids reflects two different branch-point condensation reactions, not merely a continuation of the linear C5 addition sequence. For natural rubber a different kind of prenyltransferase repeatedly extends an allylic starter with IPP, making very long chains rather than a single defined small molecule.

For a theoretical carbon balance, suppose twelve micromoles of C5 equivalents are available and all are converted to one class without losses or competing reactions. Six micromoles of GPP, four of FPP or three of GGPP could be produced if the correct proportions of IPP and DMAPP were available. The twelve micromoles of units would contain sixty micromoles of carbon atoms regardless of which product is made. If four micromoles of FPP were instead diverted completely to squalene, the maximum yield would be two micromoles of squalene. These are stoichiometric ceilings, not predictions of reaction rates in living tissue. Real flux depends on enzyme kinetics, precursor transport, other demands and metabolic regulation.

A useful examination distinction is the carbon number of an original scaffold versus the total number of atoms in a derivative. A C30 sapogenin bearing two hexose residues contains more than thirty carbon atoms; its triterpenoid identity refers to the aglycone. Conversely a hormone or scent molecule made by oxidative cleavage of a longer isoprenoid may contain fewer carbon atoms than its precursor. The cleavage product retains an isoprenoid biosynthetic ancestry without necessarily matching the simple multiple-of-five rule.

The terms allylic and homoallylic describe the relationship between the reacting group and a double bond. In DMAPP, the diphosphate is attached to a carbon adjacent to a carbon-carbon double bond; ionization can generate an intermediate whose positive charge is stabilized by electron delocalization. IPP has its double bond in a different position. This arrangement allows the double bond of IPP to participate in carbon-carbon bond formation with an activated allylic substrate. The newly formed product retains an allylic diphosphate group that may support another elongation reaction. The isomerization between IPP and DMAPP balances the need for a suitable starter and extender. A supply of one isomer without the other is insufficient for ordinary head-to-tail chain assembly.

For an ideal GPP-synthesis reaction, one DMAPP molecule contributes five carbons and one IPP molecule contributes the remaining five. FPP formation from the same initial starter requires two IPP molecules. Formation of GGPP requires three. A question may therefore give independent quantities of the two isomers rather than their combined amount. If a hypothetical preparation contains 7 micromoles of DMAPP and 10 micromoles of IPP, with no isomerization and no other substrate supply, FPP formation is limited by IPP. The largest possible amount is 5 micromoles of FPP, consuming 5 micromoles of DMAPP and all 10 micromoles of IPP. Two micromoles of DMAPP remain. Merely dividing the total of 17 micromoles by three would give 5.67 micromoles, a yield that cannot be reached under the stated conditions. This is a limiting-reactant problem with an explicitly biological substrate requirement.

The cellular availability of a prenyl diphosphate depends on its synthesis, consumption and movement. These molecules carry charged phosphate groups and cannot simply equilibrate across lipid bilayers as neutral hydrocarbons might. Compartmentalization is not an absolute rule that makes exchange impossible, but it imposes a physical and biochemical constraint. Plant cells also contain prenyltransferases serving essential pathways, such as production of the isoprenoid side chains of quinones. Therefore a measured pool of IPP or FPP cannot be assigned exclusively to specialized metabolism. A branching diagram is a map of possible carbon routes, not a promise that all pathways operate with equal rates in every cell.

Two classical inhibitors illustrate an approach to testing compartmental contributions. In an appropriate plant experiment, inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase restricts the MVA route, whereas fosmidomycin inhibits the MEP-pathway enzyme DXR. If the concentration of a plastidial carotenoid declines after inhibition of the MEP route, the result is consistent with a dependence on MEP-derived precursors. If a cytosolic sesquiterpene is also affected, several explanations are possible: intercompartment exchange, indirect changes in physiology, toxicity or longer-term regulatory responses. Comparing multiple metabolites, inhibitor doses, developmental times and genetic perturbations strengthens the inference. Inhibitor specificity in a purified enzyme assay does not by itself establish that all effects on an intact plant result solely from the target pathway.

The demand for essential metabolites can influence the consequences of increasing precursor supply. Consider a line that produces more cytosolic FPP because its upstream pathway is stimulated. A specialized sesquiterpene may increase if its synthase has unused catalytic capacity, but sterol biosynthesis may also consume some additional carbon. If the specialized synthase is already saturated, a large change in precursor concentration may produce little change in its flux. Increasing a downstream enzyme instead could lower the precursor pool and change allocation among competing pathways. A single measurement of precursor concentration cannot tell which of these alternatives is operating. At a simplified steady state, the rate of precursor input equals the sum of its consumption and any net export or loss. If that equality is disturbed, the amount in the pool changes until the system reaches a new state or continues to change.

The carbon skeleton and the electron or cofactor requirements of a reaction should be accounted for separately. Squalene synthase consumes two FPP molecules to establish a C30 backbone, but its reductive catalytic step also requires reducing power. Phytoene synthase produces a C40 compound by coupling two GGPP molecules; later desaturation changes the number of hydrogen atoms and the electronic properties without increasing the carbon count. An oxidation may increase the oxygen content while preserving the scaffold. A glycosylation may add carbons from a carbohydrate donor rather than from IPP. Thus the number of carbons in a product is a useful constraint on pathway interpretation, but it cannot establish which cofactors or modification reactions were involved.

3. Terpene synthases, cyclization and tailoring

The simplest way to generate different products from a common precursor is to change the reaction that the precursor undergoes. Terpene synthases (TPSs) can convert prenyl diphosphates into linear or cyclic carbon skeletons. The enzymatic release of diphosphate from an allylic substrate produces a highly reactive carbocation-like intermediate in many class I reactions. Divalent metal ions coordinate the diphosphate group and help its departure. In a free solution this intermediate might undergo many competing reactions, but the enzyme active site positions the substrate and restricts the paths available to it. Consequently the same chain can be folded differently by different enzymes, or by variants of one enzyme.

A carbon-carbon double bond can attack a positively charged carbon within the same molecule and generate a ring. The newly formed carbocation can undergo additional ring closure or shifts of a hydride or methyl group. These rearrangements conserve the number of carbons while changing their connectivity. Removal of a proton can terminate the reaction by creating a double bond; capture by water can generate an alcohol. Some terpene synthases produce one dominant compound, whereas others allow several alternative termination paths and release a characteristic mixture. Thus the number of detected products is not a direct measure of the number of synthase genes.

Class II diterpene cyclases use a distinct initiating mechanism, commonly protonation of a carbon-carbon double bond. In gibberellin biosynthesis the conversion of GGPP into ent-copalyl diphosphate illustrates a cyclization that retains the diphosphate group. A subsequent class I reaction converts this intermediate into ent-kaurene. Some plant diterpenoid pathways separate these two activities into different proteins, and others may involve bifunctional enzymes. It is therefore incorrect to treat release of diphosphate as the universal initial event for every terpene cyclization.

Following skeleton formation, cytochrome P450 monooxygenases can introduce oxygen-containing groups and perform additional oxidative transformations. Hydroxylation provides a chemical handle for glycosylation, esterification and further oxidation. UDP-dependent glycosyltransferases transfer sugars from activated donors, frequently increasing hydrophilicity and changing transport or storage behavior. These modifications do not necessarily increase biological activity; sometimes they mask activity until the aglycone is released. The sequence of scaffold construction and tailoring helps explain how changes in a small subset of biosynthetic genes can generate striking differences among related plant species.

An experimental test of TPS function requires more than detecting a gene transcript. A candidate enzyme can be compared with an appropriate negative control in a biochemical or heterologous expression assay using a defined substrate, followed by product identification. A product observed in such an assay establishes catalytic potential under those conditions, but does not alone establish the dominant in vivo substrate. Localization, substrate availability and genetic evidence must also agree. This distinction is central to examination problems in which one enzyme can accept GPP and FPP in vitro but normally encounters only one of them in its native compartment.

A terpene synthase can be understood as an environment that promotes selected reactions of a substrate while suppressing other chemically plausible reactions. The diphosphate group of a prenyl precursor is strongly polar, while the hydrocarbon portion is hydrophobic. In many class I synthases, conserved acidic residues help bind metal ions that coordinate the diphosphate. Formation of the reactive intermediate and subsequent rearrangements occur within a comparatively protected cavity. The enzyme may constrain water access until a particular stage, favoring either hydrocarbon formation or incorporation of water into an alcohol. The exact outcome depends on the orientation and conformational freedom of intermediates. Describing the enzyme merely as a catalyst that removes phosphate misses its central role in determining the product structure.

An important aspect of cyclization is that the substrate's configuration affects which atoms can approach each other. The initially extended geometry of GPP does not directly favor every possible ring closure. In many monoterpene cyclizations, the reaction proceeds through an isomerization involving a linalyl intermediate that permits rotation and adopts a geometry suitable for ring formation. A subsequent cyclization can generate an alpha-terpinyl carbocation-related intermediate; different termination or rearrangement paths give distinct monocyclic or bicyclic products. A student need not memorize a separate detailed carbocation mechanism for every essential-oil constituent, but should understand why isomerization and enzyme-controlled folding can be necessary before cyclization.

Cyclization alone does not change the overall number of carbon atoms. In formal unsaturation accounting, making a ring consumes one possible degree of unsaturation in the same way that introducing a double bond does. The formula C10H16 has three degrees of unsaturation, calculated as (2C + 2 − H)/2 for a hydrocarbon. An acyclic monoterpene with three double bonds, a monocyclic compound with two double bonds or a bicyclic compound with one double bond can share that formula. For limonene, one ring and two double bonds account for three degrees of unsaturation. This calculation is a check on candidate structures, not a method for distinguishing positional or stereochemical isomers. If a compound contains oxygen but no nitrogen or halogens, the simple degree-of-unsaturation formula remains unchanged by the oxygen count.

Some plant TPS proteins make several products in a reproducible ratio because alternative pathways of intermediate rearrangement and termination compete within the active site. A mutation in an amino acid lining that cavity may change the distribution of products even if the total amount of substrate consumed changes little. Such a mutant can alter the odor blend without reducing the overall rate of terpenoid synthesis by the same proportion. A product ratio is therefore often more informative than a single measure of total terpene concentration. Similarly, two plants that both produce ten micromoles of monoterpenes per unit time can expose insects to very different chemical stimuli if the proportions of individual constituents differ.

Gene duplication offers a route by which new enzyme activities can evolve. A duplicated synthase gene can retain some original activity while changes in the duplicate alter product specificity, subcellular localization or expression. If the new product benefits the plant in its environment, selection may retain that activity. Duplication does not always generate innovation; one copy can be lost or become nonfunctional. In addition, changes in regulatory DNA can produce a new ecological phenotype without altering the catalytic specificity of the encoded enzyme. A synthase that is already capable of producing a volatile may acquire a new role if its expression changes from leaves to flowers. Sequence analysis, enzyme assays and expression data together help distinguish these evolutionary mechanisms.

The tailoring enzymes often contribute as much to biological variation as the scaffold-forming enzymes. An oxidation can generate a hydroxylated intermediate whose location of substitution determines which subsequent enzyme will accept it. A glycosyltransferase may recognize a particular stereochemical arrangement and attach sugars at one or more positions. Glycosylation can influence sequestration into aqueous cellular compartments, while subsequent hydrolysis can release the less polar aglycone. If a leaf stores an odorless glycoside and releases a volatile after damage, an analysis of the intact leaf's emitted compounds underestimates its potential for volatile release. Analysis of the glycoside pool and the relevant hydrolytic activity is needed to explain the response.

The expression of a TPS gene and the identity of its product must be established with different kinds of evidence. A transcript detected specifically in flower petals indicates that transcription occurs in those tissues. A protein assay with GPP establishes that the encoded protein can form a monoterpene from that substrate. A mutant whose floral headspace lacks the corresponding compound suggests that the enzyme contributes to emission in vivo. Restoration of the product by complementation adds evidence that the mutation is causal rather than merely associated with another genomic change. None of these experiments alone answers every question. Taken together they connect a candidate gene, a chemical reaction, an organ-specific metabolite and a potential biological function.

The distinction between a prenyltransferase and a terpene synthase is worth emphasizing through an example involving GPP. Geranyl diphosphate synthase forms the C10 activated intermediate by combining appropriate C5 substrates. Limonene synthase uses that already assembled intermediate and rearranges it into a cyclic hydrocarbon. Menthol biosynthesis subsequently requires reactions acting on a limonene-derived scaffold. These three stages solve three different chemical problems: assembling the carbon skeleton, determining its topology, and altering its functional groups. A mutation in the first stage can affect several GPP-dependent products; a mutation in limonene synthase can have a narrower consequence; a mutation in a downstream menthol pathway enzyme can leave limonene production intact. This hierarchy provides a powerful organizing principle for interpreting biochemical mutant data.

In a simplified labeling exercise, suppose a hydrocarbon formed by a cyclase incorporates all ten carbon atoms from labeled GPP. If its mass spectrum indicates a C10 product, this is consistent with scaffold conservation. If the product instead has only nine carbons, an additional carbon-loss reaction or an alternative substrate must be considered. Oxygen incorporation into an alcohol can occur during synthase-catalyzed termination with water, or through a separate oxygenating enzyme in a later step. Detecting oxygen in a final product does not identify the mechanism of its introduction. A biochemical test of the purified enzyme, including appropriate controls, can establish whether it is directly capable of producing the alcohol. This distinction explains why pathway maps sometimes require revision when more precise catalytic evidence becomes available.

4. Hemiterpenes, monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes

Isoprene itself is a C5 hemiterpene emitted by many, but not all, plant species. Isoprene synthase uses DMAPP and releases the volatile hydrocarbon. Emission may increase at higher leaf temperature or strong light within a physiological range, although severe stress can suppress metabolism and alter the response. Proposed and experimentally supported protective effects involve heat and oxidative stress tolerance in some species. A plant that emits no appreciable isoprene may nevertheless cope well with stress by other mechanisms; isoprene emission is not a universal requirement for heat tolerance.

GPP supplies many C10 monoterpenes, including limonene, pinene and the oxygen-containing derivative menthol. Their molecular mass and relatively weak interactions with water favor partitioning into hydrophobic phases and, for many compounds, emission into air. Limonene is a hydrocarbon with a cyclic skeleton; menthol is an oxygenated cyclic monoterpenoid whose hydroxyl group affects intermolecular interactions. They are not produced by identical reactions simply because their carbon numbers match. In mint, limonene-derived intermediates undergo a sequence of oxygenation and reduction reactions leading to menthol. This is an example of how a scaffold becomes a distinct biological and sensory molecule through enzyme-mediated tailoring.

Flowers may emit monoterpenes as components of complex scent blends that contribute to pollinator attraction. Leaves may produce similar compounds in secretory structures, where they contribute to defense or are released when tissue is damaged. Glandular trichomes are specialized epidermal structures that can synthesize and accumulate metabolites in extracellular or intracellular compartments. Storage limits uncontrolled contact with membranes in the rest of the plant and can concentrate a product sufficiently for ecological effectiveness. A compound measured in a solvent extract of trichomes is not necessarily emitted at the same rate by intact tissue, because physical barriers and environmental variables influence release.

FPP supplies sesquiterpenes such as farnesene and caryophyllene. Sesquiterpenes can also be volatile, even though the C15 molecules generally have lower vapor pressures than comparable C10 compounds. Functional groups and molecular geometry matter in addition to molecular mass. The release of (E)-beta-caryophyllene from damaged maize roots can contribute to attraction of entomopathogenic nematodes under suitable conditions. This illustrates an indirect defense: an emitted compound affects a third organism that can harm the herbivore. The same volatile can be interpreted differently by different organisms and therefore cannot be assigned one fixed ecological meaning.

Many herbivore-induced volatile blends change in both quantity and composition. Mechanical wounding may release pre-existing volatiles immediately, whereas de novo synthesis regulated by wound and herbivore-associated signaling can contribute to emissions later. Sampling a plant at only one time point cannot distinguish those possibilities. An experimental comparison should include intact controls, mechanically wounded plants, herbivore-treated plants and where possible a genetic line deficient in the relevant synthase. Measurement of volatile emission and a separate behavioral test address different links in the causal chain. A higher emission rate by itself does not demonstrate that a predator is attracted or that herbivore damage decreases.

The fact that some terpene-rich plants are recognizable by odor illustrates the relationship between synthesis, storage and release. A volatile molecule has a measurable equilibrium vapor pressure, but emission from a living tissue is a nonequilibrium process. The rate depends on internal concentration, temperature, diffusion distance, barriers and the rate at which the external air removes molecules. A small volatile may be synthesized continuously and released without a large persistent storage pool. Another volatile may accumulate in a secretory cavity and be released mainly when the cavity is ruptured. An emitted amount and a stored amount thus reflect different aspects of plant physiology. A student interpreting experiments should ask whether the reported values refer to emission per time, concentration in tissue, or total quantity per organ.

The structure of a glandular trichome can explain why physical damage produces a sudden strong odor. Secretory cells manufacture compounds that enter a specialized storage region, sometimes beneath a cuticular covering. Accumulation separates much of the hydrophobic secretion from ordinary cytoplasm and makes it available at the epidermal interface. Rupture exposes the secretion to air, allowing more volatile components to evaporate. In a wounded leaf, the immediate peak can reflect release of compounds synthesized long before damage occurred. Later emission can reflect newly induced synthesis. Measuring both trichome contents and headspace across several time points therefore gives stronger evidence than observing odor alone.

The two enantiomers of limonene offer a convenient example of stereochemical specificity. Both possess the same carbon and hydrogen count and the same basic connectivity. In an achiral environment they have very similar physical properties, but a biological receptor can discriminate the spatial arrangement of their atoms. Chiral chromatography may be required to distinguish them reliably when ordinary chromatographic conditions and mass spectra are insufficient. A plant species' characteristic volatile signature can include stereochemical information in addition to compound identity and amount. It is incorrect to assume that an enzyme producing a specific stereoisomer can be replaced without consequence by an enzyme producing its mirror image.

A volatile involved in pollination may act as a component of a blend rather than as an isolated universal attractant. The intensity of emission, proportions of components and timing may all affect the receiver's response. A flower that emits a blend primarily when its effective pollinator is active avoids some costs of unnecessary emission. However, floral odors also may be detected by herbivores and other organisms. The function of a scent therefore reflects both the benefits of attracting desirable visitors and the possible costs of attracting undesirable ones. Experiments comparing pollinator visitation should control for flower number, age, color, nectar availability and other traits that might change simultaneously with scent production.

For herbivore-induced signals, it is useful to distinguish direct and indirect defense. A stored metabolite that reduces feeding or growth of an herbivore directly affects the organism attacking the plant. An emitted blend that changes the orientation of a parasitoid or predator can influence herbivory indirectly. The two modes can occur together. To show direct effects, scientists need a relevant assay of feeding, growth or survival under realistic exposure conditions. To demonstrate an indirect defensive outcome, they need evidence not only that a natural enemy responds to the signal but that the response changes herbivore pressure and, ideally, plant performance. A behavior change in a laboratory choice chamber is an important intermediate result rather than conclusive evidence of improved fitness in every field environment.

An illustrative dataset demonstrates why blend composition matters. Assume that intact flowers emit 8 nanomoles of compound A and 2 nanomoles of compound B per hour. A genetically altered line emits 2 nanomoles of A and 8 nanomoles of B per hour. Both release 10 nanomoles per hour in total. The A:B ratio changes from four to one-quarter, a sixteenfold difference in the ratio despite unchanged total emission. If an insect responds primarily to the proportion of A, the altered plant may have a different ecological phenotype. A controlled behavioral experiment would be needed to determine whether the receiver responds to the ratio, the absolute dose of A or some interaction among the components.

Compounds formed by oxidative cleavage of larger terpenoids are another reason not to identify volatile metabolism only with C10 and C15 synthase products. Homoterpenes such as DMNT, a C11 compound, and TMTT, a C16 compound, are examples of volatiles generated from larger prenyl-derived intermediates. Their noncanonical carbon numbers are compatible with isoprenoid ancestry because carbon atoms are removed during oxidative transformation. A carbon-count examination question should specify whether it asks for a classical intact terpene scaffold or for the biosynthetic class of a modified product. Recognizing that distinction avoids misclassifying a cleavage product as unrelated to terpenoid metabolism.

Mint oil is a useful model for reasoning about tissue-specific metabolic profiles. The developing leaf contains glandular structures whose differentiation and metabolic activity change with age. Consequently the proportions of intermediate and final monoterpenoids may vary during development. The amount of a mature product accumulated at one stage integrates earlier synthesis and later turnover rather than representing only the activity measured on that day. In an examination problem comparing young and mature leaves, a high concentration of limonene in young tissue and a greater proportion of oxygenated derivatives in older tissue would be consistent with sequential pathway conversion, but would not by itself prove that the same individual molecules persisted and were converted. A labeling experiment could more directly trace the fate of the earlier precursor.

Ecological signals also have an important spatial component. A molecule released from a leaf is transported through moving air, whereas a molecule produced by a root enters a chemically and physically heterogeneous soil environment. The distance at which a receiver encounters the signal depends on release rate, diffusion, airflow or water movement, adsorption and degradation. The concentration experienced by a receiver can differ considerably from the amount collected at the source. This distinction explains why the same compound may evoke different responses when tested in a laboratory vessel or in soil. Appropriate experiments control stimulus concentration and use an environment relevant to the ecological hypothesis, without confusing detectability with proof of a particular adaptive function.

5. Diterpenes, resins and growth-related isoprenoids

GGPP is the common precursor of C20 diterpenoids. Many plant diterpenes are less volatile than monoterpenes and remain associated with tissue surfaces or internal stores. Conifer oleoresin provides a useful integrated example. Resin is a mixture rather than a single molecular class: volatile mono- and sesquiterpenes contribute to its fluid component, while diterpenoid resin acids contribute to the less volatile material. When resin reaches an injury, it may impede invading organisms mechanically and chemically. Loss of more volatile components can leave a hardened residue. A description of resin as simply a diterpene therefore omits its mixed composition and physical behavior.

Diterpene cyclization can generate labdane-related, kaurane-related and other skeletons. Structural changes often influence hydrophobicity, membrane partitioning and biological interactions. Oxidation of methyl groups to carboxyl groups in resin-acid biosynthesis changes polarity and acidity without necessarily changing the underlying carbon count. Biosynthesis of a hydrocarbon scaffold and conversion to an acid require different catalytic activities, so a block in the oxidative branch may cause accumulation of less oxidized intermediates even when the initial cyclase is functional.

An important bridge to the plant-hormone subunit is gibberellin biosynthesis. Ent-kaurene is formed from GGPP through ent-copalyl diphosphate; later oxidation, ring modification and carbon loss generate gibberellins of different activities and carbon numbers. The mere detection of a diterpene does not establish gibberellin activity. Hormone function depends on the particular molecular structure and its interactions with signaling components. GGPP also supplies the precursor of the phytol side chain associated with chlorophyll. These products demonstrate that the plastidial isoprenoid pool supports both specialized functions and core photosynthetic or developmental requirements.

Sesterterpenes are C25 isoprenoids, corresponding formally to five C5 units. Some plant species possess enzymes that form C25 prenyl diphosphate precursors and cyclize them. Their distribution and exam prominence are more limited than those of monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, diterpenes and triterpenes. A student should know the carbon-count category without assuming that all plants synthesize abundant members of it.

The example of conifer oleoresin connects chemical diversity with a physical plant defense. The more mobile fraction of the secretion can flow toward a wound and carry less volatile components. As volatile material is lost, the remaining fraction can become more viscous or solidify. Resin acids contain a hydrophobic carbon skeleton and an ionizable carboxyl group. Their distribution among phases depends in part on protonation state and the surrounding mixture. A single resin-acid structure cannot explain the viscosity of every oleoresin because the properties of the mixture depend on composition, temperature and the rate of evaporation.

Resin production also illustrates two possible timescales of defense. Pre-existing resin in a duct may be released quickly upon injury. Induced synthesis and formation of additional secretory capacity can contribute on a slower timescale. A comparison of resin collected immediately after injury with material collected several days later should therefore not assume that both collections reflect the same biosynthetic state. The first can be dominated by previously accumulated material; the second can reflect transcriptional changes, continued synthesis, changes in the injured tissue and selective loss of volatile components. Changes in composition must be interpreted in light of these processes.

In a two-step diterpene cyclization pathway, mutations at different positions give different biochemical expectations. Loss of the class II cyclase may eliminate the particular copalyl diphosphate intermediate and all products dependent on it. Loss of the subsequent class I cyclase may allow the upstream cyclized precursor to accumulate or enter competing reactions. Detecting that precursor can therefore help locate the biochemical block, although rapid degradation or feedback can prevent visible accumulation. A rescue test using a final active gibberellin may restore elongation in a biosynthetic mutant but cannot establish the exact missing enzymatic step by itself. Metabolite profiles and genetic evidence are needed to refine the diagnosis.

There are specialized diterpenoids whose roles differ from those of gibberellins. Rice, for instance, produces diterpenoid phytoalexins in response to certain microbial challenges. A phytoalexin is an antimicrobial compound whose production or accumulation is induced by a biotic challenge; the term does not identify one chemical family. A phytoanticipin, by contrast, is present before attack or can be released from pre-existing precursors. These concepts are functional and temporal. A C20 backbone does not prove either activity, and a pathogen-induced increase in concentration is not enough by itself to establish antimicrobial efficacy. A meaningful experiment connects metabolite accumulation with inhibition of the relevant pathogen or with altered disease susceptibility in plants that differ in the pathway.

The comparison between diterpenes and sesquiterpenes also shows why a gene's localization cannot be inferred solely from the carbon count of its product. Plastidial GGPP supply commonly supports diterpene formation, whereas cytosolic FPP commonly supports sesquiterpene formation, but alternative enzyme localizations and exchange of precursors can modify this pattern. An experimentally retargeted enzyme may yield a different product profile if the new compartment provides a different set of substrates. To establish the normal biological role of the protein, data from artificial expression must be compared with the native tissue and developmental context.

An additional link between diterpene metabolism and essential cellular structures is the production of the phytyl group in chlorophyll and related compounds. The hydrophobic side chain helps anchor chlorophyll in membrane-associated pigment-protein assemblies, in contrast to the light-absorbing tetrapyrrole portion of the molecule. A compound can thus contain an isoprenoid-derived segment without its complete structure being classified as a simple diterpene. Tocopherols similarly combine a prenyl-derived hydrophobic side chain with an aromatic head group of different biosynthetic origin. Their chemistry connects hydrophobic membrane localization with antioxidant behavior. These examples reinforce the distinction between classifying a whole molecule and tracing the origin of a particular structural moiety.

Consider a plastid mutant with impaired GGPP production. Changes in carotenoids, chlorophyll-associated prenyl chains and diterpene-derived signals may occur together because they share precursor requirements. If chlorophyll abundance decreases, the phenotype cannot automatically be attributed to lack of carotenoids alone. Conversely, a mutation confined to a carotenoid-specific enzyme may alter pigment composition while the initial production of GGPP remains functional. The position of the biochemical lesion and the set of dependent pathways are necessary for predicting the range of effects. Experimental rescue with an externally supplied growth hormone could improve elongation while leaving photosynthetic defects unchanged, illustrating that shared precursors can connect otherwise distinct biological functions.

6. Triterpenes, phytosterols and saponins

The squalene branch links FPP to C30 triterpenes and sterols. Squalene is a flexible, acyclic hydrocarbon. Squalene epoxidase oxidizes one terminal double bond to form 2,3-oxidosqualene, the activated substrate for many oxidosqualene cyclases in plants. Protonation and opening of the epoxide can initiate a cascade of ring-forming reactions. Distinct cyclases direct the flexible precursor into different folding patterns and stabilize different reaction intermediates. Thus cycloartenol, beta-amyrin and lupeol can arise from the same C30 precursor while differing in ring arrangement and biological destinations.

Cycloartenol is the principal early cyclic intermediate of plant sterol biosynthesis in many species. Later demethylation, methylation, reduction and double-bond rearrangement reactions generate common phytosterols such as campesterol, beta-sitosterol and stigmasterol. These sterols generally contain 28 or 29 carbons, not thirty; their ancestry from a triterpene explains why they belong in the isoprenoid discussion. A sterol's small hydroxyl group can associate with the polar interfacial region of a lipid bilayer while its rigid rings and side chain insert among acyl chains. This arrangement affects membrane order, permeability and the properties of membrane-associated proteins. Sterols do not simply increase or decrease membrane fluidity under every condition: effects depend on temperature and lipid composition.

Campesterol also serves as a major precursor of brassinosteroids. A block early in sterol biosynthesis can therefore affect both structural membrane functions and hormone production. Giving a downstream hormone might rescue a growth response but would not necessarily correct a deficit in membrane sterols. This distinction illustrates the use of rescue experiments to determine whether a mutant phenotype has one or several biochemical causes.

A saponin consists of a hydrophobic sapogenin linked to one or more hydrophilic sugar chains. The sapogenin can be derived from a triterpenoid or steroidal scaffold. The resulting amphipathic character can generate surface-active behavior, explaining the association of certain saponin-rich extracts with foaming. A saponin's biological effects depend on molecular details, concentration and the composition of the target membrane. Some interact with sterols and can disturb membranes; others have different or weaker activities. It is not scientifically sound to infer that every saponin is equally toxic to all organisms or that every foaming extract contains the same saponin.

Oat roots provide a classical example of specialized antimicrobial triterpene glycosides known as avenacins. Genetic defects that interfere with their production can alter susceptibility to certain fungal pathogens. Such a phenotype is more persuasive when independent mutant lines and complementation agree, and when metabolic analysis confirms the missing compounds. An early cyclase mutation and a late glycosyltransferase mutation may generate different intermediate profiles and phenotypes. The plant also needs to avoid accumulating potentially disruptive intermediates in sensitive locations, making compartmentation and pathway coordination important components of the defense system.

The sterol branch and the specialized triterpenoid branch share an early precursor but use different products of oxidosqualene cyclization. It is helpful to imagine their competition at the level of enzyme access rather than as a simple switch that routes all substrate to one destination. Cycloartenol synthase and other oxidosqualene cyclases may be expressed in different organs or cell types, so they may not compete directly for a completely mixed pool. Where enzymes do access the same substrate pool, their abundance, catalytic efficiency and localization influence allocation. Increasing a specialized cyclase can therefore alter sterol availability in some systems, but a proportional sterol decrease is not inevitable. Increased upstream production or spatial separation could maintain the sterol branch.

Plant sterols have a structural role in the lipid bilayer because their molecular geometry restricts some motions of adjacent lipid chains while preventing excessively tight packing in other conditions. At higher temperatures, sterol rings can reduce excessive acyl-chain disorder; at lower temperatures sterols can interfere with the formation of tightly ordered lipid arrangements. This buffering description is a useful qualitative model, but a particular membrane's behavior also depends on its lipid species and sterol identity. Sterols may affect membrane-protein activity indirectly by changing the local lipid environment, or more directly through specific interactions. A mutant affecting one sterol conversion can thus produce a membrane phenotype even if its total sterol concentration does not fall substantially.

Campesterol, sitosterol and stigmasterol differ in features of their side chains, including substituents or double bonds. These differences can change the way sterols pack within membranes and interact with enzymes. Their biosynthetic interconversion is not an arbitrary set of naming conventions. For example, a sterol desaturase that introduces a specific double bond can change the balance between sitosterol and stigmasterol. A chemical profile reporting the sum of all sterols could miss a biologically meaningful shift in this ratio. Examination problems often provide several related sterol concentrations precisely because the distribution, not merely the total, reveals the biochemical defect.

Triterpenoid saponin pathways commonly involve a sequence of cyclization, oxidative tailoring and glycosylation. The aglycone may be modified at several positions before sugars are attached. Sugar identity, linkage and position are important because they change the three-dimensional arrangement and solubility of the resulting molecule. The word glycoside simply indicates a sugar linked to another chemical group; it does not imply that the compound is a saponin. A flavonoid glycoside, for example, has a different core biosynthetic origin even if it also contains glucose. Conversely, removal of sugars from a saponin yields a sapogenin whose properties may differ substantially from those of the intact glycoside.

The interaction of some saponins with membranes illustrates the balance between hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions. An amphipathic molecule may approach a membrane interface with its hydrophobic region tending toward the lipid interior and its hydrophilic sugars remaining exposed to water. This orientation can alter membrane organization, especially when particular sterols are present. The resulting biological effects vary with saponin structure and target membrane composition. An assay using artificial liposomes with and without sterol can therefore address a specific mechanistic hypothesis about membrane dependence; results must not be generalized automatically to the metabolism or survival of an intact organism.

Root-produced avenacins illustrate the importance of pathway completion. A plant may synthesize a triterpene scaffold normally yet fail to generate an active defensive glycoside because it lacks a later modification. The missing glycosylated product and the accumulation of its precursor provide clues to the defective step. The phenotype of such a mutant can reflect both loss of a defensive compound and unwanted accumulation of an intermediate. To distinguish these explanations, comparing multiple mutations that block different stages can be particularly useful. If all lines lacking the final product show susceptibility but only one accumulates a particular intermediate and exhibits root damage, the two phenotypes may have distinct biochemical causes.

A comparison of membrane sterols and secreted saponins also clarifies a general distinction between structural and specialized roles. A membrane sterol is positioned within the lipid bilayer and helps establish its physical environment. A saponin with a related steroidal or triterpenoid core bears sugar residues that strongly alter its interactions with water and membranes. Neither the shared biosynthetic ancestry nor the presence of a rigid ring system makes the two classes functionally interchangeable. In fact, the amphipathic properties that allow certain saponins to interact with sterol-containing membranes may require careful control of their localization in the producing plant itself.

A model experiment can examine the contribution of sugar attachment to biological activity. Suppose a purified aglycone and its glycoside are compared at equal molar concentrations in a membrane-permeability assay. Equal mass concentrations would not constitute an equal molecular exposure because the glycoside has a larger molar mass. If the glycoside is more active, the result supports a role for sugar substitution in that assay; it does not imply that increasing the number of sugars always increases activity. Position, linkage and target membrane chemistry also matter. Testing compounds with defined structures is necessary to attribute differences to particular chemical features rather than to other constituents of a crude extract.

The carbon accounting of a hypothetical glycoside provides another useful example. A C30 sapogenin receives two six-carbon sugar residues and one five-carbon sugar residue. The complete molecule contains 30 + 6 + 6 + 5 = 47 carbon atoms. Glycosidic bond formation involves a change in hydrogen and oxygen accounting, but no carbon is lost in the simple attachment described here. The compound remains a triterpenoid glycoside because the aglycone has triterpenoid ancestry. An answer that classifies it as a C45 or C50 terpene on the basis of approximate total carbon count would confuse the origin of the scaffold with the composition of the conjugate.

Membrane homeostasis involves regulation of the total sterol pool and the ratios of individual sterol species. If a plant experiences a change in temperature, the physical state of membrane lipids changes even before the composition has adjusted. Over a longer period, metabolism may change lipid and sterol composition to help maintain membrane function. A static measurement of membrane order at one temperature cannot establish the entire adaptive response. An experimental study comparing genotypes should consider their initial membrane composition and the time allowed for acclimation. This distinction is valuable when interpreting the physiological consequences of altered isoprenoid metabolism in the stress-physiology subunit.

7. Tetraterpenes, carotenoid biosynthesis and photoprotection

Phytoene synthase catalyzes the first committed step of carotenoid biosynthesis by coupling two GGPP molecules to form the C40 precursor phytoene. Phytoene lacks the extended conjugation characteristic of strongly colored mature carotenoids. Desaturation and isomerization reactions generate lycopene, an acyclic carotene with an extended conjugated double-bond system. Cyclization at the ends of lycopene produces distinct carotene branches. Two beta rings characterize beta-carotene; alpha-carotene contains a beta ring and an epsilon ring. Hydroxylation and other oxygenation reactions produce xanthophylls, including lutein and zeaxanthin. Carotenes contain only carbon and hydrogen, whereas xanthophylls contain oxygen.

Conjugation means that alternating single and double bonds permit delocalization of pi electrons over part of the molecule. The electronic transitions associated with extended conjugation allow many carotenoids to absorb visible light, especially in the blue to blue-green region. Their apparent color reflects the wavelengths that are transmitted or reflected rather than those strongly absorbed. The precise spectrum depends on molecular structure, environment and interactions with proteins. In leaves, chlorophyll often dominates the visible appearance even when substantial carotenoids are present. Chlorophyll degradation can reveal yellow carotenoids during senescence; active synthesis and altered storage of carotenoids may additionally change color during fruit ripening.

In chloroplasts, carotenoids occur in photosynthetic pigment-protein complexes and membranes. Their roles include harvesting light that can be transferred to chlorophyll, stabilizing pigment-protein assemblies, quenching harmful excited states and protecting membrane components from oxidation. Chlorophyll triplet states may interact with molecular oxygen and promote formation of singlet oxygen. Appropriately positioned carotenoids can intercept excitation or quench reactive oxygen species, limiting damage. Photoprotection therefore involves controlling the fate of energy, not simply screening light before it enters a leaf.

The violaxanthin cycle links lumen acidification under strong illumination with reversible pigment conversion. Violaxanthin de-epoxidase converts violaxanthin through antheraxanthin to zeaxanthin; zeaxanthin epoxidase favors the reverse sequence during recovery. Together with lumen pH and proteins such as PsbS, the pigment state contributes to rapidly inducible nonphotochemical quenching. Excitation energy that exceeds the capacity for photochemical use can thereby be dissipated as heat. The system must balance protection with carbon assimilation: persistent dissipation after irradiance falls can limit productive light use. Zeaxanthin concentration is informative but is not a complete measure of every component of nonphotochemical quenching.

A hypothetical experiment records maximal chlorophyll fluorescence in the dark-adapted state, Fm, and maximal fluorescence in the illuminated state, Fm prime. One conventional NPQ index is (Fm minus Fm prime) divided by Fm prime. If Fm is 900 arbitrary units and Fm prime is 300, NPQ is (900 minus 300)/300 = 2.0, a dimensionless number. If a mutant under the same conditions has Fm prime of 600 and the same dark-adapted Fm, NPQ is 0.5. The mutant's smaller NPQ suggests impaired inducible dissipation under these conditions, but the index alone does not identify the defective gene or quantify irreversible damage. Pigment analysis, recovery kinetics and matched growth conditions are needed.

Carotenoids are also precursors for several smaller signaling compounds, known collectively as apocarotenoids. Oxidative cleavage of particular epoxycarotenoids participates in abscisic acid biosynthesis, and cleavage and rearrangement of beta-carotene-derived intermediates supplies strigolactone biosynthesis. Different cleavage positions can yield products with distinct carbon counts and volatility. The plant-hormone chapters explain these signaling pathways in greater detail; here the important connection is that one pool of C40 compounds supports pigmentation, protection and production of developmental signals.

The different plastid types in plants create contrasting conditions for carotenoid accumulation. In a green leaf, chloroplasts contain thylakoid membranes, within which carotenoids occupy specific positions in photosynthetic complexes. In a ripening fruit, chloroplasts may differentiate into chromoplasts with specialized structures that accumulate large quantities of particular pigments. This transition involves changes in synthesis, degradation and storage capacity. Increasing the abundance of a carotenoid biosynthetic enzyme may have little effect on final pigment concentration if the tissue lacks appropriate structures for sequestration or if the pigment is rapidly degraded. Conversely, improved storage capacity can increase pigment accumulation without an equivalent increase in its instantaneous synthesis rate.

The biosynthesis of phytoene represents a commitment of two GGPP molecules to the carotenoid pathway. GGPP is also required for other plastidial products, so allocation at this branch point can affect several processes. A mutant deficient in phytoene synthase may have low concentrations of downstream carotenes and xanthophylls, yet the amount of GGPP need not increase in direct proportion to the loss of carotenoids. The precursor can be used by competing branches or its synthesis can be regulated. A mutation in the next series of desaturation reactions has a different expected effect: phytoene or partially desaturated intermediates may accumulate. Their presence, together with loss of downstream pigments, helps distinguish the steps that are impaired.

In higher plants the conversion from phytoene to lycopene includes several enzyme-catalyzed desaturation and isomerization reactions. Desaturation introduces double bonds, whereas isomerization changes the spatial arrangement around existing double bonds. Both kinds of reactions contribute to formation of the appropriate conjugated system and substrate geometry for downstream cyclization. A student should not equate an increase in the number of double bonds with an increase in carbon number. The C40 skeleton remains during these reactions. Nor does the presence of an extended conjugated system imply that every carotenoid has identical spectral characteristics. Differences in effective conjugation, end-group geometry and the molecular environment affect light absorption.

The two ends of the lycopene molecule offer a particularly clear example of how enzyme competition produces branches. Formation of beta rings at both ends produces beta-carotene. Formation of one beta and one epsilon ring yields alpha-carotene. These branches supply characteristic oxygenated derivatives through hydroxylation. Beta-carotene is converted into zeaxanthin by hydroxylation of the beta rings, whereas lutein derives from alpha-carotene. A reduction in epsilon-ring-forming activity may decrease alpha-carotene and lutein while favoring the beta branch, provided substrate supply and other enzyme activities permit this redistribution. This is a conditional prediction: changes in the stability of photosynthetic complexes or feedback on synthesis can modify the measured response.

Carotenoid molecules do not exist in the thylakoid as an unorganized solution of pigments. Their association with particular proteins and lipid environments determines where they can transfer excitation energy or intercept potentially damaging excited states. Energy transfer requires appropriate energetic relationships and sufficient proximity between donor and acceptor molecules. A carotenoid positioned in a light-harvesting complex may transfer excitation to chlorophyll, extending the spectrum available for photochemistry. In a different excited-state context, transfer in the opposite functional direction can contribute to protection of the photosynthetic apparatus. Thus the same family of molecules supports both acquisition and controlled dissipation of light energy. The biological outcome depends on the state of the pigment-protein system.

A distinction between triplet chlorophyll and singlet oxygen is important for understanding photoprotection. After absorption of a photon, chlorophyll enters an excited electronic state. Most excitation in a functional photosynthetic system is directed toward photochemistry, fluorescence or regulated thermal dissipation. A fraction may enter a longer-lived triplet state. Molecular oxygen in its ground state can interact with triplet chlorophyll and acquire excitation energy, generating singlet oxygen. This reactive form of oxygen may damage proteins and lipids. Carotenoids can reduce the probability of its formation by quenching chlorophyll triplets and can also quench singlet oxygen. These protective roles should not be confused with the enzymatic reduction of hydrogen peroxide by peroxidases.

Photosynthetic energy balance changes continually in natural environments. A cloud can reduce incident irradiance within seconds, while a sudden sunfleck can raise it just as rapidly. The rate of carbon fixation may not adjust immediately because enzyme activation, availability of electron acceptors and downstream metabolic demand also change over time. Under high illumination the thylakoid proton gradient increases as electron transport drives proton accumulation in the lumen. Acidification acts as a signal that helps activate protective dissipation. It also promotes activity of violaxanthin de-epoxidase in the xanthophyll cycle. Thus a physical consequence of photosynthetic electron transport is coupled to a reversible biochemical change in carotenoid composition.

Violaxanthin possesses two epoxide groups, antheraxanthin one, and zeaxanthin none. The de-epoxidase removes these groups in two sequential steps; epoxidation restores them during recovery. The sum of the three pigments can remain approximately constant during a short exposure even when their relative proportions change greatly. This illustrates a regulation strategy based on conversion of existing molecules rather than complete resynthesis of the pigment pool. Over longer periods the total pool size can also change through biosynthesis and turnover. A time-course experiment must therefore distinguish rapid interconversion from slower changes in pigment abundance.

A useful index of xanthophyll-cycle state is the de-epoxidation state, often expressed as the sum of zeaxanthin plus half the antheraxanthin divided by the total violaxanthin, antheraxanthin and zeaxanthin pool. The exact definition should always be checked because studies may report alternative indices. In an illustrative leaf sampled before intense light exposure, suppose the concentrations are 80, 10 and 10 nanomoles per gram for violaxanthin, antheraxanthin and zeaxanthin respectively. The index is (10 + 0.5 × 10)/100 = 0.15. After high light, the concentrations become 20, 20 and 60, giving (60 + 0.5 × 20)/100 = 0.70. The total is unchanged but the conversion state differs markedly. The index does not directly measure heat release, because pigment composition is only one determinant of nonphotochemical quenching.

An examination problem may compare a mutant deficient in violaxanthin de-epoxidase with a mutant deficient in a photosynthetic regulatory protein such as PsbS. Both could show impaired rapid photoprotective dissipation, yet their pigment profiles may differ. The first is expected to have impaired formation of zeaxanthin through the normal de-epoxidation sequence; the second can retain pigment interconversion but show altered coupling between lumen acidification and antenna regulation. Measuring only fluorescence may not distinguish the mutants. Combining fluorescence with chromatographic pigment quantification, proton-gradient measurements where relevant and genotyping yields a more discriminating interpretation.

The fluorescence-based NPQ parameter compares maximal fluorescence in dark-adapted and illuminated states, typically using saturating light pulses to minimize differences attributable simply to the instantaneous fraction of open PSII reaction centers. A higher NPQ often indicates greater nonphotochemical dissipation, but the index can include multiple components with different relaxation times. A rapid reversible component is particularly relevant to energy-dependent quenching. Slower changes can reflect additional regulatory processes or photoinhibition. To distinguish reversible protection from persistent damage, scientists follow fluorescence during recovery and may measure the maximum PSII photochemical efficiency, Fv/Fm, after adequate dark adaptation. No single fluorescence number is a complete description of photosynthetic health.

Pigmentation outside the chloroplast supplies another important structure–function example. Carotenoids in many flowers and fruits affect the visual signals presented to pollinators and dispersers. The color perceived by a particular animal depends on the pigment absorption spectrum, tissue scattering, other pigments and the sensory system of the observer. A red or orange phenotype is not sufficient evidence that carotenoids alone produce the color; anthocyanins or other pigment families may also contribute. Extraction, spectral analysis and chromatographic identification are needed to distinguish the molecular causes of a visible phenotype. The next chapter introduces aromatic metabolites, including the precursor chemistry that eventually gives rise to flavonoid pigments.

Carotenoid cleavage can couple the status of a pigment pool to developmental or stress-related signaling. The cleavage enzymes recognize specific substrates and positions rather than simply fragmenting carotenoids at random. Abscisic acid biosynthesis requires appropriately configured epoxycarotenoid precursors. Strigolactone biosynthesis begins with transformations of beta-carotene and subsequent cleavage reactions. These pathways use only a portion of the original C40 skeleton in their final signaling products. A mutation early in carotenoid biosynthesis can therefore affect more than pigmentation: it may influence hormonal processes, plastid function and protection under high light. Supplying a downstream hormone could rescue one hormone-dependent response but would not restore the missing pigments or their direct photoprotective roles.

Carotenoid-derived aroma compounds provide a bridge back to the volatile terpenoid section. Some cleavage products are small enough to contribute to fruit or flower scents. Their concentration may rise during ripening because precursor availability, cleavage activity and tissue structure change. An increase in the abundance of a carotenoid does not automatically produce an increase in every volatile derived from it. The cleavage enzyme must be present, the relevant molecular substrate must be accessible and the volatile must reach the surrounding air. This is a recurring principle of pathway analysis: the amount of an upstream precursor sets possible availability but does not establish the actual rate of every downstream branch.

8. Polyterpenes and natural rubber

Natural rubber is predominantly high-molecular-mass cis-1,4-polyisoprene. Its repeating units each contribute five carbon atoms and are arranged in a long flexible chain. Cis geometry makes the chain conformation distinct from that of trans-polyisoprene, a material found in other plant sources. The same gross repeat-unit formula can thus correspond to materials with quite different packing and mechanical properties. Elasticity arises in part from changes in conformational entropy as chains are stretched and allowed to recoil; macroscopic properties also depend on chain length, interactions, crosslinking and temperature.

In the rubber tree Hevea brasiliensis, natural rubber accumulates in latex within specialized laticifers. Rubber particles provide a hydrophobic interior surrounded by a lipid-protein interface. A rubber-transferase system containing cis-prenyltransferase-related machinery extends an allylic starter repeatedly using IPP, with other proteins and the particle environment contributing to the process. This polymerization should not be confused with the industrial polymerization of isolated free isoprene. In living plants the activated diphosphate precursor supplies the extending units.

When latex escapes from a damaged laticifer, its physical and chemical constituents can impede some herbivores and contribute to wound responses. Latex is not synonymous with pure rubber: the exudate can contain proteins and many other compounds, and different species have very different compositions. The ecological function of a latex mixture cannot be deduced solely from the mechanical characteristics of purified polyisoprene.

Ignoring end-group contributions, a repeat unit C5H8 has molar mass approximately 68.12 grams per mole. A hypothetical chain with a number-average molar mass of 680,000 grams per mole corresponds to a number-average degree of polymerization near 680000/68.12 = 9982, or roughly ten thousand repeat units. Real samples contain distributions of molecular sizes rather than identical chains. This calculation explains the scale difference between small terpenoids and rubber without implying that any particular natural sample has exactly this chain length.

Natural rubber is an instructive contrast to terpenoids with a defined small-molecule carbon count. In ordinary prenyl diphosphate synthesis, a prenyltransferase releases a product of characteristic length. A rubber-producing enzyme complex can continue elongation through many cycles, giving rise to molecules with a broad distribution of chain lengths. The physical environment of the growing polymer becomes important because a chain containing thousands of C5 units cannot be accommodated entirely in a conventional small enzyme active site. The rubber particle provides a location in which the hydrophobic product can accumulate while the biosynthetic machinery remains associated with an interface accessible to aqueous substrates.

An allylic diphosphate initiator can determine the starting structure of the chain, while subsequent addition of IPP supplies the repeating units. For a simplified accounting problem, if an FPP-derived C15 starter is extended by 1000 molecules of IPP, the resulting carbon skeleton contains 15 + 1000 × 5 = 5015 carbon atoms, assuming all incorporated carbons remain in the final product. The starter itself is equivalent to three five-carbon units. A question that asks for the number of added IPP molecules should not be answered by counting the starter again as an extender. In real natural rubber, initiation and termination chemistry and the distribution of chain lengths complicate the exact relationship between mass and carbon number, but the accounting principle remains useful.

The cis designation refers to the configuration of substituents around a carbon-carbon double bond within the repeating backbone. It does not mean that all parts of a long rubber molecule lie on one geometric plane or that the molecule forms a single fixed shape. Rotation around other bonds gives flexible conformations. The distribution of these conformations changes when the material is stretched. Trans-polyisoprene can pack more regularly under many conditions, tending toward different crystallization and mechanical behavior. The comparison shows that stereochemistry can profoundly affect a material even when the basic repeating-unit formula is identical.

Molar mass is an average property of a heterogeneous polymer sample. A number-average molar mass gives equal weight to every molecule when taking the average. A mass-average molar mass gives greater influence to heavier molecules. A material containing a broad distribution of polymer lengths can have substantially different values for these two averages. Dividing the number-average molar mass by the repeat-unit molar mass gives an approximate number-average degree of polymerization when end groups are negligible. It does not identify the length of the longest chain, or even imply that a chain of exactly that average length exists. This distinction is useful when interpreting polymer data without requiring specialist polymer chemistry.

Natural latex contains a dispersed phase and a continuous aqueous phase. The hydrophobic rubber is associated with particles rather than being dissolved freely in water as individual enormous chains. Proteins and lipids at the particle surface influence both the stability of that dispersion and the association of biosynthetic machinery. In a wounded plant, release of latex and subsequent changes in the exudate may depend on hydraulic pressure in laticifers, coagulation processes and environmental exposure. A measurement of rubber content in a tissue cannot by itself predict how much latex will flow after injury. Structure, storage and physiological state must also be considered.

A laticifer is a specialized cell or cellular system containing latex. It is distinct from an ordinary xylem vessel or phloem sieve element, even though all these structures can extend through substantial regions of a plant. Rubber production in laticifers therefore represents an example of metabolic specialization linked to tissue organization. A gene encoding a rubber-associated enzyme may be expressed very strongly in latex-producing tissue but weakly elsewhere. Tissue-specific expression data are useful for identifying candidates, yet catalytic assays and genetic perturbations are still required to establish the protein's precise contribution to rubber biosynthesis.

Other biological polyisoprenoids are important even when they are not macroscopic elastic materials. Dolichol-related molecules participate in cellular glycosylation processes, and related prenyl chains occur in several essential compounds. These examples should not be conflated with high-molecular-mass rubber simply because their carbon skeletons consist of repeated isoprenoid units. Chain length, terminal groups, degree of saturation, stereochemistry and biological localization differ. A question comparing a C40 carotenoid with a long polyisoprenoid must take into account the specific conjugated chromophore of carotenoids and the polymeric architecture of rubber, rather than using only total hydrophobicity as an explanation.

When a researcher finds that a mutant latex sample has lower rubber content, several explanations are possible. The mutation might reduce IPP supply, interfere with initiation, affect the rate or processivity of chain elongation, alter particle stability or reduce the development of laticifers. Measuring only the dry mass of rubber cannot distinguish these possibilities. A change in molecular-mass distribution with little change in total polymer mass might indicate altered chain elongation or termination. Fewer particles of normal polymer length might instead suggest a change in the number of initiation events or storage sites. Combining chemical analysis with microscopy and enzyme assays separates biochemical and structural hypotheses.

9. Experimental reasoning across terpene families

A pathway experiment may alter a precursor-producing enzyme, a branch-point enzyme or a final tailoring enzyme. The expected consequences differ. Reducing the supply of GGPP may affect several plastidial products simultaneously, whereas disabling a single specialized diterpene cyclase may alter a much narrower group of metabolites. Suppressing a glycosyltransferase can alter the ratio of aglycone to glycoside without reducing the total amount of the original scaffold. Such predictions assume that other enzymes and precursor transport remain unchanged; feedback, alternative routes and compensatory gene expression can invalidate this simplifying assumption.

Isotope labeling provides an independent way to test precursor-product relationships. If a labeled carbon source supplies an isoprenoid pathway, subsequent analysis can detect label incorporation into products. Labeling enrichment depends on dilution by unlabeled pools, turnover and compartmental exchange. Failure to detect a label after a brief pulse does not prove that a pathway contributes nothing to a compound. A time course and measurements of intermediate labeling help distinguish slow incorporation from genuine lack of connectivity. Because the MVA and MEP routes have different initial substrates and rearrangements, carefully designed positional labeling experiments can be more informative than simply comparing total label abundance.

Gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry is particularly useful for many volatile mono- and sesquiterpenes. Liquid chromatography with appropriate optical or mass-spectrometric detection is more suitable for many less volatile or thermally labile glycosides and pigments. Chromatographic separation precedes detection and can distinguish compounds of similar molecular mass. Authentic standards, retention behavior and fragmentation evidence improve identification; a single mass peak is not sufficient to distinguish every structural or stereochemical isomer. An extract measures recoverable stored material under particular conditions, while a headspace collection measures compounds released into air. These quantities answer different biological questions.

A conceptual branch-flux calculation illustrates the difference between pool size and flux. If a tissue supplies six micromoles of FPP per hour, and four are consumed by sesquiterpene synthesis while two are consumed by the squalene branch, the maximum squalene formation rate is one micromole per hour because two FPP molecules contribute to each squalene. If the sesquiterpene synthase activity falls, the unused FPP may become available for sterols, other pathways or degradation. It need not all become squalene, because squalene synthase may already operate near saturation. Increased precursor concentration may also reduce upstream synthesis through regulatory feedback. The stoichiometric result provides a useful constraint but cannot replace measured flux.

A functional test of an emitted compound should separate biosynthetic effects from ecological effects. For example, compare wild-type plants and a synthase-deficient line with similar growth and tissue damage, measure the relevant volatile and assess the behavior of a potential natural enemy in an appropriately controlled choice assay. Genetic complementation can support the causal relationship between the enzyme and emission. Testing restoration of the volatile signal can help determine whether the compound itself explains the behavioral effect. Field conditions introduce additional variables such as airflow, background odors, soil properties and the presence of other organisms. The conclusion should therefore match the experimental setting rather than treating one laboratory response as a universal ecological rule.

The unifying principle is that terpenoid diversity has three levels: activated precursor supply determines which carbon skeletons can be constructed, enzyme selectivity determines their connectivity and functional groups, and cellular organization determines which compounds reach the places where they act. This framework also prepares the next chapter, where aromatic precursors and the shikimate pathway explain a different but partly interconnected family of specialized metabolites.

The first step in interpreting a terpenoid experiment is to identify precisely what variable was measured. The amount of compound per gram fresh mass may depend on tissue water content. The amount per gram dry mass removes that particular denominator effect but still combines differences among cell types. The amount per organ can change simply because the organ grows larger, even if its concentration remains constant. A volatile emission rate measured in nanomoles per hour differs from all three. Examination datasets may deliberately vary denominators, requiring students to convert quantities before drawing conclusions about biosynthesis. Careful attention to units prevents a numerical difference from being mistaken for a biological mechanism.

Consider a hypothetical comparison of two plants. A control leaf has 0.5 grams fresh mass and contains 20 nanomoles of a monoterpenoid. A treated leaf has 0.8 grams fresh mass and contains 24 nanomoles. The treated leaf contains 20 percent more compound per leaf, since (24−20)/20 = 0.20. Its concentration is 30 nanomoles per gram, however, compared with 40 nanomoles per gram in the control. Concentration has therefore fallen by 25 percent while total amount has increased. Both calculations are correct. Which is informative depends on the biological question, such as chemical exposure per unit tissue eaten or total stored metabolite in an organ. Neither establishes instantaneous biosynthetic flux without additional temporal information.

Pool changes can be formalized using a simple mass balance. Suppose P denotes the amount of a product in nanomoles and its rate of synthesis is v_s in nanomoles per hour. If the product is degraded or exported at rate v_d, then dP/dt = v_s − v_d. If loss is approximately first order, v_d = kP, where k has units per hour. At steady state, P = v_s/k. Doubling the synthesis rate doubles the steady-state pool if the loss constant remains unchanged. Halving the loss constant has the same mathematical effect. Therefore a larger measured pool is compatible with increased synthesis, decreased loss or a combination. This simple model is not intended to represent every enzyme in a plant; it illustrates why concentration alone is insufficient evidence for a particular change in flux.

A pulse-labeling experiment can partly resolve this ambiguity. Introducing a labeled precursor for a short period may reveal newly synthesized product before the total pool changes appreciably. If a treated tissue has a higher rate of incorporation into a product with comparable precursor enrichment, that supports increased synthesis. But if the precursor itself is more highly labeled in one treatment, more product label can appear without any increase in total synthesis. Normalizing to the labeling of the relevant precursor and measuring its enrichment through time helps avoid this confusion. The experimental logic is to distinguish the amount of material in a pool from the movement of atoms into and out of it.

In a branching pathway, competition occurs when multiple enzymes draw on the same accessible substrate. An elementary enzyme-kinetic representation is v = Vmax S/(Km + S), where S is substrate concentration, Vmax is maximal rate and Km is the substrate concentration at half of Vmax for this simplified model. Imagine two enzymes consuming the same FPP pool, each with Vmax of 10 nanomoles per minute, but with Km values of 1 and 9 micromolar. At S = 1 micromolar, the rates are 5 and 1 nanomoles per minute. At S = 9 micromolar, they are 9 and 5 nanomoles per minute. Increasing substrate supply changes both the absolute rates and their ratio. The model assumes independent enzymes, a common accessible pool and no regulatory feedback; real intracellular organization may violate these assumptions.

A high-affinity enzyme does not always dominate a branch. A low-affinity enzyme can have a much greater Vmax if it is more abundant or has a larger catalytic turnover. At sufficiently high substrate concentration, both may approach their respective Vmax values. Conversely, an enzyme with a smaller Km can be important when substrate is scarce even if its maximum capacity is low. Questions that provide only Km values cannot determine flux allocation without information about enzyme abundance or Vmax. This principle applies equally to GPP-consuming synthases, FPP-consuming pathways and carotenoid branch enzymes, and connects the metabolic chapter with the general enzyme-kinetics unit.

The location of a gene product is another distinct line of evidence. A fluorescent protein fusion may indicate that a candidate synthase is associated with a plastid, the cytosol or an endomembrane system. Such a fusion must be designed and interpreted cautiously because attaching a marker can disrupt a targeting signal or protein behavior. Independent evidence from fractionation or other localization approaches can strengthen the conclusion. Localization constrains which substrates the enzyme normally encounters, but localization alone does not establish that catalysis occurs at a particular rate. A plastid-targeted protein could be inactive because a cofactor is lacking or because its expression occurs at a time when the appropriate substrate is scarce.

Pathway reconstruction in a heterologous host offers a way to test whether a proposed set of enzymes is sufficient to generate a compound. If expression of a terpene synthase produces a scaffold, and addition of an oxidase produces the predicted oxygenated derivative, the results support the proposed reaction sequence. If addition of a glycosyltransferase yields the final glycoside, the sequence becomes more compelling. Negative controls are necessary because the host may carry endogenous enzymes that perform related transformations. Moreover, sufficiency in the host does not establish that all the same enzymes are necessary in the original plant, which could contain redundant or alternative routes.

Gene knockout and genetic complementation address necessity and causality. If a knockout lacks a product, the gene may be required for its normal synthesis, although the effect could be indirect. Restoration of the product when a functional copy is reintroduced supports a causal role. Demonstrating catalytic activity of the encoded protein helps distinguish a direct biosynthetic enzyme from a regulator of the pathway. If the knockout does not alter product concentration, the enzyme may be redundant, required only in another organ or condition, or unrelated to the product. A null phenotype is therefore not sufficient to dismiss the gene without considering the circumstances in which it was tested.

Natural variation among plant populations can similarly reveal candidate pathways. Suppose several accessions of a species differ in whether they emit a monoterpene. Genetic mapping may identify a genomic region associated with the trait, and biochemical analysis may reveal a TPS gene within that region. Yet association does not establish that a difference in the coding sequence is responsible. Differences in expression, upstream substrate supply, copy number or adjacent regulatory DNA may account for the phenotype. Comparing enzyme sequences and their activities, measuring transcription in matched tissues and testing genetic complementation are useful steps toward identifying the relevant causal variation.

Analytical identification deserves particular attention because many terpenoids are isomeric. In gas chromatography, compounds travel through a column according to interactions with the stationary phase and their volatility. They leave the column at different retention times. Mass spectrometry then provides molecular and fragmentation information. A library match offers a candidate identity, but related structures may yield similar fragmentation patterns. Comparing with an authentic standard and retention indices under appropriate conditions improves confidence. Distinguishing enantiomers may require a chiral stationary phase or another stereochemically sensitive method. A claim that a plant emits a particular stereoisomer should be supported by a technique capable of distinguishing it.

For a nonvolatile glycoside or pigment, liquid chromatography can separate compounds without requiring them to vaporize. Detection may use absorbance at wavelengths characteristic of a chromophore or mass-spectrometric information. A mass difference corresponding to loss of a sugar residue can suggest a glycosylated structure, but it does not necessarily determine which sugar, its linkage or its attachment position. More detailed structural evidence may be required. Within the present syllabus, the important distinction is between detecting a molecular feature and establishing a full chemical structure. The same reasoning applies to identifying an unknown carotenoid by its visible spectrum: a compatible spectrum narrows candidates but may not uniquely identify the molecule.

An illustrative calibration example shows how detector signals can be converted to quantities. Suppose standards give a linear relationship between corrected peak area and amount injected, with slope 200 area units per nanomole and negligible intercept over the tested range. A sample peak area of 1000 corresponds to 5 nanomoles in the injected aliquot. If that aliquot was one-tenth of the original extract and there were no losses, the extract contained 50 nanomoles. If extraction recovery was independently estimated as 80 percent, the inferred original amount would be 50/0.8 = 62.5 nanomoles. These corrections assume the sample behaves like the standards and that the recovery estimate is applicable. The procedure explains why raw peak areas from two chemically different compounds should not automatically be compared as equal molar amounts.

A functional ecological experiment must address alternative explanations for a behavioral difference. A mutant that lacks a floral volatile may also have smaller flowers, altered nectar production or changes in flowering time. If pollinator visitation is reduced, these other traits could contribute. A rigorous comparison controls or measures relevant nonvolatile characteristics, and a restoration experiment can test whether adding the missing signal changes behavior. The strongest argument links the molecular perturbation to a chemical change and then to a specific receiver response, while considering the possibility that multiple traits interact. Field outcomes should not be predicted from a single artificial exposure without acknowledging differences in environmental conditions.

An example involving root defense makes the same point from a different direction. A line deficient in a triterpenoid glycoside might show increased fungal growth in roots. To establish a defensive role of the metabolite, researchers could compare the susceptibility of multiple independent mutants and complemented lines, and determine whether the fungus responds to the compound under relevant conditions. They should also examine root development, because unhealthy or damaged roots might be more susceptible independently of the missing chemical. If a precursor accumulates and harms root tissues, the susceptibility could partly reflect an altered physical barrier. This is why metabolite profiles and tissue phenotypes should be considered together.

A photosynthetic pigment experiment similarly needs several measurements. Lower chlorophyll fluorescence in a carotenoid-deficient line could reflect lower chlorophyll content, fewer functioning photosystems, changed energy transfer or altered quenching. Absorbance spectra and pigment separation provide information about pigment composition, while fluorescence parameters address photochemical and nonphotochemical processes. Growth and recovery after high-light exposure offer additional functional evidence. The findings are most useful when the plants are compared under matched developmental and environmental conditions. A severely pale mutant and a normal green leaf cannot be assumed to absorb the same amount of incident radiation.

A central examination skill is to distinguish a direct product of a reaction from a downstream molecule. In a TPS assay supplied with GPP, a C10 hydrocarbon might be the immediate product, whereas an oxygenated C10 glycoside in the native tissue could require several additional enzymes. In a squalene synthase assay the expected direct C30 product is squalene, not sitosterol or a saponin. In a phytoene synthase assay, the direct product is phytoene rather than beta-carotene. These distinctions matter because an enzyme can be essential for a downstream compound without catalyzing the reaction that produces that final compound directly.

The same logic applies to an inhibitor that acts before a branch point. A decline in both sterols and triterpenoid saponins after disruption of squalene synthesis would support their shared precursor ancestry. It would not mean that a sterol is converted directly into every triterpene saponin. A decline in all carotenoid pigments after loss of phytoene synthesis would likewise reflect the common entry step, not direct synthesis of each pigment by phytoene synthase. When interpreting a diagram, students should trace the arrows in their stated direction and distinguish converging from diverging reactions. A common precursor relationship does not imply an obligatory sequential relationship among the products.

Not all metabolites of mixed biosynthetic origin fit into one exclusive classification. A prenylated aromatic compound may combine a shikimate-derived or polyketide-derived aromatic skeleton with an isoprenoid side group. Some alkaloids incorporate a terpenoid-derived moiety together with a nitrogen-containing component. Such hybrid structures illustrate how specialized metabolic pathways are interconnected. Classification can refer to the core scaffold, the presence of a functional group or biosynthetic ancestry; these criteria need not yield identical labels. The subsequent nodes will develop aromatic and nitrogenous pathways in their own right, while the present chapter establishes how to recognize an isoprenoid contribution without mistaking every mixed product for a simple terpene.

A useful final synthesis is to consider the relationship between synthesis, allocation and compartmentation at the level of an entire plant. Carbon assimilated during photosynthesis can be delivered to developing roots, fruits and leaves through source–sink transport. Those organs may use the incoming carbon for growth, respiration, storage and specialized biosynthesis in different proportions. A root with active triterpene production and a flower emitting monoterpenes therefore need not use different kinds of transported sugar. They differ in the biochemical networks that process the carbon after arrival. Moreover, local precursor availability depends on energy status, cellular differentiation and regulatory state. A leaf's increased photosynthetic rate does not guarantee a proportionate rise in every specialized compound throughout the plant.

The importance of this perspective becomes clear when interpreting engineering experiments. If increasing a branch enzyme raises product concentration but substantially reduces growth, the altered allocation may have affected essential pathways or imposed other metabolic costs. A larger product concentration per gram does not guarantee a larger total yield per plant if biomass falls. Conversely, a moderate concentration in a much larger plant may correspond to greater total production. A useful assessment compares product amount, growth, essential metabolite pools and environmental responses rather than one chemical metric. The detailed principles of metabolic engineering belong to a subsequent node; here the relevant lesson is that a shared precursor network imposes biological constraints on attempts to modify a single branch.

10. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Free isoprene is the usual building block. Activated IPP and DMAPP are the actual substrates. The structural isoprene rule is not a literal polymerization mechanism; identify the activated precursor when asked about biosynthesis.
  2. All terpenoids are secondary and nonessential. Sterols and carotenoids have core cellular functions. A compound's biosynthetic family is not evidence that it is dispensable.
  3. All C30-derived compounds contain exactly 30 carbons. Sterol processing can change carbon number, and sugars can add carbons to a saponin. Classify the scaffold before counting the entire derivative.
  4. GPP is the same as GGPP. Geranyl diphosphate contains 10 carbons; geranylgeranyl diphosphate contains 20. Trace the successive C5 additions.
  5. MVA and MEP contributions are completely exclusive. The typical compartment association is a strong starting point, but precursor exchange and atypical localization can occur. Do not interpret inhibitor data as proof of absolute separation.
  6. Cyclization always begins with diphosphate loss. Class II diterpene cyclases can initiate by protonation and retain diphosphate. Compare ent-CPP formation with the subsequent class I step.
  7. One synthase gives exactly one product. Some synthases produce characteristic mixtures because of alternative reaction trajectories. Compare product ratios rather than equating product count with gene count.
  8. All small terpenes are emitted immediately. Some accumulate in glands or as glycosides. Distinguish stored pools, induced synthesis and emission rates.
  9. All resin is a single diterpene. Oleoresin contains both volatile and less volatile constituents. Diterpenoid resin acids are one part of the mixture.
  10. Squalene is a sterol. Squalene is an acyclic triterpene precursor; epoxidation, cyclization and further modifications produce sterols.
  11. A saponin is simply a sapogenin. A saponin includes linked sugar residues, whereas the sapogenin is the nonsugar portion. Account for polarity and molar mass differences.
  12. All carotenoids are xanthophylls. Carotenes are hydrocarbons; xanthophylls are oxygenated carotenoids. Beta-carotene and zeaxanthin belong to different subgroups.
  13. Zeaxanthin concentration equals NPQ. NPQ reflects multiple processes and depends on the pigment-protein system and proton gradient. Use fluorescence and pigment measurements together.
  14. Chlorophyll and carotenoids are interchangeable pigments. They differ in structure and roles; carotenoids transfer excitation and protect against excess energy, while chlorophyll performs central photosynthetic photochemistry.
  15. Rubber is made by adding free isoprene. Plant rubber synthesis uses activated IPP and an allylic starter in a cis-prenyltransferase-related system.
  16. More metabolite always means faster synthesis. Pool size also depends on removal and storage. A pulse-labeling or time-course experiment can help distinguish flux from concentration.
  17. Attraction proves ecological benefit. Attraction of a natural enemy is one causal step. Reduced herbivory or improved plant performance must be tested separately.

11. Original examination practice

Part B — Foundational concepts

B1. The ordinary activated five-carbon extender of an allylic prenyl diphosphate is:

  1. Isoprene
  2. IPP
  3. Squalene
  4. Phytoene
Answer and explanation

B. IPP — IPP contributes five carbons in chain elongation. DMAPP serves as a common allylic starter; free isoprene is not the ordinary extender.

B2. Which precursor directly supplies many monoterpene synthases?

  1. GPP
  2. GGPP
  3. Oxidosqualene
  4. Phytoene
Answer and explanation

A. GPP — GPP is C10. GGPP is C20 and oxidosqualene and phytoene belong to longer branches.

B3. FPP contains how many carbon atoms?

  1. 5
  2. 10
  3. 15
  4. 20
Answer and explanation

C. 15 — FPP contains three C5 equivalents, one DMAPP and two IPP in the common head-to-tail accounting.

B4. Which reaction forms squalene?

  1. GPP hydrolysis
  2. Two GGPP condensation
  3. IPP isomerization
  4. Reductive coupling of two FPP
Answer and explanation

D. Reductive coupling of two FPP — Squalene is C30 and is formed from two FPP molecules. Two GGPP supply phytoene instead.

B5. The immediate substrate of many plant oxidosqualene cyclases is:

  1. 2,3-oxidosqualene
  2. Lycopene
  3. Menthol
  4. DMAPP
Answer and explanation

A. 2,3-oxidosqualene — Squalene epoxidation generates the cyclase substrate. Lycopene is a carotenoid precursor.

B6. A xanthophyll is distinguished from a carotene by:

  1. A nitrogen-containing ring
  2. Oxygen-containing functionality
  3. A C15 scaffold
  4. A glycosidic bond in every case
Answer and explanation

B. Oxygen-containing functionality — Xanthophylls contain oxygen, whereas carotenes are hydrocarbons. Sugar and nitrogen are not defining features.

B7. Which molecule is an early cyclic intermediate of many plant sterols?

  1. Farnesene
  2. Zeaxanthin
  3. Cycloartenol
  4. Limonene
Answer and explanation

C. Cycloartenol — Cycloartenol is a major plant sterol-pathway intermediate, derived from oxidosqualene.

B8. The nonsugar portion of a saponin is termed:

  1. Chromophore
  2. Prenyl donor
  3. Glycone
  4. Sapogenin
Answer and explanation

D. Sapogenin — Sapogenin is the aglycone; the sugar portion is the glycone.

B9. Class II diterpene cyclization typically initiates by:

  1. Protonation of a double bond
  2. Removal of every methyl group
  3. Oxidative cleavage of carotenoid
  4. Transfer of glucose
Answer and explanation

A. Protonation of a double bond — Protonation can initiate ring formation without immediate diphosphate departure.

B10. Menthol is best described as:

  1. A tetraterpene
  2. An oxygenated monoterpenoid
  3. A sterol
  4. A polyterpene
Answer and explanation

B. An oxygenated monoterpenoid — Menthol has a C10 scaffold and a hydroxyl group.

B11. The enzyme forming phytoene uses:

  1. Two FPP
  2. Two GPP
  3. Two GGPP
  4. One squalene
Answer and explanation

C. Two GGPP — The combination of two C20 GGPP molecules creates the C40 carotenoid precursor.

B12. Which compound commonly belongs to the C15 class?

  1. Limonene
  2. Isoprene
  3. Ent-kaurene
  4. Caryophyllene
Answer and explanation

D. Caryophyllene — Caryophyllene is a sesquiterpene. Limonene is C10 and ent-kaurene is C20.

B13. Natural rubber is predominantly:

  1. Cis-1,4-polyisoprene
  2. Trans-lignin
  3. Cellulose acetate
  4. Polymerized carotene
Answer and explanation

A. Cis-1,4-polyisoprene — Natural rubber contains long chains of cis-1,4-polyisoprene rather than carbohydrate or phenylpropanoid polymer.

B14. Violaxanthin de-epoxidase promotes the formation of:

  1. Squalene
  2. Zeaxanthin via antheraxanthin
  3. Lutein directly from alpha-carotene
  4. IPP from DMAPP
Answer and explanation

B. Zeaxanthin via antheraxanthin — It removes epoxide groups successively under suitable high-light conditions.

B15. A typical plant source of a C5 volatile is:

  1. Phytoene synthase
  2. Cycloartenol synthase
  3. Isoprene synthase acting on DMAPP
  4. Squalene epoxidase
Answer and explanation

C. Isoprene synthase acting on DMAPP — Isoprene synthase releases C5 isoprene from DMAPP.

B16. The typical pathway supplying plastidial carotenoid precursors is:

  1. Phenylpropanoid
  2. Fatty acid elongation
  3. Urea cycle
  4. MEP
Answer and explanation

D. MEP — The MEP pathway operates in plastids; contributions from other compartments are not universally excluded.

B17. Which class has a conventional C25 scaffold?

  1. Sesterterpenes
  2. Sesquiterpenes
  3. Triterpenes
  4. Tetraterpenes
Answer and explanation

A. Sesterterpenes — Sesterterpenes represent five C5 equivalents.

B18. Which compound is a major precursor of brassinosteroids?

  1. Limonene
  2. Campesterol
  3. Phytoene
  4. Farnesene
Answer and explanation

B. Campesterol — Campesterol connects plant sterol metabolism to brassinosteroid biosynthesis.

B19. What is a typical role of carotenoids in photosynthetic complexes?

  1. Direct synthesis of ATP from ADP
  2. Reduction of nitrate to nitrite
  3. Light harvesting and photoprotection
  4. Polymerization of cellulose
Answer and explanation

C. Light harvesting and photoprotection — Carotenoids participate in energy transfer and protection from potentially damaging excited states.

B20. Which statement about oleoresin is most accurate?

  1. It contains only free IPP
  2. It is pure cis-polyisoprene
  3. It consists solely of carotenoids
  4. It is a mixture including volatile terpenes and resin acids
Answer and explanation

D. It is a mixture including volatile terpenes and resin acids — The volatile fraction and less volatile resin acids jointly determine many physical properties.

Part C — Analytical reasoning

C1. A preparation has 4 μmol DMAPP and 10 μmol IPP with no interconversion. Maximum FPP yield?

  1. 3 μmol
  2. 4 μmol
  3. 5 μmol
  4. 7 μmol
Answer and explanation

B. 4 μmol — Each FPP needs one DMAPP and two IPP. DMAPP limits yield to four; two IPP remain.

C2. A C30 aglycone receives two C6 sugars without carbon loss. What is the total carbon count and class?

  1. 42; triterpenoid glycoside
  2. 42; tetraterpene
  3. 30; polyterpene
  4. 12; monoterpene
Answer and explanation

A. 42; triterpenoid glycoside — 30 + 12 = 42. The scaffold, not total carbon count, defines triterpenoid ancestry.

C3. A mutant accumulates phytoene and loses downstream colored carotenes. Most direct candidate defect?

  1. Squalene synthase
  2. Isoprene synthase
  3. Phytoene desaturation or subsequent early conversion
  4. Rubber transferase
Answer and explanation

C. Phytoene desaturation or subsequent early conversion — Accumulation of phytoene demonstrates the entry reaction still occurs; a downstream block is consistent with this profile.

C4. A floral line emits 2 nmol A and 8 nmol B per hour; control emits 8 A and 2 B. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Total emission falls fourfold
  2. Both compounds increase
  3. The receptor response must be identical
  4. Total emission stays constant but blend composition changes
Answer and explanation

D. Total emission stays constant but blend composition changes — Both totals are 10. The A:B ratio shifts from 4 to 0.25; behavior requires a separate assay.

C5. A lesion abolishes the class II ent-CPP-forming cyclase. Which immediate change is predicted absent alternative activity?

  1. Reduced ent-CPP
  2. Increased ent-kaurene without precursor
  3. Increased squalene obligatorily
  4. Direct loss of every carotenoid
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced ent-CPP — The missing enzyme directly forms ent-CPP. Downstream kaurene may fall, but unrelated branches are not necessarily lost.

C6. Control Fm=800, illuminated Fm′=400. Mutant Fm=800, Fm′=640. Compare NPQ.

  1. Control 2; mutant 4
  2. Control 1; mutant 0.25
  3. Control 0.5; mutant 0.8
  4. Both 1
Answer and explanation

B. Control 1; mutant 0.25 — NPQ=(Fm−Fm′)/Fm′. Control 400/400=1; mutant 160/640=0.25.

C7. A precursor pool increases after blocking one TPS. Why is increased sterol production not guaranteed?

  1. FPP cannot contribute to sterols
  2. Sterols are always synthesized only from GGPP
  3. Downstream capacity, compartmentation and feedback may limit diversion
  4. All spare FPP evaporates
Answer and explanation

C. Downstream capacity, compartmentation and feedback may limit diversion — A shared precursor does not determine branch flux without downstream kinetic and regulatory information.

C8. A synthase forms both GPP- and FPP-derived products in vitro. What best establishes its principal in vivo role?

  1. Its name alone
  2. Only molecular mass
  3. Only the number of amino acids
  4. Native localization, substrate availability, genetics and metabolite profiles
Answer and explanation

D. Native localization, substrate availability, genetics and metabolite profiles — Catalytic potential in vitro is insufficient to show the dominant native substrate.

C9. V, A and Z concentrations are 40, 20 and 40 nmol/g. Using (Z+0.5A)/(V+A+Z), find de-epoxidation state.

  1. 0.50
  2. 0.40
  3. 0.60
  4. 1.00
Answer and explanation

A. 0.50 — (40+10)/100=0.50. Antheraxanthin contributes half under the stated index.

C10. A leaf contains 50 nmol product at 0.5 g; another contains 60 nmol at 1 g. Compare concentration.

  1. Second is 120% of first
  2. Second is 60% of first
  3. Both are 100 nmol/g
  4. Second is 30% of first
Answer and explanation

B. Second is 60% of first — First=100 nmol/g; second=60 nmol/g. Total amount rose but concentration fell.

C11. A mutant lacking a glycosyltransferase accumulates aglycone and loses glycoside. Which inference is most parsimonious?

  1. Scaffold formation is absent
  2. All precursor synthesis stops
  3. Late glycosylation is impaired
  4. The aglycone must be a carotenoid
Answer and explanation

C. Late glycosylation is impaired — The scaffold still appears; the conversion to its sugar-bearing derivative is impaired.

C12. A volatile peak appears immediately after gland rupture without increased transcript. Best explanation to test?

  1. Only new transcription
  2. All synthases instantly double
  3. No volatile was stored
  4. Release of a pre-existing storage pool
Answer and explanation

D. Release of a pre-existing storage pool — Physical rupture can release earlier synthesized compounds. Later synthesis may still contribute.

C13. A hypothetical C15 starter is extended by 100 IPP molecules. Carbon count?

  1. 515
  2. 500
  3. 115
  4. 1500
Answer and explanation

A. 515 — 15+100×5=515 carbon atoms, ignoring losses.

C14. A mutant has normal xanthophyll conversion but diminished rapid NPQ. Which defect is plausible?

  1. Mandatory loss of phytoene synthase
  2. Altered coupling of lumen acidification to antenna regulation
  3. Absence of all carotenoids
  4. Loss of natural rubber
Answer and explanation

B. Altered coupling of lumen acidification to antenna regulation — Regulatory proteins such as PsbS can affect rapid NPQ even when xanthophyll conversion persists.

C15. In a simple model v=Vmax S/(Km+S), Vmax=12 and Km=3 μM. At S=3 μM the rate is:

  1. 3
  2. 12
  3. 6
  4. 9
Answer and explanation

C. 6 — At S=Km, v=Vmax/2=6 in the corresponding rate units.

C16. A plant's saponin-deficient roots are more susceptible to fungus. Which test most strengthens causal attribution?

  1. One photograph of roots
  2. Only counting leaves
  3. Only sequencing an unrelated gene
  4. Independent mutants, chemical profiling and genetic complementation
Answer and explanation

D. Independent mutants, chemical profiling and genetic complementation — These tests link the specific genetic change, loss of metabolite and susceptibility while helping rule out unrelated mutations.

C17. A compound has C10H16. Its hydrocarbon degree of unsaturation is:

  1. 3
  2. 2
  3. 1
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

A. 3 — (2×10+2−16)/2=3. Rings and double bonds both contribute.

C18. Two samples have identical carotenoid peak areas but different recovery efficiencies, 50% and 100%. Other conditions matched. Original amounts?

  1. Equal
  2. First is twice second
  3. Second is twice first
  4. First is half second
Answer and explanation

B. First is twice second — Correcting by recovery divides the detected amount by 0.5 or 1 respectively.

C19. A line has fewer rubber particles but unchanged average chain length. What can be concluded?

  1. Cis geometry became trans
  2. Elongation necessarily ceased
  3. Particle number or initiation may contribute; chain length alone does not explain yield
  4. All IPP is absent
Answer and explanation

C. Particle number or initiation may contribute; chain length alone does not explain yield — Particle number and chain length are distinct determinants of polymer accumulation; further tests are needed.

C20. An isotope pulse produces more product labeling in treatment but also doubles precursor enrichment. Strongest conclusion?

  1. Synthesis necessarily doubled
  2. Degradation stopped
  3. The precursor is unrelated
  4. Normalize precursor enrichment before inferring flux
Answer and explanation

D. Normalize precursor enrichment before inferring flux — Label incorporation depends on enrichment and flux. The uncorrected product label does not isolate a rate change.

12. Revision and mastery

Terpenoids derive from activated C5 precursors. Carbon-chain assembly, scaffold-forming enzymes, tailoring reactions and tissue organization together create chemically diverse products. The key pathway branches are GPP to many C10 products, FPP to many C15 products and squalene, GGPP to many C20 products and phytoene, and repeated IPP extension to long polyisoprenoids. Sterols support membrane organization, saponins can provide specialized defense, carotenoids support light harvesting and photoprotection, and volatile products participate in ecological interactions.

Quantitative relationships

  • Carbon accounting: 1 DMAPP + 1, 2 or 3 IPP gives GPP, FPP or GGPP respectively.
  • Two FPP per squalene; two GGPP per phytoene.
  • NPQ = (Fm − Fm′)/Fm′; de-epoxidation state = (Z + 0.5A)/(V + A + Z).
  • Hydrocarbon unsaturation = (2C + 2 − H)/2.
  • Approximate average rubber repeat number = number-average molar mass / 68.12 g mol⁻¹.
  • Simplified steady-state pool = synthesis rate / first-order loss constant.
