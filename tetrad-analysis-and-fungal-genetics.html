Why study all four products of meiosis?

A conventional testcross reveals the genetic constitution of one gamete through the phenotype of one offspring. If a thousand offspring are scored, the geneticist can estimate the proportion of parental and recombinant gametes. What cannot normally be reconstructed is which four gametes originated in the same meiotic division. That lost association matters because recombination is an event involving chromatids in one cell, not an event occurring separately in each gamete. The complete set of meiotic products is therefore an especially informative unit of observation. In certain fungi, the products remain physically associated in a structure that allows them to be examined as a family.

A tetrad is the set of four haploid nuclear products derived from one meiosis. An ascus is a sac-like structure characteristic of sexual reproduction in ascomycete fungi, in which ascospores develop. In some organisms each of the four nuclei becomes a spore; in others an additional mitotic division increases the number of nuclei to eight. Such an octad still represents one meiosis. Counting eight spores as eight independent meiotic products would exaggerate the amount of independent evidence. Under ordinary postmeiotic mitosis, each of the four original products gives rise to two genetically matching descendants.

Consider a diploid heterozygous for a marker A/a. DNA replication before meiosis produces two sister chromatids with A and two with a, assuming no subsequent change in sequence. Homologs separate at meiosis I; sister chromatids separate at meiosis II. In a normal completed tetrad there are two nuclei with A and two with a. The distribution is a prediction about each complete meiosis, not merely an average across many randomly sampled gametes. Sampling one spore from each of ten asci could by chance yield seven A and three a, without violating segregation in any individual ascus. Finding three A and one a in one confirmed complete tetrad raises a different question about the underlying genetic process.

Haploid genetic analysis has an additional advantage: a nuclear locus is ordinarily represented by one allele rather than two. A recessive nutritional mutation that is masked by a functional copy in a diploid may directly alter the growth phenotype of a haploid spore. Nonetheless, haploidy does not guarantee an unambiguous phenotype. A mutation may depend on medium composition, a suppressor locus, or developmental state. Reliable markers must be distinguishable and scored under appropriate conditions. A genetic marker is an identifiable inherited variant used to track a chromosome region; it need not itself cause a visually obvious trait.

The standard unit of inference in this chapter will be one intact, correctly assigned ascus. First we shall learn what physical order reveals about the centromere, then what combinations of alleles reveal about relationships between two markers, and finally how an abnormal allele ratio can reveal copying and repair of DNA. These are different uses of the same family structure. They should not be reduced to a single percentage formula.

The life cycles of Neurospora and budding yeast

Neurospora crassa is a filamentous ascomycete. Its vegetative nuclear phase is haploid, although a fungal hypha can contain many nuclei and should not be equated with a single uninucleate cell. Compatible mating types permit the sexual phase. Fusion of compatible nuclei produces a diploid nucleus in the developing ascus. Meiosis of that nucleus yields four haploid nuclei. A postmeiotic mitosis produces eight nuclei that are packaged into ascospores. The long, narrow ascus generally preserves their sequence, so the order in which products are observed provides information about the two divisions. The informative order is nuclear lineage order within the ascus, not a physical sequence of DNA bases.

In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, haploid cells of compatible mating types can fuse and form a diploid. Diploid cells can reproduce mitotically and, under suitable conditions, enter meiosis and sporulation. The usual product is an ascus containing four ascospores. Their arrangement does not ordinarily provide a reproducible record of which nuclei were separated at the first division. Geneticists can nevertheless recover the four products of a single diploid meiosis and identify their marker genotypes. This is unordered tetrad analysis.

The species differ in what information is readily preserved. Neurospora provides an ordered sequence of four meiotic lineages represented by eight spores, whereas yeast provides four members of a meiotic family with no standard linear division order. Neither is intrinsically superior for all genetic questions. If the question concerns the relationship of one locus to its own centromere, an ordered ascus supplies a particularly direct observation. If the question concerns which combinations of two alleles are inherited together, unordered tetrads contain sufficient information to classify the joint distribution. A yeast centromere-linked marker can sometimes be used as a reference for indirect mapping, but this is not the direct ordered-ascus calculation.

A visible spore-colour marker offers a convenient way to illustrate ordered segregation. Suppose a brown spore allele is called A and a pale allele a. The colours in a diagram identify genotype only under a stipulated reliable marker model. In real genetic studies, pigmentation can depend on other loci or developmental conditions. Yeast studies can employ nutritional requirements or sequence markers instead. Phenotypic scoring uses growth or another observable characteristic, while genotyping examines the underlying genetic variant. Either method requires controls appropriate to the marker.

A complete asci-based dataset has advantages over a mass population of spores. The geneticist can distinguish a reciprocal recombinant pair from an unbalanced set of alleles; identify asci that show unexpected allele ratios; and determine whether an apparent deficit occurs because an entire family is missing a product. However, intact asci are a selected sample if certain genotypes reduce viability. A collection restricted to asci with all four spores growing may not represent every meiosis in the original population. Reporting the number of incomplete asci separately is therefore scientifically important.

Ordered tetrads: first- and second-division segregation

The centromere is the chromosome region associated with assembly of the kinetochore, which mediates chromosome segregation. A marker on a chromosome is physically linked to that chromosome's centromere, even if it lies far away along an arm. The linkage of a locus to its centromere can be examined by asking whether alleles became separated when homologous centromeres went to opposite poles in meiosis I, or whether their separation was postponed until chromatids separated at meiosis II.

Start with a replicated pair of homologs, each carrying two chromatids. On one homolog both chromatids carry A, while on the other both carry a. Suppose there is no exchange between the marker and centromere. At anaphase I, one daughter nucleus receives the homolog whose chromatids both carry A. The other receives the homolog whose chromatids both carry a. Subsequent separation of sisters in meiosis II produces two A nuclei adjacent to each other and two a nuclei adjacent to each other. Postmeiotic mitosis duplicates these positions, producing a sequence of four A followed by four a, or the reversed sequence. This is first-division segregation, often abbreviated FDS.

A crossover distal to the marker, on the same chromosome arm but beyond the locus, does not exchange the marker relative to its centromere. An FDS pattern therefore does not imply that no recombination happened anywhere in the chromosome. The observation concerns the genetic relationship within the interval between the marker and its own centromere. This distinction is crucial when an examination problem describes several exchanges on the same chromosome.

Now consider a single reciprocal crossover between the centromere and the marker involving one chromatid of each homolog. After exchange, each replicated homolog has one chromatid carrying A and one carrying a. Meiosis I separates homologous centromeres but does not send all copies of A to one daughter nucleus. Each daughter contains both marker alleles on separate chromatids. Only meiosis II finally places the alleles in separate nuclei. This is second-division segregation, abbreviated SDS.

Because the products remain ordered, the arrangement of chromatids at the two second-division spindles determines the observed pattern. The four meiotic products may be A a A a or A a a A, with corresponding reversed patterns. After the postmeiotic mitosis these become the 2:2:2:2 or 2:4:2 octad arrangements. Both are SDS. A student should not classify them as different numbers of crossovers merely because their colours alternate a different number of times. In the simplest model a single exchange within the interval can generate either pattern, depending on the orientation of second-division segregation.

FDS and SDS are not labels for two different kinds of organisms or two different phases at which crossing over normally occurs. The crossover generally occurs in meiotic prophase I. FDS and SDS describe the division at which the marker alleles become partitioned into separate nuclear lineages. An SDS ascus does not imply that recombination happened during the second meiotic division.

The same visible pattern can also arise after more complicated exchange histories. If two exchanges occur in the marker-centromere interval, their effect depends on the chromatids involved. A second exchange between the same pair of chromatids can restore the original marker-centromere phase. A four-strand double exchange can leave the two chromatids of each homolog carrying the same allele, but in the reversed phase. Both can look like FDS. A three-strand double exchange can leave unlike alleles associated with a centromere and produce SDS. Thus the visible order does not uniquely identify the number of molecular exchange events.

Estimating gene–centromere distance

For a sufficiently short interval, most informative recombination events can be treated as single crossovers. In an SDS ascus generated by one crossover, two of the four chromatids are recombinant with respect to marker and centromere. The other two are nonrecombinant. The contribution of such an ascus to the total recombinant chromatid fraction is therefore one half. An ordinary FDS ascus produced without an interval crossover contributes zero. When N complete ordinarily segregating asci include S SDS asci, the usual gene-centromere estimate is 100 times S divided by 2N centimorgans.

The factor of one half comes from counting chromatids, not from counting the two meiotic divisions or the eight spores. Suppose a scientist observes 70 FDS asci, 18 asci with 2:2:2:2 segregation and 12 with 2:4:2 segregation. The total is 100. The SDS count is 30, because both arrangements represent second-division segregation. In the single-exchange model each SDS ascus contributes two recombinant meiotic products. There are 60 recombinant products among 400 products, giving a fraction of 0.15 and an estimated distance of 15 cM. Duplicating the four products by mitosis would double both numerator and denominator and leave this result unchanged.

In a more realistic dataset the investigator might have 150 FDS asci, 27 alternating SDS asci, 18 symmetric SDS asci and five asci showing abnormal segregation. The five abnormal asci should not silently enter the ordinary segregation analysis. If the stated analysis excludes them, the denominator is 195 and the SDS numerator is 45. The estimate is 100 times 45 divided by 390, or approximately 11.54 cM. The conversion-like asci should also be reported as five of 200 scored asci, or 2.5 percent. This retains information about events that the mapping calculation cannot interpret under its ordinary assumptions.

A map distance in centimorgans is not a count of base pairs. The number of exchanges per physical length can vary with chromosomal location, species, genetic background and other biological factors. A short DNA segment in a region with frequent meiotic recombination may have a greater genetic length than a longer DNA segment near a region where recombination is suppressed. The mapping equation is a statistical estimate based on inherited marker configurations. It cannot determine a universal conversion between centimorgans and nucleotide length.

It is also important not to confuse the SDS percentage with the recombinant fraction of a two-marker testcross. Under a short-interval model, 40 percent SDS yields 20 cM. The raw SDS frequency is a proportion of asci; the estimated recombinant proportion uses the factor of two. Furthermore, repeated exchanges make the relationship between observed SDS and the expected number of exchanges nonlinear. If chromatid association with centromere becomes fully randomized among the six possible allocations of two A alleles to four positions, two allocations put matching alleles together at meiosis I and four put unlike alleles together. The resulting SDS proportion is two thirds under that limiting randomization model. The elementary estimate then approaches 33.3 cM despite the possibility of a much longer genetic interval. This is why a centromere distance obtained by the simple equation should not be extrapolated indefinitely.

A sample estimate carries sampling uncertainty. For example, suppose 60 of 200 independently sampled asci are SDS. The observed proportion is 0.30. In a simple binomial sampling model the standard error of the SDS proportion is the square root of 0.30 times 0.70 divided by 200, about 0.0324. The mapping estimate is 15 cM, with a corresponding standard error of about 1.62 cM. A rough normal approximation gives an interval of 15 plus or minus 3.18 cM. This quantifies sampling variation only; it does not correct systematic biases such as preferential spore death or undetected multiple crossovers.

Unordered tetrads: parental and recombinant haplotypes

When division order is unavailable, the classification of a tetrad rests on the allele combinations carried by its four products. For two loci A and B, suppose the parents contribute AB and ab. These are parental haplotypes, whereas Ab and aB are recombinant haplotypes relative to this cross. If the starting phase had been Ab/aB, the identities would reverse. Parental and recombinant are historical descriptions relative to the original parental chromosomes, not intrinsic properties of uppercase and lowercase allele symbols.

Under normal 2:2 segregation at both loci, only three classes of unordered tetrad are possible. A parental ditype, PD, consists of two AB products and two ab products. A nonparental ditype, NPD, contains two Ab products and two aB products. A tetratype, T or TT, contains one of each of the four possible haplotypes. Ditype means that two different combinations are present; tetratype means that four are present. Each class still represents four products. A tetratype is not a family of four independently sampled spores, and a ditype does not mean there were only two spores.

Why are there only three classes? Let x denote the count of AB products. The total count carrying A must be two, so the count of Ab is 2 minus x. The total count carrying B must also be two, so the count of aB is 2 minus x. The fourth count, ab, must equal x to bring the total to four. The allowed integer values of x are zero, one and two. They give NPD, T and PD, respectively. This short derivation shows that the classification follows from ordinary segregation, not a memorized list of names. A tetrad showing three A products cannot fit this proof because the first assumption fails.

The simplest linked cross yields PD when no exchange occurs between the two markers. A single crossover between the markers yields T because two non-sister chromatids become recombinant while two retain the parental combinations. A four-strand double crossover can generate NPD, with all four products recombinant. Two-strand double crossovers can restore both parental combinations and generate PD. Three-strand double crossovers can yield T. These examples make clear that tetrad classes describe outcomes, not unique crossover histories.

Two unlinked genes can also produce PD, NPD or T. If neither gene recombines relative to its centromere, the first meiotic spindle can orient the two homolog pairs in matching or opposite directions. Under fair independent orientation, PD and NPD should be equally frequent. If at least one locus undergoes SDS, tetratypes may also appear, with the exact frequencies depending on the locus-centromere relationships and chromatid behavior. Therefore the universal test for independent assortment is not an assumed 1:1:4 ratio. A 1:1:4 distribution can arise under particular randomization assumptions, but equality of PD and NPD is the relevant symmetry for the standard unlinked model.

When two genes are linked, parental combinations tend to be recovered together more often than entirely recombinant ditypes. An excess of PD over NPD supports linkage under unbiased recovery and accurate classification. Equality of PD and NPD, on the other hand, provides no strong evidence for linkage in the sample; it does not establish that loci are physically on separate chromosomes. Distant markers on the same chromosome can be genetically indistinguishable from unlinked loci. Sample size is another limitation: six PD and four NPD might reflect sampling variation, while sixty PD and four NPD offer a much stronger departure from the equal-frequency expectation.

Recombination fraction and Perkins mapping

The recombinant fraction is obtained by counting recombinant chromatids. In a PD tetrad, none of the four is recombinant. In a T tetrad, two are recombinant. In an NPD tetrad, all four are recombinant. With P parental ditypes, N nonparental ditypes and T tetratypes, the total number of products is four times P plus N plus T. The recombinant count is two times T plus four times N. Dividing gives r = (T/2 + N)/(P + N + T). Multiplying by 100 expresses this endpoint recombinant fraction as a percentage.

The Perkins mapping estimate addresses a different question: approximately how many exchange events are represented by the tetrad classes? Consider an interval with negligible triple and higher exchanges and no chromatid interference. After a first crossover between two non-sister chromatids, a second may use the same two chromatids, share one chromatid with the first exchange, or involve the other two chromatids. There are four equally likely pair choices under the no-chromatid-interference model. One gives the two-strand class, two give the three-strand class and one gives the four-strand class. The expected ratio is therefore 1:2:1.

Let D denote the total number of double-crossover tetrads and S the number of single-crossover tetrads. Four-strand doubles constitute one quarter of D and produce NPD for linked markers under this model. Hence D is estimated as four times N. Three-strand doubles constitute one half of D and contribute two times N to the observed T count. The estimated number of single-crossover tetrads is consequently T minus two times N. The estimated total number of exchanges is S plus twice D, which reduces to T plus six times N. Each exchange involves two chromatids out of four, giving a map-distance estimate of 50 times (T + 6N)/(P + N + T), in centimorgans.

The formula is an estimate, not an exact reconstruction of every meiosis. Its derivation assumes linked loci, the expected double-exchange chromatid ratio, and sufficiently low frequencies of higher exchange classes. If T is smaller than twice N, the simplified reconstruction would require a negative number of single-crossover tetrads. That result should prompt examination of sampling variation, interference, high recombination, marker phase or the suitability of the model, rather than literal interpretation. Crossover interference changes the spacing of events along chromosomes; chromatid interference instead concerns which chromatids participate in successive events. They are not interchangeable concepts.

Consider an illustrative dataset with P = 130, N = 10 and T = 60. There are 200 tetrads. The endpoint recombinant fraction is (30 + 10)/200 = 0.20, or 20 percent. The Perkins estimate is 50(60 + 60)/200 = 30 cM. The difference arises because endpoints can hide exchanges, while the model uses NPD to estimate a contribution from otherwise hidden double exchanges. Reporting 30 percent recombinant gametes would be incorrect: the observed recombinant fraction is 20 percent.

For a second dataset, P = 160, T = 40 and N = 0. The endpoint recombinant fraction and the Perkins distance have the same numerical magnitude, 10 percent and 10 cM respectively. No NPD was observed, so the correction based on four-strand doubles vanishes. This does not prove that no double crossover occurred; two-strand and three-strand doubles could have occurred even if no four-strand class happened to be sampled. The absence of a rare class is weaker evidence when the sample is small.

The use of tetrad classes also illustrates why the four products cannot be treated as four independent observations for every statistical test. They are constrained by segregation within a meiosis. A simple PD versus NPD linkage test, conditional on the number of ditypes, compares ditype counts under equal probabilities in the unlinked model. For example, if there are 80 PD and 20 NPD, the null expectation is 50 in each class. The chi-square statistic is (80−50)²/50 + (20−50)²/50 = 36, with one degree of freedom under the usual large-sample assumptions. Tetratypes do not enter this conditional symmetry test, though they remain essential to mapping.

Gene conversion and postmeiotic segregation

Ordinary reciprocal crossing over rearranges allele associations but preserves the total of each allele across the four products. Gene conversion changes this expectation. In a heterozygote, one allele sequence can be copied into the DNA molecule originally carrying the other allele, producing nonreciprocal genetic transmission. A conventional complete tetrad may then contain three products of one allele and one of the other instead of two and two. In Neurospora, postmeiotic mitosis can turn the 3:1 nuclear ratio into a 6:2 spore ratio.

To understand why this happens, return briefly to homologous recombination. Repair of a programmed meiotic DNA break can involve pairing a DNA strand from one homolog with a complementary strand from the other. Where the parental sequences differ, the resulting heteroduplex DNA can contain a mismatch. Repair of that mismatch can restore the original sequence or change it to the sequence carried by the other homolog. The genetic outcome depends on the direction of correction and the position of the affected sequence. DNA synthesis during repair can also copy information from a homologous template. Gene conversion can accompany either a crossover or a noncrossover outcome; the two terms describe distinct aspects of recombination.

Imagine that A and a differ at one nucleotide. In an ordinary meiosis there are two A-bearing chromatids and two a-bearing chromatids. If sequence repair converts one A-bearing chromatid to a before the formation of meiotic products, the resulting genotypes are A, a, a, a. After ordinary mitotic duplication of the four nuclei, the octad contains two A spores and six a spores. The reverse direction gives six A and two a. No additional chromosome needs to be created, and the allele whose count increases need not confer greater fitness.

A mismatch may persist in a heteroduplex until the next DNA replication. The two strands of that DNA duplex then act as templates for different daughter duplexes. One daughter inherits the A sequence and the other the a sequence. This produces postmeiotic segregation, which can appear as a 5:3 or 3:5 octad. For example, suppose two of the other three meiotic lineages contain A and the third contains a, while the fourth is heteroduplex. The three ordinarily segregating lineages give four A and two a spores. The heteroduplex lineage supplies one of each, yielding five A and three a in total. The critical observation is not simply an unusual count, but a discordant pair among descendants of one meiotic product.

An octad with four A and four a can also be abnormal if the order shows that postmeiotic sister pairs are not genetically identical. Reciprocal changes, more than one heteroduplex, or other complex events may preserve total allele numbers while disrupting expected pairing. Consequently total counts alone can conceal some recombination-associated alterations. Ordered data should retain both the counts and their positions.

Gene conversion is neither necessarily a de novo mutation nor synonymous with meiotic drive. A newly arising mutation can alter a marker but does not require copying an existing allelic sequence. Meiotic drive changes transmission probabilities, sometimes through the survival or recovery of particular products. A conversion hypothesis is strengthened when the altered sequence matches the corresponding homolog and when surrounding markers support a recombination-associated event. Spore lethality, an incorrect genotype call or accidental mixing of spores can generate misleading observed ratios, so these alternatives must be addressed before assigning a molecular mechanism.

The landmark value of unusual segregation patterns was that they transformed apparent exceptions to Mendelian ratios into evidence about the molecular processes of genetic exchange. Classical tetrad studies inferred nonreciprocal transmission long before routine genome-wide sequencing. Modern genotyping of intact meiotic families can reveal short conversion tracts surrounded by ordinarily segregating markers. Such observations demonstrate why a chromosome can retain its broad parental association while a short interval has acquired sequence information from its homolog.

Experimental interpretation and controls

A defensible tetrad experiment begins with a clearly stated cross and reliable marker phase. The researcher must know which alleles were originally present together, because PD and NPD labels cannot be assigned from the appearance of the alleles alone. An incorrect phase swaps the two ditype categories, potentially turning apparent linkage into apparent excess of recombinant ditypes. Marker genotypes are checked in the parental strains and in the heterozygous diploid where appropriate.

The sample should preserve the relationship among spores from one ascus. If spores from two asci are accidentally combined, an apparent 3:1 marker ratio might simply be a sampling artifact. A count of complete, incomplete and unscoreable asci makes the analysis transparent. A missing product cannot be treated as a known genotype without independent evidence. Where a nutritional marker is scored by growth, a general viability control distinguishes inability to grow under the selective condition from failure to germinate at all. Positive and negative genotype controls help test whether a scoring method actually discriminates the alleles.

Consider two hypothetical yeast crosses. In cross X, 90 percent of all asci yield four viable products, and an unusually high NPD fraction is observed among them. In cross Y, only 30 percent yield four products, and the NPD fraction among complete asci is low. The difference could reflect real recombination variation, but it could also reflect preferential loss of certain classes in cross Y. A meaningful interpretation requires the distribution of viability classes and, if possible, genotype information for incomplete families. Selection on products can change the observed distribution of the retained tetrads.

Research studies can compare conversion frequencies and crossover distances in different intervals of a chromosome using distinguishable markers. A conversion at one marker is a local observation; a crossover identified by changed flanking haplotypes is an interval observation. The two observations can coincide without being identical. A study of the left arm of yeast chromosome VII used marker-defined intervals and distinguished ordinary tetrads from those affected by conversion or postmeiotic segregation in calculating map distances. This provides an authentic example of how the denominator is matched to the assumptions of the calculation.

Modern molecular markers do not invalidate classical tetrad logic. Sequencing adds information about the exact sequence changes and the positions of recombination boundaries, but the classification of complete meiotic families still constrains the interpretation. A reciprocal change in the associations of flanking markers supports exchange; a short nonreciprocal tract supports conversion; a complex pattern may require more than a single event. The next chapter will generalize marker-based reasoning beyond intact fungal asci.
