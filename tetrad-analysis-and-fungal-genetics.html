Why study all four products of meiosis?

A conventional testcross reveals the genetic constitution of one gamete through the phenotype of one offspring. If a thousand offspring are scored, the geneticist can estimate the proportion of parental and recombinant gametes. What cannot normally be reconstructed is which four gametes originated in the same meiotic division. That lost association matters because recombination is an event involving chromatids in one cell, not an event occurring separately in each gamete. The complete set of meiotic products is therefore an especially informative unit of observation. In certain fungi, the products remain physically associated in a structure that allows them to be examined as a family.

A tetrad is the set of four haploid nuclear products derived from one meiosis. An ascus is a sac-like structure characteristic of sexual reproduction in ascomycete fungi, in which ascospores develop. In some organisms each of the four nuclei becomes a spore; in others an additional mitotic division increases the number of nuclei to eight. Such an octad still represents one meiosis. Counting eight spores as eight independent meiotic products would exaggerate the amount of independent evidence. Under ordinary postmeiotic mitosis, each of the four original products gives rise to two genetically matching descendants.

Consider a diploid heterozygous for a marker A/a. DNA replication before meiosis produces two sister chromatids with A and two with a, assuming no subsequent change in sequence. Homologs separate at meiosis I; sister chromatids separate at meiosis II. In a normal completed tetrad there are two nuclei with A and two with a. The distribution is a prediction about each complete meiosis, not merely an average across many randomly sampled gametes. Sampling one spore from each of ten asci could by chance yield seven A and three a, without violating segregation in any individual ascus. Finding three A and one a in one confirmed complete tetrad raises a different question about the underlying genetic process.

Haploid genetic analysis has an additional advantage: a nuclear locus is ordinarily represented by one allele rather than two. A recessive nutritional mutation that is masked by a functional copy in a diploid may directly alter the growth phenotype of a haploid spore. Nonetheless, haploidy does not guarantee an unambiguous phenotype. A mutation may depend on medium composition, a suppressor locus, or developmental state. Reliable markers must be distinguishable and scored under appropriate conditions. A genetic marker is an identifiable inherited variant used to track a chromosome region; it need not itself cause a visually obvious trait.

The standard unit of inference in this chapter will be one intact, correctly assigned ascus. First we shall learn what physical order reveals about the centromere, then what combinations of alleles reveal about relationships between two markers, and finally how an abnormal allele ratio can reveal copying and repair of DNA. These are different uses of the same family structure. They should not be reduced to a single percentage formula.

The life cycles of Neurospora and budding yeast

Neurospora crassa is a filamentous ascomycete. Its vegetative nuclear phase is haploid, although a fungal hypha can contain many nuclei and should not be equated with a single uninucleate cell. Compatible mating types permit the sexual phase. Fusion of compatible nuclei produces a diploid nucleus in the developing ascus. Meiosis of that nucleus yields four haploid nuclei. A postmeiotic mitosis produces eight nuclei that are packaged into ascospores. The long, narrow ascus generally preserves their sequence, so the order in which products are observed provides information about the two divisions. The informative order is nuclear lineage order within the ascus, not a physical sequence of DNA bases.

In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, haploid cells of compatible mating types can fuse and form a diploid. Diploid cells can reproduce mitotically and, under suitable conditions, enter meiosis and sporulation. The usual product is an ascus containing four ascospores. Their arrangement does not ordinarily provide a reproducible record of which nuclei were separated at the first division. Geneticists can nevertheless recover the four products of a single diploid meiosis and identify their marker genotypes. This is unordered tetrad analysis.

The species differ in what information is readily preserved. Neurospora provides an ordered sequence of four meiotic lineages represented by eight spores, whereas yeast provides four members of a meiotic family with no standard linear division order. Neither is intrinsically superior for all genetic questions. If the question concerns the relationship of one locus to its own centromere, an ordered ascus supplies a particularly direct observation. If the question concerns which combinations of two alleles are inherited together, unordered tetrads contain sufficient information to classify the joint distribution. A yeast centromere-linked marker can sometimes be used as a reference for indirect mapping, but this is not the direct ordered-ascus calculation.

A visible spore-colour marker offers a convenient way to illustrate ordered segregation. Suppose a brown spore allele is called A and a pale allele a. The colours in a diagram identify genotype only under a stipulated reliable marker model. In real genetic studies, pigmentation can depend on other loci or developmental conditions. Yeast studies can employ nutritional requirements or sequence markers instead. Phenotypic scoring uses growth or another observable characteristic, while genotyping examines the underlying genetic variant. Either method requires controls appropriate to the marker.

A complete asci-based dataset has advantages over a mass population of spores. The geneticist can distinguish a reciprocal recombinant pair from an unbalanced set of alleles; identify asci that show unexpected allele ratios; and determine whether an apparent deficit occurs because an entire family is missing a product. However, intact asci are a selected sample if certain genotypes reduce viability. A collection restricted to asci with all four spores growing may not represent every meiosis in the original population. Reporting the number of incomplete asci separately is therefore scientifically important.

From parental strains to interpretable meiotic families

A tetrad analysis is only as informative as the cross that produces the tetrads. To see why, compare two parents with the same marker phenotype. Even if a crossover occurs between their chromosomes, it may be impossible to recognize that exchange when neither marker varies between them. Genetic mapping requires a heterozygous locus or interval in which inherited variants distinguish the two parental homologs. Heterozygosity is an informational requirement, not a requirement that every gene differ between the parents. Ideally, markers are selected so that their states can be independently identified in each haploid product.

The common notation A and a is convenient but potentially misleading. In a conventional dominant–recessive cross, A might be a dominant allele, while a is a recessive allele. In tetrad analysis we often use the same lettering simply to distinguish two marker states. The two states could be neutral nucleotide variants, alleles that determine nutrient requirements, or different colours in a reliably scored spore. Neither uppercase lettering nor the adjective parental implies an advantage in survival. The same locus can be scored as different states in different crosses, and the resulting genetic map depends on the chromosomal interval rather than on the typography of the allele.

Imagine a yeast cross in which one parent carries a functional enzyme allele at the first marker but requires an amino acid supplement because of a mutation at the second marker. The other parent has the opposite combination. We can label the parents Ab and aB. In the diploid, the functional copies may mask both defects. After meiosis each haploid product has only one of the two variants at each locus, so four combinations are possible: AB can perform both functions, Ab retains only the first, aB retains only the second and ab lacks both. The parental class is Ab and aB, not AB and ab. If an investigator erroneously classifies AB as parental because both alleles are functional, every PD and NPD count will be reversed.

When scoring such a cross using phenotypes, one should distinguish a product that is viable but fails to grow under a marker-specific condition from one that cannot form a colony at all. For example, a dead product does not tell us whether it carried A or a. If a nutritional mutation reduces the probability of initial germination under all conditions used in the experiment, the products carrying that mutation may be underrepresented even before classification. The logically relevant comparison is between growth under a condition permissive for all marker genotypes and growth under a condition that discriminates a marker. The former answers whether the product can be assessed; the latter helps determine the allele it carries.

A single scored phenotype may also represent more than one genotype. Suppose production of a pigment requires functional copies of both A and B. AB spores are coloured while Ab, aB and ab spores are pale. The observation of one coloured and three pale spores is consistent with a tetratype, but only if the cross and ordinary segregation assumptions are already known. Observing no coloured spores is consistent with a ditype containing only Ab and aB. Without independent marker scoring, the pale phenotypes do not individually reveal whether the spores carry Ab, aB or ab. A different gene interacting with the pigment pathway could further complicate the inference. Mapping experiments should therefore distinguish the underlying genotype categories rather than relying uncritically on aggregate phenotype classes.

The same logic extends to sequence-based assays. Suppose a sequence marker produces two different allele-specific signals. A reliable assay should distinguish the two known parental states and identify ambiguous or failed calls rather than forcing every sample into one class. An ambiguous result is not automatically an additional allele. In a haploid sample, a mixed signal may indicate mixed material, contamination, a copy-number change or heterogeneity within the descendants of a meiotic product. A postmeiotic segregation hypothesis becomes compelling only after such alternatives have been considered.

Several units of observation may coexist in one dataset. The parent is the source of an allele phase. The ascus identifies a single meiosis. The spore identifies a particular product or descendant of that meiosis. A colony growing from a spore provides material to phenotype or genotype that product. A repeated assay on the same colony is a technical replicate, not another meiosis. These distinctions determine the meaning of a sample size. If 25 asci each supply four spores and each spore is assayed twice, there are 200 assay measurements, 100 spore products and only 25 meiotic families. Which denominator is appropriate depends on the scientific question.

A further distinction concerns the experimental population. Suppose the experiment compares a standard strain with a strain carrying a variant that influences meiosis. Many colonies recovered from the same diploid culture may share environmental or developmental conditions. Repeating the cross in distinct biological samples can reveal whether an apparent recombination difference is reproducible rather than peculiar to one preparation. Within each replicate, classification should follow the same criteria and the number of excluded families should be reported. Pooling data is defensible only when the conditions and relevant marker phase are comparable.

The useful conclusion is that a genetic map is not generated by counting colours alone. It is the result of a chain of evidence linking parental phase, meiotic family identity, product genotype, classification and the assumptions of an estimator. If one link is missing, a numerical answer can still be calculated but its biological meaning may be doubtful. Examination questions often deliberately remove one of these links. A careful student should identify the missing assumption before selecting a formula.

Ordered tetrads: first- and second-division segregation

The centromere is the chromosome region associated with assembly of the kinetochore, which mediates chromosome segregation. A marker on a chromosome is physically linked to that chromosome's centromere, even if it lies far away along an arm. The linkage of a locus to its centromere can be examined by asking whether alleles became separated when homologous centromeres went to opposite poles in meiosis I, or whether their separation was postponed until chromatids separated at meiosis II.

Start with a replicated pair of homologs, each carrying two chromatids. On one homolog both chromatids carry A, while on the other both carry a. Suppose there is no exchange between the marker and centromere. At anaphase I, one daughter nucleus receives the homolog whose chromatids both carry A. The other receives the homolog whose chromatids both carry a. Subsequent separation of sisters in meiosis II produces two A nuclei adjacent to each other and two a nuclei adjacent to each other. Postmeiotic mitosis duplicates these positions, producing a sequence of four A followed by four a, or the reversed sequence. This is first-division segregation, often abbreviated FDS.

A crossover distal to the marker, on the same chromosome arm but beyond the locus, does not exchange the marker relative to its centromere. An FDS pattern therefore does not imply that no recombination happened anywhere in the chromosome. The observation concerns the genetic relationship within the interval between the marker and its own centromere. This distinction is crucial when an examination problem describes several exchanges on the same chromosome.

Now consider a single reciprocal crossover between the centromere and the marker involving one chromatid of each homolog. After exchange, each replicated homolog has one chromatid carrying A and one carrying a. Meiosis I separates homologous centromeres but does not send all copies of A to one daughter nucleus. Each daughter contains both marker alleles on separate chromatids. Only meiosis II finally places the alleles in separate nuclei. This is second-division segregation, abbreviated SDS.

Because the products remain ordered, the arrangement of chromatids at the two second-division spindles determines the observed pattern. The four meiotic products may be A a A a or A a a A, with corresponding reversed patterns. After the postmeiotic mitosis these become the 2:2:2:2 or 2:4:2 octad arrangements. Both are SDS. A student should not classify them as different numbers of crossovers merely because their colours alternate a different number of times. In the simplest model a single exchange within the interval can generate either pattern, depending on the orientation of second-division segregation.

FDS and SDS are not labels for two different kinds of organisms or two different phases at which crossing over normally occurs. The crossover generally occurs in meiotic prophase I. FDS and SDS describe the division at which the marker alleles become partitioned into separate nuclear lineages. An SDS ascus does not imply that recombination happened during the second meiotic division.

The same visible pattern can also arise after more complicated exchange histories. If two exchanges occur in the marker-centromere interval, their effect depends on the chromatids involved. A second exchange between the same pair of chromatids can restore the original marker-centromere phase. A four-strand double exchange can leave the two chromatids of each homolog carrying the same allele, but in the reversed phase. Both can look like FDS. A three-strand double exchange can leave unlike alleles associated with a centromere and produce SDS. Thus the visible order does not uniquely identify the number of molecular exchange events.

Reading the geometry of an ordered ascus

Consider an ordered ascus as a record of two successive partitions. The first partition separates homologous centromeres into two nuclear lineages. The second partitions chromatids within each of those lineages. In the eight-spored Neurospora model, a final mitosis duplicates the products without changing their order. Thus the first four spores correspond to one meiosis I lineage and the last four to the other. Within each group of four, two adjacent pairs represent the two meiosis II products. This nested structure explains the standard interpretation of the colour sequences.

A useful procedure is to collapse each pair of adjacent, genetically identical spores into a single symbol. For AA aa AA aa the collapsed sequence is A a A a. Examine the first two symbols, representing one meiosis I lineage, and the last two symbols, representing the other. Both lineages carry both alleles, so the alleles became separate at the second division. For AA AA aa aa, the collapsed sequence is A A a a. Each meiosis I lineage is homogeneous, so this is first-division segregation. The reversal aa aa AA AA gives exactly the same classification. The direction in which an ascus is read is not biologically meaningful for this inference.

This algorithm has a deliberate stopping condition. If the two members of an adjacent pair differ, collapsing the pair would hide information. For instance, in a sequence A A A a a a a a, the second pair contains A and a. Treating this as an ordinary ordered tetrad by arbitrarily assigning that pair to A or a would discard evidence of a potential postmeiotic event. An analysis of ordinary segregation must first establish that the paired lineages behave in the expected way. Abnormal octads are recorded separately for conversion analysis.

An intact ascus with a total of four spores of each type is not automatically an FDS ascus. The total 4:4 count describes ordinary allele number. The arrangement 2:4:2 also has four A and four a; the four in the middle are of one allele while the two at each end belong to the other. The notation is potentially confusing because the middle number four is visually prominent. Classification depends on the first and second halves, not on the presence of any run of four identical spores. This simple distinction prevents a common numerical error.

The chromatid-level explanation can be made more precise by assigning centromere identities C and c to the two homologs. Before exchange the chromatids can be represented C–A, C–A, c–a and c–a. A reciprocal exchange between the centromere and the marker involving one chromatid from each homolog produces C–A, C–a, c–A and c–a. At meiosis I, the two C-bearing chromatids remain associated in one lineage, and the two c-bearing chromatids in the other. Each lineage now contains one A and one a. Any ordinary orientation of the two second-division spindles gives either A a A a or A a a A, up to reversal or allele exchange.

This representation also clarifies what would happen if a crossover took place on the other chromosome arm. An exchange involving DNA that does not lie between the centromere and A cannot change which A allele is connected to a particular centromere under the ordinary reciprocal exchange model. The genetic interval under consideration is bounded by a centromere and a locus, not by the full chromosome or by whichever two visible markers happen to be convenient. If there are multiple marker loci, a crossover may be inside one marker-centromere interval but outside another. The same ascus may therefore show FDS for one marker and SDS for another.

Suppose A lies near the centromere and B lies farther away on the same arm. A single crossover between A and B can make the B allele SDS while the A allele remains FDS. A single crossover between the centromere and A can make both loci SDS, since both are distal to the exchange. In an ideal short-interval model, a dataset in which B consistently exhibits more SDS than A would be compatible with this order. But frequency alone does not prove that A and B are on the same arm. A marker on the opposite arm may have a similar genetic distance to the centromere. Independent two-marker observations are needed to distinguish these geometries.

To illustrate, suppose two loci each show an estimated gene-centromere distance of 10 cM. If both occupy approximately the same genetic position on one arm, their intermarker distance can be small. If they occupy different arms and genetic distances are additive, the expected distance between them can be about 20 cM. The absolute distance to a shared reference does not uniquely identify direction from that reference. This is analogous to two places each located ten kilometres from a city centre: they may be adjacent or lie in opposite directions. The analogy is limited because a chromosome's recombination landscape is not a uniformly scaled road.

The first-division partition can be inferred only when the ascus preserves the appropriate lineage structure. In some cases, spores may become displaced during development or observation. A visually linear arrangement alone is not sufficient unless the organism and developmental process justify interpreting it as ordered meiotic products. An organism with unordered asci cannot be made suitable for direct centromere mapping by arbitrarily placing its four spores in a row. Artificial sorting records the investigator's chosen order, not the order of nuclear division.

One can also ask what happens when homologous centromeres fail to separate normally. Such a case violates the partition model used in ordinary ordered analysis. Nondisjunction can generate products with missing or extra chromosomes and may alter viability or genotype signals. The classical FDS and SDS equations assume successful chromosome segregation and haploid products carrying the relevant chromosome. A geneticist must not force unusual ploidy patterns into an ordinary centromere mapping class merely because four or eight bodies are visible in an ascus. The number of spores and the chromosomal content of those spores are distinct observations.

Estimating gene–centromere distance

For a sufficiently short interval, most informative recombination events can be treated as single crossovers. In an SDS ascus generated by one crossover, two of the four chromatids are recombinant with respect to marker and centromere. The other two are nonrecombinant. The contribution of such an ascus to the total recombinant chromatid fraction is therefore one half. An ordinary FDS ascus produced without an interval crossover contributes zero. When N complete ordinarily segregating asci include S SDS asci, the usual gene-centromere estimate is 100 times S divided by 2N centimorgans.

The factor of one half comes from counting chromatids, not from counting the two meiotic divisions or the eight spores. Suppose a scientist observes 70 FDS asci, 18 asci with 2:2:2:2 segregation and 12 with 2:4:2 segregation. The total is 100. The SDS count is 30, because both arrangements represent second-division segregation. In the single-exchange model each SDS ascus contributes two recombinant meiotic products. There are 60 recombinant products among 400 products, giving a fraction of 0.15 and an estimated distance of 15 cM. Duplicating the four products by mitosis would double both numerator and denominator and leave this result unchanged.

In a more realistic dataset the investigator might have 150 FDS asci, 27 alternating SDS asci, 18 symmetric SDS asci and five asci showing abnormal segregation. The five abnormal asci should not silently enter the ordinary segregation analysis. If the stated analysis excludes them, the denominator is 195 and the SDS numerator is 45. The estimate is 100 times 45 divided by 390, or approximately 11.54 cM. The conversion-like asci should also be reported as five of 200 scored asci, or 2.5 percent. This retains information about events that the mapping calculation cannot interpret under its ordinary assumptions.

A map distance in centimorgans is not a count of base pairs. The number of exchanges per physical length can vary with chromosomal location, species, genetic background and other biological factors. A short DNA segment in a region with frequent meiotic recombination may have a greater genetic length than a longer DNA segment near a region where recombination is suppressed. The mapping equation is a statistical estimate based on inherited marker configurations. It cannot determine a universal conversion between centimorgans and nucleotide length.

It is also important not to confuse the SDS percentage with the recombinant fraction of a two-marker testcross. Under a short-interval model, 40 percent SDS yields 20 cM. The raw SDS frequency is a proportion of asci; the estimated recombinant proportion uses the factor of two. Furthermore, repeated exchanges make the relationship between observed SDS and the expected number of exchanges nonlinear. If chromatid association with centromere becomes fully randomized among the six possible allocations of two A alleles to four positions, two allocations put matching alleles together at meiosis I and four put unlike alleles together. The resulting SDS proportion is two thirds under that limiting randomization model. The elementary estimate then approaches 33.3 cM despite the possibility of a much longer genetic interval. This is why a centromere distance obtained by the simple equation should not be extrapolated indefinitely.

A sample estimate carries sampling uncertainty. For example, suppose 60 of 200 independently sampled asci are SDS. The observed proportion is 0.30. In a simple binomial sampling model the standard error of the SDS proportion is the square root of 0.30 times 0.70 divided by 200, about 0.0324. The mapping estimate is 15 cM, with a corresponding standard error of about 1.62 cM. A rough normal approximation gives an interval of 15 plus or minus 3.18 cM. This quantifies sampling variation only; it does not correct systematic biases such as preferential spore death or undetected multiple crossovers.

Unordered tetrads: parental and recombinant haplotypes

When division order is unavailable, the classification of a tetrad rests on the allele combinations carried by its four products. For two loci A and B, suppose the parents contribute AB and ab. These are parental haplotypes, whereas Ab and aB are recombinant haplotypes relative to this cross. If the starting phase had been Ab/aB, the identities would reverse. Parental and recombinant are historical descriptions relative to the original parental chromosomes, not intrinsic properties of uppercase and lowercase allele symbols.

Under normal 2:2 segregation at both loci, only three classes of unordered tetrad are possible. A parental ditype, PD, consists of two AB products and two ab products. A nonparental ditype, NPD, contains two Ab products and two aB products. A tetratype, T or TT, contains one of each of the four possible haplotypes. Ditype means that two different combinations are present; tetratype means that four are present. Each class still represents four products. A tetratype is not a family of four independently sampled spores, and a ditype does not mean there were only two spores.

Why are there only three classes? Let x denote the count of AB products. The total count carrying A must be two, so the count of Ab is 2 minus x. The total count carrying B must also be two, so the count of aB is 2 minus x. The fourth count, ab, must equal x to bring the total to four. The allowed integer values of x are zero, one and two. They give NPD, T and PD, respectively. This short derivation shows that the classification follows from ordinary segregation, not a memorized list of names. A tetrad showing three A products cannot fit this proof because the first assumption fails.

The simplest linked cross yields PD when no exchange occurs between the two markers. A single crossover between the markers yields T because two non-sister chromatids become recombinant while two retain the parental combinations. A four-strand double crossover can generate NPD, with all four products recombinant. Two-strand double crossovers can restore both parental combinations and generate PD. Three-strand double crossovers can yield T. These examples make clear that tetrad classes describe outcomes, not unique crossover histories.

Two unlinked genes can also produce PD, NPD or T. If neither gene recombines relative to its centromere, the first meiotic spindle can orient the two homolog pairs in matching or opposite directions. Under fair independent orientation, PD and NPD should be equally frequent. If at least one locus undergoes SDS, tetratypes may also appear, with the exact frequencies depending on the locus-centromere relationships and chromatid behavior. Therefore the universal test for independent assortment is not an assumed 1:1:4 ratio. A 1:1:4 distribution can arise under particular randomization assumptions, but equality of PD and NPD is the relevant symmetry for the standard unlinked model.

When two genes are linked, parental combinations tend to be recovered together more often than entirely recombinant ditypes. An excess of PD over NPD supports linkage under unbiased recovery and accurate classification. Equality of PD and NPD, on the other hand, provides no strong evidence for linkage in the sample; it does not establish that loci are physically on separate chromosomes. Distant markers on the same chromosome can be genetically indistinguishable from unlinked loci. Sample size is another limitation: six PD and four NPD might reflect sampling variation, while sixty PD and four NPD offer a much stronger departure from the equal-frequency expectation.

Tracing multiple exchanges without losing chromatid identity

The relationship between tetrad class and crossover number can be derived by following the four chromatids individually. Label the two chromatids of the first replicated homolog 1 and 2 and those of the second homolog 3 and 4. Let the markers be A and B, with the initial haplotypes AB on chromatids 1 and 2, and ab on chromatids 3 and 4. Imagine a first crossover in the interval between the markers involving chromatids 1 and 3. After this exchange, the distal segment of chromatid 1 carries b and that of chromatid 3 carries B. Their haplotypes become Ab and aB, while chromatids 2 and 4 retain AB and ab. The tetrad is a tetratype.

Now consider a second exchange in the same genetic interval, distal to the first exchange. If it involves the same chromatids 1 and 3, their distal segments are exchanged again. The B marker on chromatid 1 becomes B, and the marker on chromatid 3 becomes b. At the scored endpoints, all four chromatids have regained parental haplotypes. The outcome is PD even though two exchange events occurred. The DNA between the exchange positions need not have returned to its original arrangement. An internal marker may reveal the exchanges that the outer two markers cannot detect.

If the second exchange instead involves chromatids 2 and 4, it exchanges distal segments on the two chromatids that remained parental after the first exchange. Chromatid 2 becomes Ab and chromatid 4 becomes aB. All four chromatids now carry recombinant marker combinations and the tetrad is NPD. This is a four-strand double crossover. It is particularly useful because, for an ordinarily segregating linked two-marker interval in the simplest model, the NPD class cannot be produced by a single ordinary reciprocal crossover.

The remaining possibilities involve three different chromatids across the two exchanges. For example, a second exchange involving chromatids 1 and 4 can redistribute distal marker alleles such that the final tetrad still contains one of each of the four marker haplotypes. Similarly, a second exchange involving chromatids 2 and 3 can retain a tetratype outcome. A three-strand double crossover is thus hidden among the tetratypes when only the two endpoint markers are scored. Under the elementary model these four pair choices have equal probabilities, so two-strand, three-strand and four-strand double exchanges occur in the expected ratio 1:2:1.

This derivation is conditional on the first event having occurred, and concerns participation in the second event. It does not state that double exchanges themselves occur at any particular probability. If a crossover reduces the likelihood of another crossover nearby, fewer double exchanges may be present. That is crossover interference. If the second event, when it does occur, preferentially uses certain chromatids, the conditional 1:2:1 ratio may change. That is chromatid interference. One can have strong crossover interference while retaining no detectable chromatid interference. A calculation based on the second kind of independence does not necessarily require the first kind.

There is a helpful parity rule for an endpoint marker on a given chromatid. If it changes association an odd number of times relative to the proximal marker, it ends recombinant; an even number of changes can restore the original association. This rule works when the changes being counted are switches of marker origin along the tracked chromatid. It is not sufficient to count the number of cytological chiasmata on the whole homolog pair, because different events may involve different chromatids. The distinction between a pair-level event count and a chromatid-level endpoint state is the reason genetic mapping functions and tetrad estimators are necessary.

Three or more exchanges can create outcomes that also belong to PD, NPD or T. For example, after two exchanges have restored a parental class, an additional single exchange can again yield T. With increasing interval length the number of possible histories leading to the same endpoint distribution grows. A two-marker tetrad therefore does not contain enough information to count every crossover event without a model. Even a complete family of four products cannot recover sequence information about an unscored region. An extra marker placed inside the interval reduces ambiguity by revealing exchanges between smaller marker segments.

Consider an illustrative three-marker cross ABC/abc, with physical order A–B–C. A double crossover involving the same two chromatids, once between A and B and again between B and C, can produce AbC and aBc while the other chromatids remain ABC and abc. If we ignore B, all four products have the parental associations AC or ac. The two outer markers alone give PD. If we inspect A and B or B and C, each pair yields a tetratype. Thus the addition of a middle marker can uncover exchange events that an outer-marker-only comparison misses.

The purpose of a multi-marker analysis is not simply to accumulate more percentages. It tests whether a proposed chromosome order can explain the pattern of changes in ancestry along a chromatid. A chromatid with proximal and distal parental segments but an opposite parental origin in the middle suggests two ancestry boundaries under a simple exchange model. A short conversion tract at the middle marker can produce a superficially similar pattern, however. Examining the other members of the tetrad is decisive: reciprocal exchange should ordinarily preserve 2:2 segregation at all three loci, whereas conversion can generate a 3:1 distribution at the affected marker.

An exam question may describe a tetrad with a PD outcome at the outer markers and ask whether recombination occurred. The scientifically defensible answer is that no recombinant endpoint haplotype was observed; recombination could still have occurred within the interval. An answer claiming no crossing over anywhere in the chromosome overstates the evidence twice: even one interval may hide exchanges, and other chromosome regions are not assayed. The precise wording of the observation matters.

Recombination fraction and Perkins mapping

The recombinant fraction is obtained by counting recombinant chromatids. In a PD tetrad, none of the four is recombinant. In a T tetrad, two are recombinant. In an NPD tetrad, all four are recombinant. With P parental ditypes, N nonparental ditypes and T tetratypes, the total number of products is four times P plus N plus T. The recombinant count is two times T plus four times N. Dividing gives r = (T/2 + N)/(P + N + T). Multiplying by 100 expresses this endpoint recombinant fraction as a percentage.

The Perkins mapping estimate addresses a different question: approximately how many exchange events are represented by the tetrad classes? Consider an interval with negligible triple and higher exchanges and no chromatid interference. After a first crossover between two non-sister chromatids, a second may use the same two chromatids, share one chromatid with the first exchange, or involve the other two chromatids. There are four equally likely pair choices under the no-chromatid-interference model. One gives the two-strand class, two give the three-strand class and one gives the four-strand class. The expected ratio is therefore 1:2:1.

Let D denote the total number of double-crossover tetrads and S the number of single-crossover tetrads. Four-strand doubles constitute one quarter of D and produce NPD for linked markers under this model. Hence D is estimated as four times N. Three-strand doubles constitute one half of D and contribute two times N to the observed T count. The estimated number of single-crossover tetrads is consequently T minus two times N. The estimated total number of exchanges is S plus twice D, which reduces to T plus six times N. Each exchange involves two chromatids out of four, giving a map-distance estimate of 50 times (T + 6N)/(P + N + T), in centimorgans.

The formula is an estimate, not an exact reconstruction of every meiosis. Its derivation assumes linked loci, the expected double-exchange chromatid ratio, and sufficiently low frequencies of higher exchange classes. If T is smaller than twice N, the simplified reconstruction would require a negative number of single-crossover tetrads. That result should prompt examination of sampling variation, interference, high recombination, marker phase or the suitability of the model, rather than literal interpretation. Crossover interference changes the spacing of events along chromosomes; chromatid interference instead concerns which chromatids participate in successive events. They are not interchangeable concepts.

Consider an illustrative dataset with P = 130, N = 10 and T = 60. There are 200 tetrads. The endpoint recombinant fraction is (30 + 10)/200 = 0.20, or 20 percent. The Perkins estimate is 50(60 + 60)/200 = 30 cM. The difference arises because endpoints can hide exchanges, while the model uses NPD to estimate a contribution from otherwise hidden double exchanges. Reporting 30 percent recombinant gametes would be incorrect: the observed recombinant fraction is 20 percent.

For a second dataset, P = 160, T = 40 and N = 0. The endpoint recombinant fraction and the Perkins distance have the same numerical magnitude, 10 percent and 10 cM respectively. No NPD was observed, so the correction based on four-strand doubles vanishes. This does not prove that no double crossover occurred; two-strand and three-strand doubles could have occurred even if no four-strand class happened to be sampled. The absence of a rare class is weaker evidence when the sample is small.

The use of tetrad classes also illustrates why the four products cannot be treated as four independent observations for every statistical test. They are constrained by segregation within a meiosis. A simple PD versus NPD linkage test, conditional on the number of ditypes, compares ditype counts under equal probabilities in the unlinked model. For example, if there are 80 PD and 20 NPD, the null expectation is 50 in each class. The chi-square statistic is (80−50)²/50 + (20−50)²/50 = 36, with one degree of freedom under the usual large-sample assumptions. Tetratypes do not enter this conditional symmetry test, though they remain essential to mapping.

Combining ordered data with two-marker maps

A genetic map can contain a centromere as a reference point and several loci at different positions along the chromosome. The ordered tetrad estimate gives the unsigned genetic distance from each locus to its own centromere under the short-interval assumptions. Two-locus tetrad data add information about the distance between marker loci. Together these observations can distinguish chromosome geometries that neither measurement resolves alone.

Suppose an ordered analysis yields 12 percent SDS at A and 28 percent SDS at B. The elementary estimates are 6 cM and 14 cM from their respective centromeres. If the two genes are on the same arm of the same chromosome and the distances are accurately estimated in a locally additive map, their predicted separation is approximately 8 cM. If they are on opposite arms of the same chromosome, the separation is approximately 20 cM. A measured two-marker distance near 8 cM supports the first arrangement, whereas one near 20 cM supports the second. This reasoning uses the centromere as a reference coordinate, with distances either subtracted or added depending on orientation.

There is a necessary qualification: the ordered estimates do not themselves demonstrate that the two markers belong to the same chromosome. A and B may each lie near the centromere of different chromosomes. In that case a direct two-marker comparison should show the symmetry expected for independent assortment, subject to sampling and recovery biases. Computing a difference of 8 cM between loci on different chromosomes has no genetic-map meaning. Before assigning loci to a common map, establish a linkage relationship or other evidence of shared chromosome membership.

A second qualification is that the elementary half-SDS estimates are not exact additive distances in intervals where multiple crossovers are common. The observed fractions can underestimate true map lengths, and subtraction can combine errors from two separate estimates. If the expected and observed distances differ moderately, possible explanations include sampling variation, hidden exchanges, interference or an incorrect arm assignment. A discrepancy is a prompt to test assumptions, not necessarily proof that the map is impossible.

Suppose 500 ordered asci provide joint segregation information for two markers. Of these, 350 are FDS for both, 50 are SDS for A only, 75 are SDS for B only and 25 are SDS for both. The marginal SDS frequencies are 75/500 = 0.15 for A and 100/500 = 0.20 for B. The elementary estimates are 7.5 cM and 10 cM. The joint table is richer than the marginal counts: it tells us that 25 asci display SDS at both markers. Under statistical independence of the two SDS statuses, the expected count in the joint SDS class would be 500 times 0.15 times 0.20, or 15. The observed excess suggests association of the statuses, but it does not by itself identify the exact crossover history. Shared interval geometry and other biological features may generate dependence.

A student might attempt to infer the exact distance between A and B by subtracting these two marginal frequencies. The subtraction is meaningful only under the assumed geometry and mapping approximation. If A and B lie on one arm with little multiple exchange, the difference of estimates is 2.5 cM. But the presence of many asci in which A is SDS and B is FDS challenges a model in which all events are single exchanges and B is distal to A. In that simple model every single event that makes A SDS should also make B SDS. Multiple exchanges, conversion, marker misclassification or a different map geometry must be considered.

This example illustrates an important examination skill: inspect the joint pattern, not just the final percentage. A model can fit marginal observations while failing to explain correlations between them. Genetic maps should account for how actual chromatid configurations generate joint segregation. When complete ordered data are available, discarding those configurations merely to calculate two distances wastes information.

For unlinked loci, the relationship between tetratype frequency and the centromere segregation patterns can also be derived. If both loci are FDS, the two meiosis I halves each contain a matching allele state at both loci. Independent orientation of the homolog pairs produces either a PD or an NPD tetrad, never a tetratype. If one locus is FDS and the other SDS, each half has one allele state of the FDS marker but both states of the SDS marker. Across the two halves all four haplotypes are recovered, so a tetratype results.

If both loci are SDS, each meiosis I half contains both states of both markers. In a simple model of independent second-division orientations, the two halves have equal chances of containing the same or different pairs of haplotypes. When both halves contain the same pair, the outcome is a ditype; the parental and nonparental choices are equally likely. When they contain different pairs, the outcome is a tetratype. The conditional tetratype probability is one half in this case, not one. These conditional relationships explain why the T proportion depends on the frequencies of centromere recombination even for unlinked markers.

For example, let the SDS probabilities of two independently behaving unlinked loci be p = 0.20 and q = 0.30. The probability that exactly one is SDS is p(1−q) + (1−p)q = 0.14 + 0.24 = 0.38. The probability that both are SDS is pq = 0.06; half of those yield T under the stated assumptions. The expected T fraction is therefore 0.41. The combined ditype fraction is 0.59, divided equally into PD and NPD fractions of 0.295 each. The result differs markedly from 1:1:4 despite the loci being unlinked.

If both SDS probabilities reach two thirds in the limiting randomization model, the same calculation gives 2/3 tetratypes and 1/6 each of the ditypes. This is the familiar 1:1:4 ratio, but it emerges from specified conditions. It should never be treated as a universal requirement for unlinked inheritance. The decisive reasoning is whether the symmetry between the two ditype classes is expected under the model and whether the observed sampling deviation is compatible with that symmetry.

Gene conversion and postmeiotic segregation

Ordinary reciprocal crossing over rearranges allele associations but preserves the total of each allele across the four products. Gene conversion changes this expectation. In a heterozygote, one allele sequence can be copied into the DNA molecule originally carrying the other allele, producing nonreciprocal genetic transmission. A conventional complete tetrad may then contain three products of one allele and one of the other instead of two and two. In Neurospora, postmeiotic mitosis can turn the 3:1 nuclear ratio into a 6:2 spore ratio.

To understand why this happens, return briefly to homologous recombination. Repair of a programmed meiotic DNA break can involve pairing a DNA strand from one homolog with a complementary strand from the other. Where the parental sequences differ, the resulting heteroduplex DNA can contain a mismatch. Repair of that mismatch can restore the original sequence or change it to the sequence carried by the other homolog. The genetic outcome depends on the direction of correction and the position of the affected sequence. DNA synthesis during repair can also copy information from a homologous template. Gene conversion can accompany either a crossover or a noncrossover outcome; the two terms describe distinct aspects of recombination.

Imagine that A and a differ at one nucleotide. In an ordinary meiosis there are two A-bearing chromatids and two a-bearing chromatids. If sequence repair converts one A-bearing chromatid to a before the formation of meiotic products, the resulting genotypes are A, a, a, a. After ordinary mitotic duplication of the four nuclei, the octad contains two A spores and six a spores. The reverse direction gives six A and two a. No additional chromosome needs to be created, and the allele whose count increases need not confer greater fitness.

A mismatch may persist in a heteroduplex until the next DNA replication. The two strands of that DNA duplex then act as templates for different daughter duplexes. One daughter inherits the A sequence and the other the a sequence. This produces postmeiotic segregation, which can appear as a 5:3 or 3:5 octad. For example, suppose two of the other three meiotic lineages contain A and the third contains a, while the fourth is heteroduplex. The three ordinarily segregating lineages give four A and two a spores. The heteroduplex lineage supplies one of each, yielding five A and three a in total. The critical observation is not simply an unusual count, but a discordant pair among descendants of one meiotic product.

An octad with four A and four a can also be abnormal if the order shows that postmeiotic sister pairs are not genetically identical. Reciprocal changes, more than one heteroduplex, or other complex events may preserve total allele numbers while disrupting expected pairing. Consequently total counts alone can conceal some recombination-associated alterations. Ordered data should retain both the counts and their positions.

Gene conversion is neither necessarily a de novo mutation nor synonymous with meiotic drive. A newly arising mutation can alter a marker but does not require copying an existing allelic sequence. Meiotic drive changes transmission probabilities, sometimes through the survival or recovery of particular products. A conversion hypothesis is strengthened when the altered sequence matches the corresponding homolog and when surrounding markers support a recombination-associated event. Spore lethality, an incorrect genotype call or accidental mixing of spores can generate misleading observed ratios, so these alternatives must be addressed before assigning a molecular mechanism.

The landmark value of unusual segregation patterns was that they transformed apparent exceptions to Mendelian ratios into evidence about the molecular processes of genetic exchange. Classical tetrad studies inferred nonreciprocal transmission long before routine genome-wide sequencing. Modern genotyping of intact meiotic families can reveal short conversion tracts surrounded by ordinarily segregating markers. Such observations demonstrate why a chromosome can retain its broad parental association while a short interval has acquired sequence information from its homolog.

Distinguishing conversion, segregation distortion and scoring error

Gene conversion can be investigated through the relationship among all four meiotic products. A single gamete carrying allele a in an A/a heterozygote provides no evidence for conversion because ordinary segregation can also transmit a. A complete meiotic family carrying three a alleles and one A allele is much more informative. If the genotypes are correctly assigned, all products are present and there is no change in chromosome copy number, the family cannot be explained by ordinary reciprocal exchange alone. A local sequence-copying mechanism is a plausible explanation.

The molecular basis of this inference is the distinction between strand ancestry and allele identity. A DNA duplex can contain one strand derived from one homolog and a complementary strand derived from the other. At a position where the parental sequences differ, the two strands may not form an ordinary complementary base pair. This mismatch is an opportunity for repair. If one strand is removed and resynthesized using the other as a template, the resulting duplex can become homoallelic at that position even though the broader chromosome retains its original ancestry. A short segment may acquire donor information without the entire chromosome arm being exchanged.

Restoration and conversion are alternative genetic outcomes of processing a mismatch. If repair restores the sequence that the recipient chromatid had before the recombination intermediate was formed, the marker still segregates normally. If the repaired duplex acquires the homolog's marker state, a conversion is observed. A normal 2:2 ratio therefore does not show that heteroduplex DNA never formed. It may indicate no mismatch, successful restoration or multiple changes whose effects cancel in the allele count. The assay detects the subset of molecular events that leave informative genetic outcomes.

Postmeiotic segregation provides a complementary observation. Suppose a heteroduplex persists into the first mitosis after meiosis. During semiconservative DNA replication, each parental strand templates its complementary strand. A mismatched position can then yield two daughter duplexes carrying different marker states. Unlike a completed conversion, which changes the state in the entire product lineage, unrepaired heteroduplex DNA can generate different descendants within that lineage. In an ordered octad the pair of descendants is spatially identifiable, making such evidence especially clear.

An illustrative sequence A A A a a a a a has three A and five a spores, with one discordant adjacent pair. Another sequence A A A A A A a a has six A and two a spores, with all adjacent pairs internally matching. The latter is consistent with one completed conversion in a standard octad model; the former is consistent with postmeiotic segregation of an unrepaired heteroduplex. Neither sequence alone establishes the identity of every enzyme involved, the precise length of the recombination intermediate or whether crossing over also occurred at flanking markers.

To ask whether conversion is associated with crossing over, score additional markers on both sides of the affected locus. Suppose the initial haplotypes are L–A–R and l–a–r. After meiosis one family contains L–A–R, L–a–R, l–a–r and l–a–r. There is a 1:3 segregation of the central marker, while both outer markers retain parental combinations. This family is consistent with a conversion at A without a detectable reciprocal crossover between the outer markers. A different family may show nonparental associations of L and R together with a conversion at the central marker. That outcome suggests a conversion accompanied by exchange somewhere in the flanked region. The two types of evidence are scored separately.

The reason for retaining flanking markers is that marker conversion can itself make an apparent recombinant haplotype. If a gene acquires the other allele without a reciprocal exchange of the neighbouring segment, a two-marker analysis that assumes ordinary segregation can mistake that product for an ordinary recombinant. Complete tetrad data reveal the abnormal allele count and permit the family to be excluded or analyzed with an appropriate model. The correct exclusion rule must be chosen before comparing treatment groups, otherwise unusual classes may be selectively removed to obtain a desired result.

An alternative mechanism for an unusual observed ratio is differential survival. In an ordinary A/a tetrad with two A and two a products, loss of an A product produces one recovered A and two recovered a products. This is a three-product observation, not a complete 1:3 tetrad. If another product is accidentally counted twice, a misleading complete family can be manufactured by sampling error. If a missing product is known to have been nonviable, it should be designated unknown or dead rather than automatically assigned to the underrepresented allele. These precautions protect the distinction between biological transmission and observation.

Meiotic drive likewise requires careful definition. A transmission advantage can arise because certain products are preferentially included in functional gametes or because competing products are destroyed. In a fungal tetrad, examining all products may reveal whether the distorted recovery reflects spore viability rather than a change in the DNA sequence. Gene conversion changes allele identity within a lineage, whereas a survival advantage changes which lineages contribute to the recovered population. Both can alter population-level allele proportions, but their evidence at the level of a complete ascus differs.

A final distinction concerns mutation. A new mutation might produce the same visible phenotype as the opposite parental allele without copying that parental DNA sequence. For instance, a different disruptive mutation in the same pigment gene could cause loss of colour. Sequence-level comparison can reveal whether the altered region matches the homologous donor or carries a novel sequence. The stronger inference of conversion rests on nonreciprocal inheritance of recognizable parental sequence information. Classical phenotypic evidence may establish aberrant segregation, while molecular data refine the explanation.

Published genome-wide tetrad studies have used sequence markers to examine recombination-associated changes across fungi and other organisms. Such studies demonstrate that conversion frequency can vary among species and regions, and they caution against assuming that gene conversion always preferentially copies one nucleotide class. For NET and SET reasoning, the key point is that a conversion mechanism does not automatically imply directional bias. If 6:2 asci favour A as often as 2:6 asci favour a, individual events are non-Mendelian but the aggregate transmission may still show no net preference. Conversely, a reproducible imbalance in directions can support biased transmission under appropriate controls.

How fungal genetic observations shaped recombination theory

The value of the complete meiotic family was recognized during the development of chromosome mapping long before the nucleotide sequence of any fungal chromosome could be determined. Classical geneticists had established that markers lying on the same chromosome could show linkage and that linkage weakened when exchanges separated their alleles. Fungal asci provided a way to test a more detailed prediction: a reciprocal exchange should alter the association of particular alleles without altering their total number among the products of one heterozygous meiosis. This prediction does not depend on whether the organism has a dominant phenotype.

If a geneticist had access only to individually sampled haploid spores, finding a marker in 55 percent of the population might initially appear to be an unexplained departure from equal segregation. The observation might reflect a modest sampling fluctuation, preferential survival or transmission, or a change within a meiotic product. The same aggregate percentage can arise from very different compositions of meiotic families. In contrast, discovering individual complete asci with three copies of one parental allele immediately focuses attention on the mechanism of segregation within the ascus. This shift in the unit of observation was central to the interpretation of unusual fungal ratios.

Perkins's 1949 work on biochemical mutants in the smut fungus Ustilago maydis contributed to the formal development of tetrad mapping. The geneticist could not simply count crossover events directly from the two-marker phenotypes. Two exchanges between the same chromatids could restore the endpoint associations; exchanges involving all four chromatids could reveal an NPD class. Tetrad classifications thus enabled a correction for some exchanges that would remain hidden in ordinary gamete analysis. Perkins's later paper on detecting linkage in tetrad analysis emphasized that the products of a meiosis are associated observations. A test based on a family class is not equivalent to a test that incorrectly assumes every recovered chromatid arose independently.

Why did this correction matter? Suppose two geneticists analyze the same cross, one recording each spore as if it were an independent gamete and the other retaining the ascus identities. Both can compute the observed recombinant fraction. The second can also estimate how many of the observed all-recombinant families may represent four-strand double exchanges and infer hidden classes under a chromatid model. It is not the biological crossover frequency that has changed between their analyses, but the amount of information retained during observation. Complete family recording is therefore an advance in experimental design, not just a special equation.

Aberrant fungal segregation subsequently encouraged a second kind of theoretical development. An ordinary exchange of equal, corresponding DNA segments is reciprocal. It cannot by itself change a tally of two alleles into three alleles of one kind and one of the other. Geneticists needed a model in which information could move between homologs in a manner that was not necessarily reciprocal at the sequence level. The ability to recognize the postmeiotic descendants of a single nuclear product further suggested that an intermediate could contain different inherited information on its two DNA strands.

Robin Holliday's 1964 model proposed a molecular framework involving hybrid DNA and correction of sequence differences, helping connect the unusual fungal patterns to the physical structure of DNA and reciprocal exchange. Its importance for an examination lies in the explanatory change it introduced: recombination could generate regions of paired strands with different parental origins, and processing of such a region could alter allele identity. The original historical model should not be presented as the complete modern description of all meiotic recombination. Later evidence supported double-strand-break initiation and multiple pathways of repair and resolution. The modern account retains the distinction between heteroduplex formation, restoration or conversion, and crossover or noncrossover outcomes.

A useful historical question is what the model would predict if a mismatch persisted without correction. A repaired conversion would be represented by a uniform change in a product lineage. An uncorrected difference between the two DNA strands could lead to descendants carrying different alleles after replication. These are distinguishable genetic predictions. Octad analysis can test the prediction at a level that an ordinary diploid phenotype might conceal. The observation of postmeiotic segregation is thus not a decorative historical curiosity; it supplies evidence that genetic information may be resolved after the original meiotic division.

The transition from phenotype-based to molecular markers strengthened these inferences. Consider a classical observation in which a formerly pale spore becomes coloured. It may be consistent with conversion of an allele, but pigmentation is the end product of a biochemical pathway and could be influenced by a different mutation. If DNA markers reveal that the altered region specifically matches the sequence of the homolog and that neighbouring sequences retain their original ancestry, the copying hypothesis becomes much stronger. A marker panel can also show whether the local nonreciprocal event was accompanied by a change in the phase of flanking markers.

A study of recombination on the left arm of chromosome VII in budding yeast examined marker intervals across an extended chromosome region. Its authors analyzed reciprocal exchange and non-Mendelian marker segregation as related but separately recorded outcomes. They excluded conversion or postmeiotic-segregation families from ordinary interval mapping where those events prevented application of the simple assumptions. The key lesson for interpreting the evidence is that exclusion is a model-specific choice: the families are informative for conversion, even if inappropriate for calculating an ordinary two-marker distance. A complete experiment can use the same recovered family for different questions with different inclusion criteria.

A published comparative study of complete meiotic products in yeast, Neurospora, Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis used sequence information to investigate gene conversion across different organisms. A principal finding was that rates varied considerably and did not establish a general GC-favouring bias in that dataset. This does not imply that biased conversion cannot occur elsewhere; it means that an assumed universal preference is not warranted by those observations. The study illustrates the difference between a mechanism and a statistical tendency in its outcomes. A mechanism can generate changes in either direction, while the average directional effect depends on the distribution of those changes.

History therefore provides a connected route through the chapter: observing linkage suggested an exchange of chromosome segments; retaining the complete family allowed more precise analysis of that exchange; unexpected families suggested that sequence transmission could be nonreciprocal; and molecular markers helped distinguish local sequence copying from changes of chromosome ancestry. This progression explains why tetrad analysis remains conceptually important even when modern sequencing can identify many more markers than classical fungi supplied.

Experimental interpretation and controls

A defensible tetrad experiment begins with a clearly stated cross and reliable marker phase. The researcher must know which alleles were originally present together, because PD and NPD labels cannot be assigned from the appearance of the alleles alone. An incorrect phase swaps the two ditype categories, potentially turning apparent linkage into apparent excess of recombinant ditypes. Marker genotypes are checked in the parental strains and in the heterozygous diploid where appropriate.

The sample should preserve the relationship among spores from one ascus. If spores from two asci are accidentally combined, an apparent 3:1 marker ratio might simply be a sampling artifact. A count of complete, incomplete and unscoreable asci makes the analysis transparent. A missing product cannot be treated as a known genotype without independent evidence. Where a nutritional marker is scored by growth, a general viability control distinguishes inability to grow under the selective condition from failure to germinate at all. Positive and negative genotype controls help test whether a scoring method actually discriminates the alleles.

Consider two hypothetical yeast crosses. In cross X, 90 percent of all asci yield four viable products, and an unusually high NPD fraction is observed among them. In cross Y, only 30 percent yield four products, and the NPD fraction among complete asci is low. The difference could reflect real recombination variation, but it could also reflect preferential loss of certain classes in cross Y. A meaningful interpretation requires the distribution of viability classes and, if possible, genotype information for incomplete families. Selection on products can change the observed distribution of the retained tetrads.

Research studies can compare conversion frequencies and crossover distances in different intervals of a chromosome using distinguishable markers. A conversion at one marker is a local observation; a crossover identified by changed flanking haplotypes is an interval observation. The two observations can coincide without being identical. A study of the left arm of yeast chromosome VII used marker-defined intervals and distinguished ordinary tetrads from those affected by conversion or postmeiotic segregation in calculating map distances. This provides an authentic example of how the denominator is matched to the assumptions of the calculation.

Modern molecular markers do not invalidate classical tetrad logic. Sequencing adds information about the exact sequence changes and the positions of recombination boundaries, but the classification of complete meiotic families still constrains the interpretation. A reciprocal change in the associations of flanking markers supports exchange; a short nonreciprocal tract supports conversion; a complex pattern may require more than a single event. The next chapter will generalize marker-based reasoning beyond intact fungal asci.

Sampling, linkage tests and limits of map estimates

A tetrad count is a sample from a biological process. Two repeated experiments using genetically identical parents need not recover the same numbers of PD, NPD and T. Before treating an observed difference as evidence for a new mechanism, one should compare it with the variation expected under a stated null model. The model specifies what would be observed if the proposed genetic relationship were absent. It also specifies which observations are independent and how they were selected.

For the simplest unlinked two-locus model, parental and nonparental ditypes have equal probabilities. Conditioning on the total number of ditypes removes the need to specify the overall tetratype probability. Suppose the experiment produces P = 32, N = 28 and T = 140. There are 200 total families, but the conditional ditype comparison involves 60. The expected counts are 30 PD and 30 NPD. The chi-square statistic is 4/30 + 4/30 = 0.267. With one degree of freedom, this is a small deviation. It would not ordinarily support rejecting equal ditype frequencies at the conventional five-percent level. The high tetratype proportion is not by itself sufficient evidence against independent assortment.

Compare a second set with P = 95, N = 5 and T = 100. The expected conditional counts are 50 each and the chi-square statistic is 81. This large imbalance strongly contradicts the equal-ditype expectation under the stated assumptions. The appropriate biological interpretation is evidence supporting linkage or another process that violates the assumed symmetry. If the researcher has independent evidence that recombinant ditypes have low viability, the imbalance cannot automatically be attributed solely to linkage. A statistical test identifies incompatibility with a model; it does not, by itself, name the biological cause.

When the number of ditypes is small, a chi-square approximation can be misleading. The exact conditional model is a binomial distribution with a probability of one half for a PD among the ordinary ditypes. Suppose five ditypes are observed and all five are parental. The probability of obtaining all five PD is (1/2)^5 = 1/32. If the question asks for a two-sided test of any departure from equality, the equally extreme outcome of five NPD must also be counted. The two-sided probability is 2/32 = 0.0625. Even an apparently perfect five-to-zero ratio is therefore not decisive at a five-percent threshold for this two-sided test. With eight ditypes all parental, the analogous two-sided probability is 2/256, or about 0.0078.

It is tempting to treat the observed recombinant fraction as a binomial estimate based on 4N independent products. That model is usually inappropriate for the complete-family sample because recombinant counts within a tetrad are constrained. A tetratype always contains two recombinant products under ordinary segregation. An NPD always contains four. A PD contains none. A more appropriate simple approach treats each ascus as an independent observation whose recombinant proportion is 0, 0.5 or 1, according to its class. The sample mean of these family-level values equals the pooled recombinant fraction because each complete family has the same size.

As an illustrative example, suppose 100 complete families contain 70 PD, 20 T and 10 NPD. The mean recombinant fraction is 0.20. The mean squared family-level value is 0.70(0²) + 0.20(0.5²) + 0.10(1²) = 0.15. The sample variance estimate, corrected for the finite sample size, is approximately (100/99)(0.15−0.20²) = 0.1111. The standard error of the family mean is approximately the square root of 0.1111/100 = 0.0333. This calculation recognizes that the independently sampled observations are families, not the four members taken separately.

The Perkins estimator can be viewed in a similar manner. Each PD contributes zero to the distance numerator, each T contributes 50 and each NPD contributes 300, when the result is expressed as the mean contribution over all families in centimorgans. In the preceding sample, the estimate is [70(0) + 20(50) + 10(300)]/100 = 40 cM. The large weight placed on NPD reflects the model-based correction for hidden double crossovers. It also means that a relatively small change in the NPD count can have a substantial influence on the estimate. A student should not be surprised when a map distance based on a modest number of complete tetrads appears imprecise.

For two crosses with otherwise identical tetratype counts, the sensitivity of the estimator can be seen directly. Cross 1 has 89 PD, 10 T and 1 NPD; cross 2 has 87 PD, 10 T and 3 NPD. The observed recombinant proportions are 6 and 8 percent respectively, but the Perkins estimates are 8 and 14 cM. Two additional NPD asci change the map estimate by 6 cM when the total is fixed at 100. This is neither an arithmetic defect nor proof of a large biological difference. It is a consequence of weighting NPD according to the double-exchange model. Comparison between such samples should consider uncertainty and the possibility that rare classes were unevenly sampled.

Another important boundary is the relationship between a recombinant proportion and a genetic map length. Under the usual linked-marker model without systematic distortions, the expected recombinant proportion cannot exceed one half for the standard two-locus recombination fraction as linkage is lost through randomization. An individual tetrad can nevertheless be entirely recombinant. A finite sample can have an observed fraction above one half. This does not mean that a chromosome contains a negative map distance or that the expected recombination ceiling has been abolished. The observation may reflect sampling variation, misassigned parental phase, selection, segregation distortion or a model outside ordinary assumptions.

A map length estimates exchange along a chromosome interval and may exceed 50 cM. The observed endpoint recombinant proportion saturates because an even number of exchanges can restore an initial association. The Perkins correction can yield lengths greater than the endpoint percentage, but it too becomes unreliable when higher-order exchanges are common. The biological meaning of a long map interval is better resolved by dividing it into shorter marker intervals, where exchange histories are less ambiguous, and summing appropriately estimated map distances.

For a simple quantitative illustration, suppose the two intervals A–B and B–C are each estimated to be 12 cM using suitable short-interval data. The map distance from A to C is approximately 24 cM if B lies between them. If a particular set of gametes has observed recombination fractions 0.12 in both intervals and events are independent for the purpose of a simple calculation, the outer markers are recombinant when exactly one of the intervals has an odd endpoint switch. The probability is 0.12(0.88) + 0.88(0.12) = 0.2112, not 0.24. This difference arises because gametes recombinant in both intervals are nonrecombinant at the outer endpoints. The example is a simplified endpoint model, not an assertion that all real crossovers are independent.

The data analysis should distinguish precision from validity. Increasing the number of asci reduces random sampling error when observations are appropriately independent. It does not repair misclassified markers or a mapping model systematically affected by many higher-order crossovers. Conversely, a well-designed small sample may have valid assumptions but wide uncertainty. A strong experimental conclusion requires attention to both sources of error. In the examination, a question that doubles the sample size while preserving class proportions should leave the point estimate unchanged but generally improve sampling precision, all else being equal.

Integrated problems: choosing the correct observable

The first task in a tetrad problem is to determine what has been observed. An ordered octad records lineage order; an unordered tetrad records the combinations of alleles within a family; a set of independent progeny records endpoint genotypes without family structure. Although the same letter symbols can occur in all three datasets, the appropriate calculation changes. The following worked cases are illustrative, not measurements from published experiments.

Case one concerns a single marker in ordered asci. There are 420 ordinary asci showing 4:4 in the first-division arrangement, 48 showing 2:2:2:2 and 32 showing 2:4:2. A further ten asci have nonstandard allele counts. First calculate the denominator for the ordinary centromere estimate: 420 + 48 + 32 = 500. The SDS count is 80. The half-SDS distance is 50(80/500) = 8 cM. The abnormal fraction among all scored asci is 10/510, approximately 1.96 percent. These percentages answer two different questions. Using 510 as the denominator of the ordinary centromere equation would mix families whose patterns cannot be classified by the model. Reporting only the 8 cM result would conceal the abnormal families.

Case two involves two markers in a cross with parental haplotypes Ab and aB. The observer records 120 families with two Ab and two aB, 15 with two AB and two ab, and 65 with one of each haplotype. The first class is PD, the second NPD and the third T. Total families equal 200. The observed recombinant fraction is (15 + 32.5)/200 = 0.2375. Thus 23.75 percent of the recovered products carry nonparental haplotypes. The Perkins estimate is 50(65 + 6×15)/200 = 38.75 cM, if its assumptions are suitable. Had the observer assumed that AB and ab were parental, the class assignments would have reversed, leading to a very different and inappropriate result. Identify parental phase before performing arithmetic.

Case three compares three pairwise maps from a linked chromosome. Short intervals give approximately 7 cM between A and B, 11 cM between B and C, and an outer distance near 18 cM. The sum 7 + 11 = 18 supports order A–B–C, up to reversal, provided the three measurements are comparable and approximately additive. Suppose the outer observed recombinant proportion is only 0.16. This need not invalidate the order. Endpoint recombination can be smaller than map length because multiple exchanges restore parental endpoint associations. One should distinguish an observed fraction from a model-corrected map distance before declaring the measurements inconsistent.

Case four is designed to reveal an improper assumption about ditypes. A pair of genes on different chromosomes both lie very near their respective centromeres, so both markers are nearly always FDS. Across a large collection of asci, the geneticist observes approximately equal numbers of PD and NPD and almost no T. A student who memorized the ratio 1:1:4 for unlinked genes might conclude that the genes must be linked. Yet the geometry predicts precisely this near-absence of tetratypes. Both allele pairs separate in the first division, and the two independent homolog pairs may orient in either of two relative ways, producing the two ditypes. Unlinked does not mean that all four haplotypes must occur in every meiosis.

Case five examines centromere inference in an unordered organism. Two markers X and Y are known to lie on different chromosomes, and X is essentially centromere-linked, showing negligible SDS. If the marker Y shows SDS in some meioses, those families should produce tetratypes when X is FDS under ordinary independent assortment. Suppose the unordered data have T = 36 among 200 families. With the stated assumptions, the frequency of SDS at Y is approximately 0.18, giving an estimated Y-centromere distance of 9 cM. This is indirect mapping based on a reference marker and model. It does not rely on visual ordering of the four yeast spores. If X itself has appreciable SDS, the approximation changes and the tetratype fraction cannot simply be equated to the SDS frequency of Y.

Case six concerns a yeast marker A/a and a region flanked by L/l and R/r. A family yields LAR, LaR, lar and lar. The central allele has a 1:3 segregation while both flanking markers have ordinary 2:2 counts. No exchange of L–R association is visible. This is consistent with conversion of A to a in one lineage without a detectable reciprocal exchange across the flanking interval. A claim that all four chromosomes are recombinant would be mistaken. Another family with LAr, LaR, lAR and lar has to be checked locus by locus rather than assigned by visual appearance. Check the central locus directly: LAr and lAR carry A, while LaR and lar carry a, so the central count is 2:2. Counting L gives two L and two l. Counting R gives two R and two r. The observed outer haplotypes Lr, LR, lR and lr constitute a tetratype. This deliberate example illustrates the value of tabulating allele counts instead of relying on an apparent similarity to a previous family.

Case seven compares two mutant backgrounds. In a hypothetical experiment, the reference strain produces 6:2 octads at a frequency of 3 percent and 5:3 octads at a frequency of 1 percent. A second strain produces 6:2 at 1 percent and 5:3 at 4 percent. One interpretation is that a change in mismatch processing has reduced the proportion of intermediates resolved before postmeiotic replication. However, the observations alone do not establish that all recombination initiation rates were identical or that one specific mismatch repair protein is responsible. The stronger experimental design would include independent evidence for the genotype, comparable recovery of asci, sufficient sample size, and measurements that distinguish intermediate formation from processing. The inference is a mechanistic hypothesis consistent with the shift in genetic outcomes.

Case eight addresses the direction of gene conversion. In a collection of 1000 completely scored octads, 30 have six A and two a spores, and 30 have two A and six a spores. The other 940 each have four of each, with no additional abnormal events stipulated. The proportion of octads with completed conversion-like segregation is 6 percent. Yet the total number of A spores is 30(6) + 30(2) + 940(4) = 4000. The total number of a spores is also 4000. This dataset supports frequent local nonreciprocal changes without an aggregate directional preference. If instead 40 octads favour A and 20 favour a, with 940 ordinary families, A would be counted 4040 times out of 8000 spores, or 50.5 percent. A directional asymmetry may be biologically meaningful even though the overall departure from half is modest. Its statistical reliability would depend on the sample and controls.

Across these problems, the same reasoning discipline applies. Identify the organism and the observation type; determine parental phase; verify ordinary segregation where it is required; classify complete families; state which denominator is used; select the estimator that answers the question; and interpret the result within its assumptions. When a dataset provides more information than the chosen formula needs, examine that information before drawing a final conclusion. An apparent exception may be precisely the evidence that makes the problem scientifically interesting.

Biological significance and limits of genetic inference

The methods developed in this chapter show how the physical organization of a biological system determines the kind of genetic evidence that can be collected. An ascus retains relationships among meiotic products that are usually lost in an ordinary progeny population. An ordered ascus retains an additional relationship: the products can be assigned to the two divisions. These are separate advantages. It is possible to have a complete meiotic family without division-order information, and it is possible to have a visually ordered set of spores without assurance that the order represents divisions. A good experimental description states which kind of information has actually been preserved.

Tetrad analysis is not confined to a single species or to pigment mutants. Its underlying logic applies whenever the four meiotic products can be recovered and identified as one family. Chlamydomonas, a unicellular green alga, has also provided useful genetic systems for analyzing the products of meiosis. Plants can sometimes preserve pollen products in tetrads, and genetic approaches can exploit such family relationships. These examples are relevant because they separate the experimental principle from the particular morphology of a Neurospora ascus. In each case the researcher must establish the developmental origin and genotype of the products before applying a tetrad model.

There is also a fundamental distinction between a marker that records chromosome position and a gene whose molecular function is being investigated. A marker with no known effect on fitness can be extremely useful for mapping if it distinguishes parental homologs. Conversely, a gene with a well-characterized biochemical function may be useless as a marker in a particular cross if both parents carry the same allele. Marker informativeness depends on variation and reliable detection, not necessarily on whether the encoded protein is important. The next chapter will extend this principle to molecular markers in organisms where intact meiotic products are not readily available.

Tetrad data are useful for studying genetic mutants that alter recombination. Suppose a variant is hypothesized to decrease the number of reciprocal exchanges. In a short linked interval, the simplest prediction is an increased fraction of PD and a decreased fraction of T relative to an otherwise comparable reference cross. NPD may also become less frequent if double exchanges decline. Yet that pattern alone does not tell us whether fewer breaks were initiated, more intermediates were repaired without exchange, or specific recombinant classes were less viable. The experimental comparison should distinguish the predicted genetic endpoint from the proposed molecular cause.

A second kind of mutant might affect chromosome segregation without directly changing the probability of exchange. Such a mutant could change the fraction of complete viable tetrads, the ploidy of surviving spores or the frequency of unexpected marker counts. If only normal four-spore asci are retained, the remaining sample may underestimate the mutant's effect on meiosis. The appropriate first analysis is the distribution of product recovery and ordinary segregation, followed by a mapping calculation on the eligible families. An inability to calculate a standard map for some families may itself be a biologically relevant observation.

A third example concerns the interpretation of mismatch repair. A genetic background that increases postmeiotic segregation but decreases completed conversions could be consistent with reduced processing of mismatches. The total number of detectable aberrant families, however, need not remain constant, since restoration events are ordinarily invisible at the marker. In addition, DNA synthesis and the formation of heteroduplex regions can vary. Thus the observed ratio of 5:3 to 6:2 octads estimates the relative outcomes among the detected events, not the probability of repair among every molecular intermediate that formed. This is an important distinction between a measured phenotype and a hidden mechanistic parameter.

The same distinction applies to the measurement of crossover interference. Tetrad classes from one two-marker interval can support an estimate of double exchanges under specified assumptions. They cannot by themselves reveal how exchanges are spaced across the whole chromosome. Interference is a relationship between events in neighbouring intervals or positions. Examining several markers allows a more direct comparison of exchange occurrence across adjacent regions, much like the three-point mapping logic studied in the preceding chapter. Chromatid interference instead concerns the distribution of participating chromatids. Different experimental designs are needed to address the two kinds of dependence.

Even a map with very precise genetic distances does not establish physical distances or the locations of all intervening genes. Imagine two markers separated by 10 cM in two different strains. One interval may span more DNA than the other because structural variation or regional crossover frequency differs. Conversely, two nearby DNA markers can be separated by an unexpectedly large genetic interval if exchange is common in their region. Genetic maps record a functional property of meiosis; physical maps record sequence position. Both can be valuable without being interchangeable.

A rigorous interpretation can be summarized as a hierarchy of evidence. The parental genotypes determine phase. The intact family determines which products belong together. The observed alleles determine segregation classes. The model converts the class frequencies into a recombination or mapping estimate. Additional flanking markers and sequence observations test whether the assumed reciprocal-exchange mechanism explains the pattern. Each higher level depends on the reliability of the earlier ones. A numerical distance calculated from a questionable classification does not become valid merely because the arithmetic is correct.

For examination mastery, practice explaining each calculation in words before substituting the numbers. In a centromere problem the half factor represents recombinant chromatids within a simple SDS family. In a two-marker tetrad the half weight of T represents two recombinant members, while the full weight of NPD represents four. In the Perkins correction the sixfold coefficient of NPD derives from estimating the hidden double-exchange classes. In a conversion problem the counts are first used to test whether ordinary segregation is satisfied. These explanations reveal that the formulas are not interchangeable even when they share similar arithmetic.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Eight spores mean eight independent meioses — A Neurospora octad normally represents four meiotic products duplicated once. The mistake comes from counting bodies rather than lineages; when asked for sample size, count asci for family-level inference.

  2. 4:4 always means FDS — Ordinary 4:4 allele totals also occur in SDS. The visual run-length notation is easy to confuse with the aggregate ratio; inspect the two first-division halves.

  3. SDS means crossing over at meiosis II — Crossing over normally precedes the divisions. The name refers to when allele lineages separate; trace centromere association through meiosis I.

  4. FDS proves no exchange occurred anywhere — Distal exchanges or hidden multiple exchanges can yield FDS. The error is equating a marker's observed phase with all chromosome events; state the interval and model.

  5. SDS percent equals centromere distance — A simple SDS ascus contains two recombinant chromatids of four. Students often use the readily visible percent directly; halve it for the elementary estimate.

  6. Uppercase haplotypes are automatically parental — Phase comes from the actual parents, not allele notation or dominance. Crosses in repulsion reverse the identities; write parental haplotypes first.

  7. PD means two spores — Ditype refers to two genotype types among four products. The name can mislead; enumerate all four genotypes before calculating.

  8. T always demonstrates a single crossover — A tetratype can also arise from unlinked assortment or three-strand double exchange. Endpoint class is not event history; ask what assumptions are stated.

  9. NPD proves two crossovers in every situation — An ordinary linked interval in the elementary model can yield NPD through a four-strand double exchange, but unlinked markers also yield it. Verify linkage before applying the model.

  10. All unlinked genes give 1:1:4 — PD and NPD symmetry is the standard prediction, while T depends on centromere segregation. Memorizing a limiting ratio hides its assumptions; examine the SDS status.

  11. Perkins distance is the observed recombinant percentage — Perkins adds a correction for hidden exchanges. Both expressions use the same counts, but different weights; distinguish map length from observed fraction.

  12. Crossover interference equals chromatid interference — The former concerns occurrence or spacing of exchanges; the latter concerns selection of chromatids. The repeated word interference conceals different variables; identify the conditional probability.

  13. Genetic distance fixes base-pair distance — Recombination varies across the genome. Both maps describe position but in different units; never assume a universal bp/cM conversion.

  14. A 5:3 octad is just ordinary SDS — SDS retains four of each allele. A 5:3 ratio with discordant descendants supports postmeiotic segregation; inspect counts and pair identities separately.

  15. Gene conversion always causes a reciprocal crossover — Local copying can occur with or without crossing over. Students may conflate recombination outcomes; use flanking haplotypes to assess exchange separately.

  16. An abnormal allele ratio proves a new point mutation — Conversion, ploidy error, sampling and marker error are alternative explanations. A phenotype alone cannot identify sequence ancestry; seek complete-family and molecular evidence.

  17. A missing spore can be assigned to the less common allele — Its genotype is unknown unless independently observed. Filling the missing count to make 2:2 forces the model; report viability and missing data before mapping.

  18. A larger dataset cures every mapping problem — It can improve sampling precision, but cannot correct systematic misclassification or inappropriate exchange assumptions. Separate precision from validity.

Part B practice

Original practice questions. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

  1. What is a tetrad in the strict genetic sense?

    1. Four unrelated gametes
    2. Four diploid nuclei after fertilization
    3. Four products of one meiosis
    4. Eight independent meiotic products
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. The common origin is essential. Four products from different meioses are not a tetrad; an octad can represent mitotic duplication of four meiotic nuclei.

  2. Why is an ordinary Neurospora octad informative about a centromere?

    1. Its lineage order preserves the meiotic division partitions
    2. Its eight spores have eight independent centromeres
    3. Each allele is dominant
    4. It has no crossing over
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The linear arrangement reflects the succession of divisions. Unordered spore genotypes do not by themselves supply this direct partition information.

  3. Which sequence is ordinary FDS for marker A/a?

    1. AAaaAAaa
    2. AAaaaaAA
    3. AAAAAaaa
    4. AAAAaaaa
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The first four spores carry A and the last four a. The 2:2:2:2 and 2:4:2 arrangements have SDS patterns; a 5:3 count is aberrant rather than ordinary SDS.

  4. Which two ordinary octad patterns are SDS?

    1. 4:4 and 6:2
    2. 2:2:2:2 and 2:4:2
    3. 5:3 and 3:5
    4. 4:4 and 8:0
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. In both SDS patterns each first-division half has both alleles. The other ratios are ordinary FDS or aberrant segregation.

  5. When does the ordinary reciprocal crossover that produces SDS occur?

    1. Anaphase II
    2. Prophase I
    3. Postmeiotic mitosis
    4. After spore germination
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Crossing over occurs before meiotic segregation, typically during prophase I. SDS names the division when the alleles separate, not the timing of the exchange.

  6. Why is the elementary gene-centromere estimate half the SDS percentage?

    1. Half of asci are viable
    2. There are two homologous chromosomes in each haploid spore
    3. SDS is observed only in one mating type
    4. A simple SDS event makes two of four chromatids recombinant
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The factor follows from chromatid participation. Neither viability nor the number of divisions alone supplies the factor.

  7. For an AB/ab cross, which family is NPD?

    1. Ab Ab aB aB
    2. AB AB ab ab
    3. AB Ab aB ab
    4. AB AB AB ab
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The Ab and aB products both carry nonparental marker combinations in an AB/ab cross. AB and ab alone define PD; all four combinations define T; unequal single-locus segregation violates the ordinary classification.

  8. For a cross Ab/aB, which haplotypes are parental?

    1. AB and ab
    2. AB and Ab
    3. Ab and aB
    4. aB and ab
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Parental phase is set by the original homologs. Capitalization is not a criterion for phase.

  9. What is the recombinant chromatid count in an ordinary tetratype?

    1. 0
    2. 1
    3. 4
    4. 2
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Exactly two of the four possible marker haplotypes are parental for any fixed parental phase, and the other two are recombinant.

  10. Under ordinary independent assortment and unbiased recovery, which comparison is expected for unlinked loci?

    1. PD always zero
    2. T always zero
    3. PD = NPD
    4. NPD always greater than PD
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Independent orientation gives symmetry between the two ditypes. The frequency of T depends on the centromere segregation patterns.

  11. What can produce PD despite two crossovers within a linked interval?

    1. A two-strand double crossover
    2. A four-strand double crossover in the simple model
    3. A single reciprocal crossover
    4. An ordinary 3:1 conversion
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Two exchanges involving the same chromatids can restore parental endpoint associations; a four-strand double exchange yields NPD.

  12. Which double-crossover class produces NPD in the elementary linked-locus model?

    1. Two-strand
    2. Four-strand
    3. Three-strand
    4. Sister-only exchange outside the interval
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. All four chromatids acquire recombinant endpoint associations in a four-strand double exchange. The other ordinary double-exchange classes are PD or T.

  13. What is the expected two-, three- and four-strand double-crossover ratio without chromatid interference?

    1. 1:2:1
    2. 1:1:1
    3. 2:1:2
    4. 1:0:1
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Given a first exchange, four equally likely non-sister pair choices for the second produce one, two and one of the respective classes.

  14. Which expression gives the observed recombinant fraction for P, N and T?

    1. (P+T)/total
    2. (N+T/2)/total
    3. (6N+T)/total
    4. T/total
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. PD contributes zero, T contributes two out of four recombinant products, and NPD contributes four. The sixfold correction is used in the Perkins distance, not the observed fraction.

  15. Which expression gives the Perkins estimate in cM?

    1. 100(T+N)/total
    2. 50(P+T)/total
    3. 50(T+6N)/total
    4. 100N/total
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. The coefficient accounts for hidden double-exchange classes under the stated approximation. The other expressions misweight one or more classes.

  16. What is gene conversion?

    1. Any reciprocal chromosome exchange
    2. Failure of all homologs to segregate
    3. Loss of an entire ascus
    4. Nonreciprocal copying of sequence information between homologous DNA molecules
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Conversion changes local allele transmission and can accompany either crossover or noncrossover outcomes. Nondisjunction and loss of products are different mechanisms.

  17. A typical 6:2 octad with matching sister pairs suggests which event?

    1. Ordinary FDS
    2. Ordinary SDS
    3. Four independent meiotic divisions
    4. A completed conversion at the scored locus
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The 6:2 ratio represents 3:1 among the four product lineages under the simple model. FDS and SDS retain four of each allele.

  18. An informative heteroduplex persists into postmeiotic replication. Which outcome is especially diagnostic?

    1. Ordinary 4:4 with matching pairs
    2. PD at every possible locus
    3. 5:3 octad with a discordant sister pair
    4. A diploid cell before replication
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Different strands can template different alleles in two descendants. A normal paired 4:4 observation is not direct evidence of this event.

  19. Which is a genetic map unit?

    1. Base pair
    2. Centimorgan
    3. Micrometre
    4. Dalton
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. A centimorgan measures map length inferred from meiotic exchange. It has no universal fixed conversion to base pairs.

  20. Why record incomplete asci separately?

    1. Because missing products may bias recovered tetrad classes
    2. To make all distance estimates larger
    3. Because all incomplete asci are NPD
    4. Because missing products prove conversion
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Genotype-dependent death or scoring failure can alter the sample. Unknown products must not be assigned arbitrary allele states.

Part C practice

Original practice questions. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

  1. Of 250 ordinary octads, 40 are 2:2:2:2 and 20 are 2:4:2. What is the estimated gene–centromere distance?

    1. 24 cM
    2. 12 cM
    3. 8 cM
    4. 60 cM
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. SDS = 60, fraction = 60/250 = 0.24. Half the percentage is 12 cM. Twenty-four percent is the SDS frequency, not the estimated distance.

  2. An experiment records 160 FDS, 30 SDS and 10 aberrant octads. Excluding aberrant families for mapping, what is the estimate?

    1. 7.5 cM
    2. 15.79 cM
    3. 5 cM
    4. 7.89 cM
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The eligible total is 190, not 200. The estimate is 50×30/190 = 7.89 cM; the abnormal fraction among all 200 is 5%.

  3. For P=72, N=8, T=20, which pair gives recombinant percentage and Perkins distance?

    1. 18%, 34 cM
    2. 28%, 34 cM
    3. 18%, 18 cM
    4. 34%, 18 cM
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The endpoint percentage is 100(8+10)/100=18. Perkins gives 50(20+48)/100=34 cM. The estimates answer different questions.

  4. For a linked interval, P=180, T=20, N=0. What follows?

    1. The observed r is 10%
    2. No exchange occurred
    3. The estimated map distance is 5 cM, and no NPD was observed
    4. The map length is exactly known
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Twenty tetratypes give 40 recombinant chromatids among 800, or 5%; Perkins also yields 5 cM. A zero count does not establish absence of double exchanges.

  5. If P=70, T=10, N=20, why should the elementary Perkins reconstruction be scrutinized?

    1. The total is not 100
    2. T is always greater than P
    3. The chromosome must be circular
    4. T−2N is negative
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The reconstruction estimates singles as T−2N = −30, which is impossible as an event count. The model, phase, sampling and interval length must be examined.

  6. Two loci have 8 and 12 cM elementary distances to the same centromere. A reliable pairwise map gives 20 cM. Which geometry is most consistent?

    1. Same arm, 4 cM apart
    2. Separate chromosomes proven
    3. Opposite arms
    4. Identical physical position
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Opposite-arm lengths add to 20 cM. Same-arm lengths would differ by about 4 cM under the model; physical identity is not established.

  7. Two loci on different chromosomes are almost always FDS. What is the predicted approximate PD:NPD:T ratio?

    1. 1:1:0
    2. 1:1:4
    3. 4:1:1
    4. 0:0:1
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Independent first-division orientations generate the two ditypes equally; without SDS there are no tetratypes in the elementary model.

  8. Two independently behaving unlinked loci have SDS probabilities 0.4 and 0.2. Under independent second-division orientations, expected T frequency?

    1. 0.60
    2. 0.48
    3. 0.52
    4. 0.08
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Exactly one SDS has probability 0.4×0.8+0.6×0.2=0.44; both SDS adds 0.5×0.08=0.04. Total 0.48.

  9. A reference marker X on a different chromosome is essentially FDS. Among 300 unordered tetrads with marker Y, 90 are T. Estimate Y-centromere distance under the reference model.

    1. 15 cM
    2. 30 cM
    3. 45 cM
    4. Not calculable under any assumptions
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. With X nearly always FDS and independent assortment, T approximates Y SDS. Half of 30% gives 15 cM; this is indirect centromere mapping.

  10. Given parental ABC/abc, a family contains ABC, abc, AbC, aBc. What is the outer-marker AC classification?

    1. T
    2. PD
    3. NPD
    4. Conversion
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Ignoring B gives AC twice and ac twice, a parental ditype. Internal B reveals changes consistent with a two-strand double exchange, while ordinary 2:2 counts are retained.

  11. In an AB/ab linked cross a researcher swaps the parental phase labels. Which metric is directly inverted?

    1. Number of T families
    2. Total family count
    3. PD and NPD identities
    4. Number of meiotic divisions
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Swapping phase exchanges parental and nonparental ditypes. The tetratype class remains all four combinations; r becomes 1−r under the ordinary complete classification.

  12. A marker gives 30 octads of 6A:2a and 30 of 2A:6a, plus 940 ordinary 4:4 families. What can be concluded?

    1. A is favoured overall
    2. a is favoured overall
    3. There were no conversions
    4. Nonreciprocal events occur without net directional imbalance in this dataset
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. A totals 4000 of 8000 spores, but 60 families have completed conversion-like counts. Frequency and direction of conversion are distinct.

  13. A family from LAR/lar is LAR, LaR, lar, lar. Which interpretation fits best?

    1. Ordinary tetratype at L and R
    2. Four-strand double crossover at outer markers
    3. Nondisjunction proven
    4. Conversion-like 1:3 at A with parental L–R phase
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The central marker counts one A and three a, while outer haplotypes LR and lr occur twice each. It supports local nonreciprocal change without detected flanking crossover.

  14. A mutation decreases 6:2 and increases 5:3 octads. Which hypothesis is supported but not proved?

    1. All crossing over occurs at meiosis II
    2. Every mutant spore is diploid
    3. Mismatch processing is altered
    4. The centromere disappears
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Persistent heteroduplex can increase PMS while completed conversions decline. Intermediate formation and product recovery must also be controlled.

  15. Which additional observation best distinguishes a local conversion from a new mutation with the same phenotype?

    1. More photographs of the phenotype
    2. Sequence identity with the parental homolog across a local tract
    3. Total chromosome length alone
    4. The number of unrelated colonies
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Copying recognizable homologous sequence is more directly supported by molecular ancestry than by a shared phenotype, which different mutations can cause.

  16. For P=32, N=28, T=140, what is the chi-square statistic for conditional equal ditype frequencies?

    1. 0.267
    2. 4
    3. 60
    4. 140
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Ditype expectation is 30 and 30. Sum (2²/30)+(2²/30)=0.267. T counts are not part of this conditional symmetry statistic.

  17. Five observed ditypes are all PD. Under a two-sided exact 1:1 test, what is the probability of an equally extreme result?

    1. 1/32
    2. 5/32
    3. 1/16
    4. 1/2
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. All PD and all NPD are equally extreme, each with probability (1/2)^5. Their total is 1/16 = 0.0625.

  18. Two crosses have equal class proportions; one has four times as many independent asci. What changes in a simple sampling model?

    1. The standard error approximately halves
    2. The point estimate doubles
    3. The recombinant percentage quadruples
    4. The physical distance necessarily changes
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The standard error of a sample proportion or family mean scales as 1/√n. Identical proportions give identical point estimates.

  19. In a short interval, a variant increases PD and reduces T but many more spores are inviable. Which inference is justified?

    1. Crossover initiation is certainly reduced
    2. All dead spores were recombinant
    3. Gene conversion is impossible
    4. The recovered-class distribution changed, but recovery bias must be assessed
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The observed class shift may reflect altered exchange or preferential loss. A viability assessment is needed before choosing the causal explanation.

  20. A is proximal to B on the same arm. Under a single-crossover-only model, which ordinary pattern contradicts that simple geometry?

    1. FDS for both
    2. SDS A, FDS B
    3. FDS A, SDS B
    4. SDS for both
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. An exchange between centromere and A also lies between centromere and B, so both would be SDS. Multiple exchanges or a different geometry can explain the exceptional pattern.

  21. In the illustrated mapping graph (100 asci, T = 20), NPD rises from 2 to 5. How do the two estimates change?

    1. Both increase by three percent
    2. Observed recombination rises by nine percentage points, Perkins length by three cM
    3. Observed recombination rises by three percentage points, Perkins length by nine cM
    4. Neither estimate changes
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. The observed recombinant percentages are 12 and 15; the model-based lengths are 16 and 25 cM. Subtraction gives the stated changes. The other choices confuse two distinct units or incorrectly ignore NPD.

  22. Consider these statements: I. Every complete ordinary tetratype contains two recombinant products. II. A 5:3 ordered octad necessarily indicates normal SDS. III. A large PD excess over NPD supports linkage when ordinary segregation and unbiased recovery apply. Which are correct?

    1. I and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. II and III only
    4. All three
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. I follows from the four haplotypes of a tetratype. II is false because ordinary SDS preserves 4:4 allele counts and 5:3 is consistent with postmeiotic segregation. III is the standard linkage inference under the specified assumptions.

SET and mixed practice

Original practice questions. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

  1. Assertion: A normal 4:4 allele count proves FDS. Reason: SDS patterns can also contain four spores of each allele.

    1. Both true, reason explains
    2. Both true, reason does not explain
    3. Both false
    4. Assertion false, reason true
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Total allele number is not the same as lineage order; 2:2:2:2 and 2:4:2 both preserve 4:4 totals.

  2. Assertion: An NPD is always evidence of physical linkage. Reason: Unlinked markers can produce NPD by independent first-division orientation.

    1. Assertion false, reason true
    2. Both true
    3. Assertion true, reason false
    4. Both false
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. NPD can occur in linked and unlinked crosses. Linkage is inferred from the class distribution and assumptions, not the presence of one NPD.

  3. Assertion: Gene conversion can accompany a crossover. Reason: Local allele copying and flanking reciprocal exchange are distinguishable outcomes of recombination.

    1. Both true, reason unrelated
    2. Assertion true, reason false
    3. Both true, reason explains
    4. Assertion false, reason true
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Recombination intermediates can yield local copying with or without exchange of flanking markers, so these outcomes are not mutually exclusive.

  4. Which study design most directly tests whether a 5:3 sequence reflects a discordant postmeiotic lineage?

    1. Pool all spores before genotyping
    2. Retain ordered octad identities and score each adjacent pair
    3. Observe one random colony only
    4. Measure colony diameter without identifying genotypes
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Preserving the ordered pairs allows the investigator to identify different alleles in descendants of the same product. Pooling discards the needed information.

  5. A family from AB/ab contains AB, AB, Ab, ab. Which is the correct first step?

    1. Record aberrant A segregation before ordinary mapping
    2. Classify as PD because AB repeats
    3. Classify as T because three types are visible
    4. Count it as NPD
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: A. There are three A and one a; B segregates 2:2. The ordinary three-class derivation assumes both loci segregate 2:2.

  6. In 1000 complete octads, 40 show 6A:2a and 20 show 2A:6a. The rest are ordinary. What proportion of all spores is A?

    1. 50.0%
    2. 50.5%
    3. 52.0%
    4. 60.0%
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: B. A count = 40×6+20×2+940×4=4040, and total spores =8000. Thus A is 50.5%.

  7. Which set of observations can directly reveal a reciprocal two-strand double crossover hidden from the outer markers?

    1. Only outer marker genotypes
    2. One unrelated phenotype
    3. An informative internal marker plus the full family
    4. Only the number of spores
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. An internal marker may reveal the two ancestry changes even if the outer haplotype remains parental; the family helps discriminate reciprocal exchange from conversion.

  8. Which statement about an apparent gene–centromere distance of 30 cM by the half-SDS formula is appropriate?

    1. It fixes the physical distance in base pairs
    2. It necessarily represents a highly accurate additive map length
    3. It proves the locus is on a different chromosome
    4. It should be treated cautiously because multiple exchanges may hide additional genetic distance
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The elementary estimate may saturate as chromatid assignments randomize and is most reliable for short intervals.

  9. Which observation separates crossover interference from chromatid interference?

    1. Both mean 6:2 conversion
    2. Both require unordered tetrads
    3. Chromatid interference is merely spore lethality
    4. Crossover interference concerns spacing or co-occurrence of events; chromatid interference concerns the participating chromatids
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The two forms of dependence concern different conditional probabilities. An event may alter the probability of another event without biasing chromatid selection.

  10. A researcher scores 30 complete asci, four spores each, with duplicate genotyping of each spore. Which count is the number of meiotic families?

    1. 120
    2. 240
    3. 30
    4. 60
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Each ascus represents one meiosis. There are 120 product genotypes and 240 assay measurements, not 240 independent meioses.

Revision and mastery

The central distinction is between the four-product meiotic family, its order when preserved, and the inherited allele combinations. Ordered octads report first- or second-division segregation; unordered tetrads report joint marker haplotypes. Nonstandard segregation can reveal local copying or persistence of heteroduplex DNA.
