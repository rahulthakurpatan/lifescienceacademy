1. From molecular interactions to the energetic logic of life

Imagine a cell in a nutrient solution. Glucose enters, proteins fold, chromosomes are copied, ions accumulate against concentration gradients, and damaged structures are replaced. Each event is a molecular transformation. Yet the cell neither creates energy from nothing nor escapes the universal laws of chemistry. It converts energy between chemical, electrical, mechanical and thermal forms, continuously exchanging energy and material with its environment. Understanding those conversions is the purpose of thermodynamics.

In Node 1.A.01 we explored atoms and bonds; in 1.A.02 we examined water and noncovalent interactions. Acid–base equilibria from 1.A.03 determine proton activities, while reaction dynamics from 1.A.04 explain rates and barriers. This node unites these ideas. Why does a reaction favour one direction? Why does it stop at a particular mixture? How can a cell drive a reaction that is otherwise unfavourable? How do electrons and ions carry useful free energy?

Picture two substances A and B. Even if B is energetically favoured, A might survive for years because of a large activation barrier. Conversely an enzyme may allow rapid interconversion without changing the ultimate equilibrium composition. Thermodynamics predicts direction, equilibrium and maximum work, not the speed or molecular route. Kinetics predicts how rapidly and by which mechanism a system changes. This distinction will follow us throughout the lesson.

Learning outcomes

  • Apply the four laws of thermodynamics and distinguish state functions from path-dependent heat and work.
  • Calculate enthalpy, entropy, Gibbs energy, chemical potential, reaction quotients, equilibrium constants and temperature effects.
  • Understand binding, protein stability, biochemical standard states, ATP coupling and non-equilibrium steady states.
  • Balance redox reactions, compare reduction potentials, calculate Nernst potentials and electrochemical transport energies.
  • Interpret bioenergetic experiments and solve original CSIR-NET Part B and C questions.

Reaction ΔG: direction now. Activation ΔG‡: kinetic barrier. K: equilibrium composition. Never substitute one for another.

2. Systems, variables, boundaries and constraints

Define the system before doing an energy calculation. The system is the portion of the universe selected for study; its surroundings are everything outside its boundary. A membrane may form the boundary, but the boundary may also be imaginary. A living cell is open because it exchanges matter and energy. A sealed flask in a water bath is closed because it exchanges heat but not matter. An ideal insulated sealed vessel would be isolated, exchanging neither.

SystemMatterEnergyExample
OpenExchangeExchangeCell, tissue, ecosystem
ClosedNo exchangeExchange possibleSealed reaction vessel
IsolatedNo exchangeNo exchangeIdeal insulated vessel

A thermodynamic state can be described by temperature T, pressure P, volume V, amounts ni and composition. A state function depends only on initial and final states, whereas a path function depends on how a change occurs. Internal energy U, enthalpy H, entropy S, Gibbs energy G and Helmholtz energy A are state functions. Heat q and work w describe transfers; a system does not contain heat or work as stored state quantities.

Extensive quantities scale with the amount of material: doubling identical portions doubles total U, H, S, G and V. Intensive quantities such as temperature, pressure, concentration and chemical potential do not double. Dividing by amount gives molar properties. Reaction energies are reported per mole of reaction as written, so doubling the balanced equation doubles its reaction energy but not an intensive potential.

Process constraints

Isothermal means constant temperature; isobaric means constant pressure; isochoric means constant volume; adiabatic means no heat transfer. Adiabatic compression can warm a gas. Isothermal expansion can require heat input. A reversible process is an ideal limiting path with infinitesimal driving forces and no dissipation; a reaction that is chemically reversible can nevertheless occur irreversibly when driven far from equilibrium.

Use T in kelvin, R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1, F = 96,485 C mol−1. At 298.15 K, RT ≈ 2.479 kJ/mol and 2.303RT ≈ 5.708 kJ/mol. Convert J and kJ before calculation. A volt equals a joule per coulomb. These simple dimensional checks prevent many examination errors.

3. The four thermodynamic laws and living systems

Zeroth law — temperature

If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. Temperature is therefore a measurable property that determines the direction of heat transfer. A thermometer operates by approaching thermal equilibrium with the sample. Equal temperature does not imply equal internal energy.

First law — conservation

ΔU = q + w; heat entering and work done on the system are positive.

Work performed by the system is negative using this chemistry convention. For expansion against external pressure, wPV = −∫PextdV; for constant external pressure, −PextΔV. Compression is positive work. Work may also be electrical, mechanical or associated with transport. A closed system absorbing 600 J of heat and performing 200 J work gains 400 J of internal energy. An isolated system has ΔU=0. The first law alone does not select a spontaneous direction.

Second law — entropy and direction

ΔStotal = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings ≥ 0 for an isolated composite.

For a spontaneous irreversible process the total increases; in the reversible limit it is unchanged. The system alone may lose entropy if the surroundings gain more. A cell can build organised structures by consuming chemical free energy, releasing heat and exchanging matter. Heat, concentration, voltage and chemical gradients are driving forces; irreversible fluxes dissipate available free energy while tending to erase those gradients. Open systems can continually renew them.

Third law — entropy reference

A pure perfect crystal with a unique ground-state arrangement has entropy tending to zero as T approaches absolute zero. Disordered crystals may have residual entropy. Absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite sequence of ordinary cooling operations. The reference permits absolute molar entropy tabulation by integrating reversible heat capacity over temperature and including phase transitions. Do not confuse S° with standard formation enthalpy: an element in its reference state has ΔfH°=0 by convention but typically has positive molar entropy at room temperature.

4. Enthalpy, reaction heat and calorimetry

Biochemical reactions frequently occur in aqueous solution under nearly constant pressure. Enthalpy simplifies the energy balance for these conditions.

H = U + PV; ΔH = qp at constant pressure when only PV work occurs.

At constant volume with only PV work, qv = ΔU. For an ideal-gas reaction at the same temperature, ΔH = ΔU + ΔngasRT. For aqueous condensed phases the PV correction is often small, not necessarily zero. If the system exports significant electrical work, one cannot simply equate total heat exchange with ΔH.

Negative ΔH is exothermic; positive ΔH is endothermic. Breaking a bond requires energy, forming a bond releases energy. Reaction enthalpy includes all bond changes, hydration, electrostatics and other interactions. Average gas-phase bond enthalpies are rough estimates for appropriate gas-phase reactions, not direct predictors of aqueous ATP hydrolysis or protein association.

Hess's law

Since H is a state function, the enthalpy of a net balanced reaction is the sum for any pathway connecting the same endpoints. Reverse a reaction to negate ΔH; multiply its equation to scale ΔH. If A→B releases 20 kJ/mol and B→C absorbs 8 kJ/mol, A→C releases 12 kJ/mol regardless of whether B can be isolated.

ΔrH° = ΣνiΔfH°i

ν is positive for products and negative for reactants. The standard formation enthalpy refers to formation of one mole of species from elements in their reference states. Standard conditions specify reference states, not the composition of a real cell.

Calorimetry and heat capacity

Heat capacity C is heat required per temperature increment under specified constraints; specific heat c is per mass and molar heat capacity is per mole. For small temperature intervals with nearly constant c, q=mcΔT. Heating 100 g of water by 2 K with c≈4.18 J g−1K−1 requires 836 J. In an ideal insulated calorimeter the reaction releases corresponding heat, including corrections for the apparatus. Differential scanning calorimetry follows excess heat capacity during transitions; isothermal titration calorimetry measures heat associated with binding or reaction at fixed temperature.

Kirchhoff's relation at fixed pressure is dΔH/dT=ΔCp. If ΔCp is approximately constant, ΔH(T2)=ΔH(T1)+ΔCp(T2−T1). Hydration and exposure of nonpolar surfaces during unfolding can lead to significant heat-capacity changes. Negative enthalpy by itself does not establish spontaneity; entropy also contributes.

5. Entropy from microstates to molecular assembly

Entropy is not merely visible disorder. A macrostate describes observable bulk properties; a microstate describes microscopic configurations compatible with those properties. For W equally probable microstates, Boltzmann's equation connects the two descriptions.

S = kB ln W; for arbitrary probabilities S = −kBΣpiln pi.

kB is Boltzmann's constant; pi is the probability of microstate i. Four distinguishable particles each occupying either of two compartments have 16 positional assignments. Only one puts all four on the left; six put exactly two on either side. With macroscopic numbers, configurations corresponding to mixing dominate overwhelmingly. No special mixing force is needed to explain this statistical tendency.

For reversible heat transfer, dS=δqrev/T. This computes a state-function change using a reversible reference path even if the actual path is irreversible. An ideal gas expanding freely into vacuum has q=0 and w=0, but its entropy rises. Compute the entropy using a reversible isothermal expansion: ΔS=nR ln(V2/V1). Doubling the volume of one mole increases entropy by R ln2≈5.76 J/K. Using actual irreversible q/T would incorrectly give zero.

Mixing, phase changes and absolute entropy

For ideal mixing of distinguishable species, ΔSmix=−RΣniln xi, where x is mole fraction. Equal mole fractions of two species give R ln2 per mole of mixture. Removing a partition between identical gases at equal temperature and pressure gives no macroscopic mixing entropy. For a reversible phase transition, ΔStr=ΔHtr/Ttr. Standard reaction entropy is ΣνS° using absolute molar entropies.

Biological ordering

Protein folding usually reduces chain conformational entropy. Water and counterions can be released into states with more accessible configurations; packing and hydration also change enthalpy. Binding often restricts translational, rotational and conformational freedom, but release of bound solvent or ions can compensate. Association is not universally entropy-unfavourable. The hydrophobic effect is temperature dependent, and its enthalpic and entropic contributions cannot be assigned one universal sign. Consider the whole system rather than the order of the protein alone.

6. Gibbs and Helmholtz energy: deriving spontaneity

Calculating total entropy for every reaction is cumbersome. At constant T and P, with a thermal reservoir and only PV work during spontaneous relaxation, ΔSsurr=−ΔHsys/T. Then −TΔStotal=ΔH−TΔS. This produces the Gibbs criterion.

G = H − TS; at constant T, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS.

For a closed system at fixed T and P with no externally driven non-PV work, a spontaneous change decreases G and equilibrium minimises G subject to constraints. A negative ΔG indicates a favourable direction, positive indicates the reverse, zero characterises equilibrium for an infinitesimal permitted reaction. The maximum reversible non-PV work delivered by the system at fixed T and P is −ΔG. Irreversible processes deliver less useful work. At fixed temperature and volume the analogous potential is Helmholtz energy A=U−TS. Choose the potential appropriate to the constraints.

ΔHΔSΔG sign at positive T if values constant
−+Negative at all T
+−Positive at all T
−−Negative below T=ΔH/ΔS
++Negative above T=ΔH/ΔS

Example: ΔH=−40 kJ/mol, ΔS=−100 J mol−1K−1. At 300 K, ΔG=−40+30=−10 kJ/mol; at 450 K, +5 kJ/mol. The crossing temperature is 400 K if both quantities remain constant. Real biomolecules may undergo transitions and heat-capacity changes, so extrapolation is conditional.

Standard Gibbs energy of formation ΔfG° refers to forming a mole of species from reference-state elements. Calculate ΔrG°=ΣνΔfG°. Formation Gibbs energy of an element in its reference state is zero by convention. Gibbs energy, like enthalpy, follows Hess's law.

7. Chemical potential, activity and biochemical reference states

The chemical potential μi is partial molar Gibbs energy: (∂G/∂ni) at fixed T, P and all other amounts. For a homogeneous multicomponent system dG=−S dT+V dP+Σμidni. A substance tends to transfer from higher to lower chemical potential under fixed temperature and pressure when no additional coupling or constraints intervene. Concentration is only a proxy for chemical potential.

μi = μi° + RT ln ai

Activity a is dimensionless. In an ideal dilute solution a≈c/c°, where c°=1 M for a concentration reference. More generally a=γc/c° with activity coefficient γ. Ionic strength, crowding and molecular interactions can alter activities. For an ideal gas a≈p/p° with p°=1 bar. Pure solids and liquids in their reference state have activity one. Water in dilute aqueous solution is often approximated as unity; in concentrated solution this may fail. Logarithms formally require dimensionless arguments.

For a reaction written ΣνiAi=0, reaction extent ξ gives dni=νidξ. Consequently ΔrG=(∂G/∂ξ)T,P=Σνiμi. This derivative gives the instantaneous free energy per mole of reaction. It is zero at equilibrium for a reaction able to proceed both ways; G itself need not be zero. If composition changes substantially during a finite reaction, integrate this derivative over ξ instead of assuming a constant reaction energy.

Biochemical transformed standard values

Chemical ΔG° and E° refer to chemical standard states. Biochemical ΔG°′ and E°′ commonly use a fixed pH, often pH 7, with a proton reservoir setting proton chemical potential. Biochemical reactants can be sums of protonation species. Ionic strength, magnesium and temperature also affect apparent values. Do not insert an additional proton activity in a transformed quotient when the proton contribution was already incorporated into the primed reference. Inspect tabulated reference conditions before combining numbers.

Because pH=−log10aH+, a one-unit rise changes proton chemical potential by −2.303RT. Proton-consuming reactions can therefore have different free energies in two compartments even if all other substrate activities are identical. This is where biological buffers connect directly with thermodynamics.

8. Chemical equilibrium and the reaction quotient

Consider aA+bB⇌cC+dD. Substitute each chemical potential into Σνμ. The resulting standard contribution is independent of current composition and the remaining logarithms combine into the reaction quotient.

Q = aCcaDd/(aAaaBb); ΔrG = ΔrG° + RT ln Q.

At equilibrium Q=K and actual reaction free energy is zero. Rearrangement gives the bridge between standard energy and equilibrium composition:

ΔrG° = −RT ln K; ΔrG = RT ln(Q/K).

ConditionActual ΔGNet direction
Q < KNegativeForward
Q = KZeroNo net conversion
Q > KPositiveReverse

At 298 K, K=100 gives ΔG°≈−11.42 kJ/mol. If current Q=1000, actual ΔG=RT ln10≈+5.71 kJ/mol, so reverse reaction is favoured despite the negative standard value. If Q=0.1, actual ΔG≈−17.12 kJ/mol. A reaction with positive ΔG° can similarly be driven forward by sufficiently low Q. Cells exploit product removal and substrate supply to control actual reaction direction.

Dynamic equilibrium

Microscopic forward and reverse events continue at equal rates. For ideal elementary A⇌B, kf[A]=kr[B] at equilibrium, giving K≈kf/kr with appropriate standard normalisation. Do not apply this simple ratio to arbitrary complex apparent rate laws. At true equilibrium each microscopic reversible elementary process satisfies detailed balance. A driven open cycle can maintain steady concentrations and nonzero net flux, which is not equilibrium.

9. Equilibrium composition, perturbations and temperature

Reaction algebra and mass balance

If A⇌B has K, the reverse has 1/K; doubling the balanced equation gives K². For A⇌B followed by B⇌C, the net constant is K1K2 because standard free energies add. These rules require compatible standard states and balanced net stoichiometry.

For ideal A⇌B with initial A=0.10 M and B=0, K=4, let x be the amount converted in concentration units. x/(0.10−x)=4 gives x=0.08 M. For A+B⇌AB with equal initial concentrations C and no complex, the concentration-based Kd=(C−x)²/x. Solve the quadratic and reject roots violating material balance. Free concentrations, not totals, belong in the quotient.

Le Châtelier through Q

Adding reactant usually reduces Q; adding product increases it. Removing product can pull a reversible reaction forward. For ideal-gas reactions, compression favours the side with fewer gas molecules when the reactive partial pressures increase together. Adding an inert gas at fixed volume and temperature does not change the reactive partial pressures; at constant pressure it can change them. Pure solid and liquid activities are one while their pure phases remain present. Conventional textbook Kp=Kc(RT)Δngas uses compatible unit-based numerical constants; rigorous dimensionless definitions also include standard-state factors.

van 't Hoff temperature dependence

d ln K/dT = ΔH°/(RT²); ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH°/R)(1/T₂−1/T₁) if ΔH° is constant.

For endothermic reactions K rises with temperature; for exothermic reactions K falls. Heating may increase both rates while shifting equilibrium in the endothermic direction. If K=10 at 298 K and ΔH°=+40 kJ/mol, then at 310 K ln(K₂/10)≈0.625, K₂≈18.7. Plotting ln K against 1/T gives slope −ΔH°/R and intercept ΔS°/R when both properties are approximately constant. Changes in ionic strength, protonation, solvent and phase can affect apparent equilibrium constants; always identify the fixed conditions.

Solubility and coupled equilibria

For a sparingly soluble solid MX⇌M++X−, Ksp=aM+aX−. Adding a common ion often lowers the amount that dissolves, while a ligand that complexes M can lower its free activity and promote dissolution. Likewise protonation can change the apparent solubility of weak acids or bases. The useful principle is to combine mass balance and the relevant equilibria, not to assume total dissolved concentration equals free ion activity.

10. Binding, folding and thermodynamic stability

For P+L⇌PL in an ideal dilute solution, Kd=[P][L]/[PL] has concentration units and the concentration association constant is 1/Kd. The dimensionless association constant is c°/Kd, so ΔG°bind=RT ln(Kd/c°). Lower Kd means more favourable standard binding energy.

For one independent site: θ = [L]free/(Kd+[L]free).

Free ligand equal to Kd gives 50% occupancy; nine times Kd gives 90%. A tenfold decrease in Kd makes ΔG° more favourable by about 5.71 kJ/mol at 298 K. If free ligand is 10 nM, occupancy is 50% for Kd=10 nM but approximately 1% for Kd=1 μM. When substantial ligand is bound, total ligand cannot replace free ligand; solve material balances. Affinity is not necessarily residence time: for elementary binding Kd=koff/kon, and both rates can vary.

For a two-state U⇌N folding reaction, Kfold=aN/aU, ΔG°fold=−RT ln Kfold and folded fraction=K/(1+K) in ideal dilute solution. At a reversible two-state melting midpoint K=1 and ΔG°=0 for those conditions. Intermediates, aggregation and oligomerisation can invalidate the model. A measured irreversible transition temperature is not necessarily an equilibrium melting point.

Allosteric proteins interconvert conformations with distinct binding affinities. A ligand can shift conformational populations because it binds different states with different free energies. The Hill equation can describe cooperative occupancy empirically, but the Hill coefficient is not generally the number of binding sites. Protein structure nodes will expand these mechanisms.

Heat capacity and folding curves

For approximately constant ΔCp, ΔH(T)=ΔH(T₀)+ΔCp(T−T₀) and ΔS(T)=ΔS(T₀)+ΔCpln(T/T₀). Substituting into ΔG=ΔH−TΔS gives curved stability profiles. Some proteins therefore undergo cold denaturation as well as heat denaturation. Always state whether ΔG describes folding or unfolding. The sign reverses when direction reverses.

11. ATP and the physical mechanism of energy coupling

Cells need uphill reactions for polymer synthesis, solute transport and mechanical motion. Energy coupling combines an unfavourable step and a favourable step through shared chemistry, a common intermediate or a conformational cycle. Free energies add for a balanced net reaction, but merely carrying out two reactions in the same vessel does not guarantee energy coupling.

Why ATP hydrolysis is favourable

ATP+H2O→ADP+Pi has conventional ΔG°′≈−30.5 kJ/mol at pH 7. The actual cellular value is often more negative because the ATP activity is maintained high relative to ADP and Pi. The net hydrolysis is favourable because of relative hydration, electrostatics, resonance and entropy of all species. Breaking a phosphoanhydride bond alone requires energy; it does not release a packet of energy.

For the transformed reaction, approximating water activity as one, ΔG′=ΔG°′+RT ln(aADPaPi/aATP). Suppose ATP=5 mM, ADP=0.5 mM, Pi=5 mM and ideal normalised activities. Q′=0.0005×0.005/0.005=0.0005. At 298 K RT ln Q′≈−18.84 kJ/mol, giving −49.3 kJ/mol. Intracellular values depend on pH, Mg²⁺, temperature and compartment; this example is not universal.

Glucose+Pi→glucose-6-phosphate+H2O has ΔG°′≈+13.8 kJ/mol. Adding ATP hydrolysis (−30.5) gives glucose+ATP→glucose-6-phosphate+ADP, ΔG°′≈−16.7 kJ/mol. Hexokinase actually transfers a phosphoryl group from ATP to glucose through its active site; it does not first hydrolyse ATP and then use released heat to phosphorylate glucose. This physical coupling is essential.

Activated carriers and phosphoryl-transfer potential

Phosphoenolpyruvate and 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate have larger standard phosphoryl-transfer potentials than ATP and can support substrate-level phosphorylation. ATP transfers phosphoryl groups to lower-potential acceptors. ATP cleavage to AMP+PPi coupled to PPi hydrolysis can strongly favour nucleotide and aminoacyl-tRNA synthesis. Removing PPi changes Q and adding its hydrolysis changes the net reaction energy. Individual microscopic reactions remain reversible in principle.

Adenylate energy charge=([ATP]+0.5[ADP])/([ATP]+[ADP]+[AMP]), ranging from zero to one. It is not identical to ATP hydrolysis energy, which depends on Pi and other activities. Adenylate kinase interconverts 2ADP⇌ATP+AMP. GTP, thioesters such as acetyl-CoA, and reduced carriers such as NADH have specific activated chemical roles.

If an uphill step costs +65 kJ/mol and ATP hydrolysis supplies −50 kJ/mol, one ATP gives +15; two give −35. This is a thermodynamic lower bound on integer stoichiometry, not evidence of a real two-ATP mechanism. A finite forward flux requires a driving force; the ideal reversible limit corresponds to zero net driving force.

12. Steady state, kinetics and dissipation

A living cell is an open non-equilibrium system. In steady state, concentrations can be approximately constant even while matter flows through them. For A⇌X⇌B, d[X]/dt=vin−vout=0 if influx equals outflux; both can be large. Maintaining A high and B low can sustain net flux. If input and removal stop the system relaxes toward equilibrium.

Metabolic steps with actual ΔG near zero respond strongly to mass action and may reverse when concentrations change. A strongly negative actual ΔG biases forward flux under prevailing conditions but does not forbid reverse microscopic events. Separate pathways can bypass such steps with different reaction coupling. Chemical dissipation contributes entropy production at a rate −ΔrG(dξ/dt)/T under appropriate isothermal conditions. Equilibrium has zero net flux and zero driving force. A perfectly impermeable barrier may preserve a gradient without continuous expenditure; a leaky barrier requires energy input to sustain one.

Catalysis and reaction coordinate

A catalyst changes activation barriers, not the free energies of the same initial and final states or their equilibrium constant. Transition-state theory approximately gives k=(kBT/h)exp(−ΔG‡/RT) for an elementary process under its assumptions. Activation entropy and enthalpy contribute to the barrier; they are different from reaction entropy and enthalpy. The forward and reverse barriers are related through the endpoint free energy difference. An enzyme can couple reactions or change the distribution of bound species, but cannot alter the equilibrium constant of the same free-species reaction at fixed conditions.

13. Oxidation, reduction and electron balance

In metabolism, controlled electron transfer is a central way to harvest free energy. Oxidation is loss of electrons or increase in oxidation number; reduction is gain of electrons or decrease in oxidation number. A reducing agent donates electrons and itself becomes oxidised. An oxidising agent accepts electrons and itself becomes reduced. Electron loss and gain must balance.

Oxidation numbers are formal bookkeeping values. Free elements have zero, monatomic ions have their charge, oxygen usually has −2 except peroxides and other exceptions, hydrogen usually +1 except metal hydrides. The sum equals the species charge. Formal oxidation number is not necessarily the actual partial charge. In glucose the average oxidation number of carbon is zero, whereas in CO₂ carbon is +4. Complete oxidation of six carbons transfers 24 electrons formally; individual carbons in glucose have different oxidation numbers.

Balancing half-reactions

In acidic solution, balance atoms other than O and H, add water for O, H⁺ for H, then electrons for charge. Scale oxidation and reduction half-reactions to cancel electrons and add. For basic solution add OH⁻ to neutralise H⁺ and cancel water. When checking mass and charge use chemical equations; transformed biochemical notation can suppress proton participation.

Example: Fe²⁺→Fe³⁺+e⁻ and O₂+4H⁺+4e⁻→2H₂O. Multiply the iron half-reaction by four to obtain 4Fe²⁺+O₂+4H⁺→4Fe³⁺+2H₂O. Both sides have +12 total charge. Organic oxidation often gains bonds to oxygen or loses hydrogen, but use electron accounting when exceptions occur.

14. Reduction potentials and the energy of electron transfer

A reduction potential describes a half-reaction's electron-accepting tendency relative to a reference. The standard hydrogen electrode defines E°=0 V for 2H⁺+2e⁻⇌H₂ under chemical standard conditions. Only a potential difference is directly measurable. A more positive reduction potential indicates a stronger electron-accepting tendency under specified conditions. For two couples, electrons tend to flow from the lower actual reduction potential donor to the higher actual potential acceptor.

ΔE = Eacceptor − Edonor; ΔG = −nFΔE.

Here n is the number of electrons transferred per mole of the balanced overall reaction. Do not multiply E by stoichiometric coefficients: potential is intensive. Doubling the entire reaction doubles n and ΔG, not ΔE. For n=2 and ΔE=+0.20 V, ΔG=−38.6 kJ/mol. At equilibrium of the overall redox reaction ΔE=0, although individual half-cell potentials need not be zero.

Respiratory complexes transfer electrons via intermediate carriers and capture some energy in electrochemical gradients; actual available energy depends on activities, pH and oxygen. An electron donor's E alone does not establish the overall reaction's direction.

15. Nernst equation, concentration and pH effects

Combine ΔG=ΔG°+RT lnQ with ΔG=−nFE and ΔG°=−nFE° to obtain the concentration correction to voltage.

E = E° − (RT/nF)ln Q = E° − (2.303RT/nF)log₁₀Q.

At 25 °C the base-10 coefficient is 0.05916 V/n; at 37 °C it is about 0.0615 V/n. For Ox+ne⁻⇌Red, Q=aRed/aOx. Electrons are omitted from the chemical quotient. Increasing Red/Ox lowers the reduction potential; increasing Ox/Red raises it. For a complete balanced reaction construct its own Q, and use the electron count and potential difference consistent with that equation.

Example: a two-electron couple E°=+0.10 V, Red/Ox=100 at 298 K gives E=0.10−0.02958 log₁₀100=+0.04084 V. If Red/Ox=0.01, E=+0.15916 V. A four-decade change shifts the potential by 118.3 mV. When comparing two couples, calculate each actual reduction potential and then subtract donor from acceptor.

Proton-coupled electron transfer

For Ox+mH⁺+ne⁻⇌Red, Q=aRed/(aOxaH+m). Expanding the logarithm gives E=E°−(RT/nF)ln(Red/Ox)−(2.303RT/F)(m/n)pH. At 298 K a pH-unit increase lowers E by 59.16(m/n) mV if other activities and proton stoichiometry remain fixed. If E°′ refers to pH 7, apply the shift relative to 7, not absolute pH, under the same simple stoichiometry. Changes in protonation can alter apparent slopes.

For O₂+4H⁺+4e⁻→2H₂O, m/n=1. At fixed oxygen and water activities, pH rise lowers E by about 59 mV/unit. This explains the difference between chemical standard oxygen reduction potential near +1.23 V and pH 7 value near +0.82 V. A tenfold decrease in oxygen activity lowers the potential by about 14.8 mV for n=4. Oxygen depletion may still have large kinetic effects despite the logarithmic potential dependence.

ln K = nFΔE°/(RT); at 298 K log₁₀K = nΔE°/0.05916 V.

A two-electron reaction with ΔE°=+0.11832 V gives log₁₀K=4, K=10⁴. Reversing the reaction negates ΔE and ΔG and inverts K. Doubling the reaction leaves potential unchanged, doubles n and ΔG, and squares K. These consistency checks are useful in Part C.

16. Electrochemical potential and membrane gradients

Charged solutes have both chemical and electrical contributions to their potential. For ion i, μ̃i=μi°+RT ln ai+ziFψ, where z is signed ionic valence and ψ is voltage. For transfer between compartments with the same reference solvent:

ΔG1→2 = RT ln(a₂/a₁) + zF(ψ₂−ψ₁).

A positive ion favours a more negative voltage; a negative ion favours a more positive voltage. For neutral solutes z=0. Chemical and electrical terms may act together or oppose. A membrane pathway is required for actual movement. A gradient can be energetically favourable to dissipate but persist when the membrane is impermeable.

At equilibrium for a permeant ion, ΔG=0. For inside-minus-outside voltage, Eion=(RT/zF)ln(aout/ain). For K⁺ with 140 mM inside and 5 mM outside at 37 °C, EK=61.5 log₁₀(5/140) mV≈−89 mV. If actual inside voltage is −70 mV, outward potassium movement is favoured in a K-permeable membrane. At a more negative voltage than EK, inward movement is favoured. For Cl⁻, use z=−1. A real membrane permeable to several ions need not sit at any single ion's equilibrium voltage.

Proton motive force

Define Δψ=ψin−ψout and ΔpH=pHin−pHout. For inward proton movement, ΔG=FΔψ−2.303RTΔpH. Define Δp=Δψ−(2.303RT/F)ΔpH, so ΔG=FΔp. When inside is negative and alkaline both terms favour inward movement. At 298 K Δψ=−0.150 V and ΔpH=+0.5 give Δp≈−0.1796 V and ΔG≈−17.3 kJ/mol per proton. An ATP synthesis cost of +50 kJ/mol could be covered by three protons in this ideal energy calculation; actual stoichiometry and associated transport costs are determined by molecular machinery.

Secondary active transport couples downhill ion movement to uphill solute movement. Symport transfers both in the same direction; antiport in opposite directions. Calculate each transfer energy using its own destination, signed charge and stoichiometry, then add. Primary pumps can couple uphill transport to ATP hydrolysis, light or redox chemistry.

17. Bioenergetic integration, redox buffering and measurements

Trace electrons from a nutrient to a final acceptor. Dehydrogenases can reduce NAD⁺ to NADH. Electron transfer toward oxygen has a favourable free-energy difference under aerobic conditions. Respiratory protein complexes capture part of that drop to pump protons, generating an electrochemical gradient. ATP synthase uses the inward proton flux to couple ATP formation to gradient dissipation. The specific complexes and mechanisms belong to later metabolism nodes; here the principle is conversion of one free-energy gradient into another.

Anaerobic pathways may use other final acceptors; fermentation can regenerate NAD⁺ by reducing an organic intermediate. The energetics depend on the actual donor and acceptor and coupling, not merely oxygen presence. Photosynthetic systems use light as external energy to generate charge separation and drive electron transfer that would otherwise be unfavourable. Light-driven transformations obey conservation and entropy constraints.

Redox buffering and compartmentation

For GSSG+2H⁺+2e⁻⇌2GSH at fixed pH, Q′ includes aGSH²/aGSSG. The glutathione potential depends on absolute concentrations as well as their ratio. Increasing GSH tenfold at fixed GSSG lowers E by about 59 mV at 298 K; increasing GSSG tenfold at fixed GSH raises E by about 29.6 mV. Thiol systems, thioredoxin and NADPH-linked reactions help maintain redox states. Compartments can have different redox potentials and pH because membranes restrict exchange and enzymes maintain distinct pools. One cannot assume all cellular couples are mutually at equilibrium.

Experimental interpretation

Isothermal titration calorimetry measures binding heats and, under a binding model, affinity; compute ΔG° from K and ΔS°=(ΔH°−ΔG°)/T. Differential scanning calorimetry measures heat capacity changes across a transition. A van 't Hoff plot estimates enthalpy from equilibrium constants across temperatures, with assumptions. Redox potentiometry measures voltage relative to a reference electrode; spectroscopy can track oxidation state. Disagreement between calorimetric and van 't Hoff enthalpy can reflect linked proton uptake, intermediates, temperature-dependent enthalpy or model failure. These techniques measure different observables.

Efficiency and limits

If an oxidation provides 220 kJ/mol of available Gibbs energy and ATP synthesis costs 50 kJ/mol, four ATP store 200 kJ/mol in this ideal integer comparison; five require 250, exceeding the available energy. Actual yields can be fractional averages over many cycles and depend on coupling stoichiometry, transport, leakage and conditions. Use actual ATP synthesis cost when given, not the standard hydrolysis value. Thermodynamics imposes a maximum, not a guarantee of complete conversion into useful work.

Spontaneous does not mean fast. A negative reaction ΔG may coexist with a large activation barrier. Exothermic does not mean spontaneous. Entropy and temperature matter. Cellular entropy need not increase alone. The second law concerns the isolated composite.

Equilibrium does not mean equal concentrations. Q=K. At equilibrium ΔG° need not be zero. Actual reaction ΔG is zero. A steady state is not equilibrium. It can maintain flux. Enzymes do not change K for the same defined reaction. They change rates or enable coupling.

Breaking ATP's bond does not itself release energy. Net hydrolysis is favourable. Redox potential does not scale with electron stoichiometry. n does. A positive E is insufficient to infer electron transfer direction. Compare both actual couples. Do not memorise a concentration ratio without writing the reaction. Redox and membrane Nernst equations follow from different expressions.

Solving analytical questions

  1. Write the reaction or transfer direction; balance mass, charge and electrons.
  2. Identify temperature, pressure, pH, standard versus actual values and voltage convention.
  3. Build Q from free activities and balanced stoichiometry.
  4. Convert units and predict signs before calculating magnitudes.
  5. Check limiting cases: Q=K implies ΔG=0; higher Ox/Red increases E for a simple reduction; positive charge favours negative potential.
  6. Distinguish feasible from fast and require a physical coupling mechanism for uphill work.

19. Original CSIR-NET and SET Part B and Part C practice

Attempt these 32 original exam-style questions before opening their answers. They are not represented as previous-year questions. Part B tests precise conceptual distinctions; Part C requires multiple-step quantitative reasoning. Unless stated otherwise assume ideal activities and the constants defined above.

1. Part B · A system absorbs 500 J and does 120 J of work. ΔU?

A −620; B −380; C +380; D +620 J

Answer: C

q=+500, w=−120, total +380 J.

2. Part B · Which is a path function?

A H; B S; C G; D q

Answer: D

Heat depends on the process; H, S and G are state functions.

3. Part B · Which entropy statement is correct?

A Every subsystem gains entropy; B Calculate ΔS along a reversible reference path; C Adiabatic means ΔS=0; D A crystal has zero entropy at all T

Answer: B

Entropy is a state function; irreversible adiabatic changes can generate entropy.

4. Part B · ΔH<0 and ΔS>0, both constant. ΔG at positive T?

A Always negative; B Always positive; C Zero; D Never negative

Answer: A

Both contributions are negative.

5. Part B · K=1. What follows?

A Forward rate zero; B ΔG°=0; C Product absent; D Actual ΔG always zero

Answer: B

ln K=0; actual ΔG still depends on Q.

6. Part B · What changes K for a fixed ideal reaction?

A Catalyst; B Adding product; C Temperature; D Initial concentrations

Answer: C

Composition changes Q; temperature can change K.

7. Part B · Free ligand is 3K_d for one independent site. Occupancy?

A 0.25; B 0.50; C 0.75; D 0.90

Answer: C

3/(1+3)=0.75.

8. Part B · Reducing agent in A_red+B_ox→A_ox+B_red?

A A_red; B B_ox; C A_ox; D B_red

Answer: A

A_red donates electrons.

9. Part B · A half-reaction is tripled. Its E° becomes?

A E°/3; B 3E°; C −3E°; D E°

Answer: D

Potential is intensive.

10. Part B · Ox+2e⁻⇌Red: increasing Red/Ox does what?

A E rises; B E falls; C E unchanged; D E exactly zero

Answer: B

Nernst subtracts the positive log of Q.

11. Part B · Constant intermediates with continuous net metabolic flux describe:

A True equilibrium; B Non-equilibrium steady state; C Isolated reversible state; D No dissipation

Answer: B

Inflow and outflow can balance with net flux.

12. Part B · Why can ATP hydrolysis be favourable?

A Breaking a bond releases energy; B Products collectively have lower Gibbs energy under conditions; C ΔG always −30.5; D Catalyst changes K

Answer: B

Net free energy includes all species and depends on activities.

13. Part C · At 298 K ΔG°=+11.42 kJ/mol and Q=10⁻³. K and direction?

A 100 reverse; B 0.01 forward; C 0.01 reverse; D 100 forward

Answer: B

K≈exp(−11.42/2.479)=0.01; Q/K=0.1; ΔG≈−5.71 kJ/mol.

14. Part C · A⇌B K=10 and B⇌C K=0.02. Current C/A=0.02. Net K and direction?

A 0.2 forward; B 0.2 reverse; C 500 reverse; D 0.002 forward

Answer: A

K_net=10×0.02=0.2; Q<K. Kinetics may limit flux.

15. Part C · ΔH=−24 kJ/mol, ΔS=−80 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. ΔG at 280 and 320 K?

A +1.6, −1.6; B −1.6, +1.6; C −46.4, −49.6; D −24, −24

Answer: B

Convert entropy to −0.080 kJ/mol/K; −24+22.4=−1.6; −24+25.6=+1.6.

16. Part C · Exothermic ΔH°=−50 kJ/mol, K=20 at 300 K. Estimate K at 310 K.

A 38.2; B 20; C 10.5; D 0.05

Answer: C

ln(K₂/20)≈−0.647; K₂≈10.5. Heating shifts equilibrium away from exothermic direction.

17. Part C · Total P and L each 10 μM, K_d=5 μM. Fraction bound?

A 0.33; B 0.50; C 0.67; D 0.90

Answer: B

(10−x)²/x=5 gives x=5 or 20 μM; only 5 satisfies mass balance. Using total ligand gives an incorrect 0.67.

18. Part C · Mutation raises K_d from 10 nM to 1 μM at 298 K. ΔΔG°bind?

A −11.42; B +11.42; C +5.71; D zero kJ/mol

Answer: B

100-fold increase yields RT ln100=+11.42 kJ/mol, weakening binding.

19. Part C · ATP 2 mM, ADP 1 mM, Pi 10 mM, ΔG°′=−30.5 at 298 K. Actual hydrolysis ΔG′?

20. Part C · A +72 kJ/mol step is coupled to ATP hydrolysis of −46 per ATP. Minimum integer ATP for negative net ΔG?

One gives +26; two give −20. Actual machinery must permit this coupling.

21. Part C · Actual donor E=−0.18 V, acceptor +0.12 V, n=2. ΔG?

ΔE=+0.30 V, −2×96,485×0.30≈−57.9 kJ/mol.

20. Consolidation and transition to carbohydrates

We began with a cell that must transform energy to sustain order. The four laws provide the foundations; enthalpy and entropy combine into Gibbs energy; chemical potential introduces composition; the quotient Q and equilibrium constant K tell us how far a reaction is from equilibrium. ATP coupling connects favourable and unfavourable transformations physically. Reduction potentials express electron-transfer tendencies; the Nernst equation adjusts for composition and pH; electrochemical potential extends the same logic to membrane transport. Living cells maintain fluxes and gradients by operating away from equilibrium.

The next assigned topic is 1.B.01, Carbohydrate Chemistry and Structural Organisation. The energetics of sugar cyclisation, glycosidic bond formation and activated carbohydrate intermediates will build on this foundation. Protein structure, enzymes, metabolism and membrane physiology will return to the same free-energy principles at higher resolution.

Mastery checkpoint: derive ΔG = RT ln(Q/K), justify ATP hydrolysis without the bond-breaking misconception, derive the Nernst equation and predict ion direction from both chemical and electrical gradients.
