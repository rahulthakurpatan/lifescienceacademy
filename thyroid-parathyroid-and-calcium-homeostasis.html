Two endocrine problems in one anatomical region

A mammal must continuously adjust the rate at which its tissues use fuel while maintaining the extracellular ionic environment required for electrical activity. These are related but distinguishable tasks. A cell cannot respond appropriately to nutrients, cold or developmental signals if its expression of metabolic enzymes remains fixed. Equally, an excitable membrane cannot operate normally if extracellular calcium changes without restraint. In mammals, the thyroid and parathyroid glands contribute to these tasks through different chemical messengers. The thyroid follicular epithelium produces iodinated thyroid hormones; a separate population of parafollicular cells produces calcitonin. The parathyroid chief cells produce parathyroid hormone (PTH). Anatomical proximity is not evidence of a single endocrine axis.

Thyroid hormone is especially important in establishing the metabolic capacity of tissues over relatively long periods. A change in its concentration can change the abundance of mitochondrial proteins, ion pumps and other proteins that determine how rapidly chemical energy is processed. Calcium regulation, by contrast, must correct deviations that may arise in minutes. A sudden fall in ionized calcium is detected by the parathyroid gland without requiring a releasing hormone from the hypothalamus. The different organization of these control systems provides an opportunity to understand two designs of endocrine feedback: a hierarchical axis and a gland that directly samples its controlled variable.

The important distinction in this chapter is between a material and a signal. Iodine is a substrate from which thyroid hormones are constructed, whereas TSH is a signal controlling their manufacture. Calcium is a mineral that is transported, stored and used by tissues, whereas PTH conveys information about the availability of that mineral. Giving a cell more substrate does not necessarily increase the concentration of a circulating hormone indefinitely. Feedback, enzyme activity, transport capacity and losses can limit the response. Throughout the chapter, distinguish the compartment in which a reaction occurs, the molecule transported and the step at which regulation takes place.

Regulation is a problem of flux, not simply concentration

To understand an endocrine control system, imagine sampling the same animal several times after a meal or after a change in environmental temperature. The blood contains a mixture of molecules whose concentrations have been shaped by earlier secretion, absorption, storage, distribution and loss. A concentration is consequently a snapshot of a dynamic process. The body can change the delivery of a hormone without producing a proportional immediate change in its measured concentration, because a reservoir or a clearance mechanism can buffer the change. It can also maintain the concentration of a mineral even when the animal is losing that mineral from the body. These distinctions are fundamental to both thyroid and parathyroid physiology.

An endocrine gland differs from an exocrine gland in the destination of its secreted product. An exocrine gland delivers material through a duct to an epithelial surface or cavity, whereas the usual route for an endocrine signal is through extracellular fluid into blood. The thyroid follicle presents a subtle exception to a beginner's expectation of endocrine anatomy. Its lumen is a temporary storage compartment rather than a duct carrying the final product to the outside. The cell must send material toward that compartment, modify it there, and retrieve the resulting hormone before delivering it to blood. When drawing an endocrine pathway, it is therefore useful to specify not only the source and target but also the intermediate spaces traversed by the molecule.

The two systems illustrate different sensing strategies. The hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis integrates signals at more than one endocrine level. It regulates the synthetic organ by changing a trophic hormone, and the product influences the upstream levels. By contrast, the parathyroid gland contains a receptor that samples the extracellular ion whose abundance it helps control. Such an arrangement is well suited to regulating calcium because the variable is present near the secretory cells themselves. The principal PTH stimulus does not have to be translated first into a pituitary tropic signal. Nevertheless, parathyroid regulation also involves slower feedback from calcitriol, mineral availability, kidney function and changes in glandular capacity. Calling it a direct-sensing system does not imply that it has only one input.

Distinguish four categories when examining an endocrine experiment. A substrate is consumed or incorporated in a biochemical pathway. A signal changes the activity or amount of a biological component. A sensor detects an input, and an effector changes the flux that determines the controlled variable. For example, iodide is a substrate, TSH is a signal, and NIS and TPO are components of the synthetic machinery. In mineral physiology, extracellular ionized calcium is both a transported substance and the sensed variable, CaSR is a sensor, PTH is a signal, and the kidney and skeleton contain important effectors. The same molecule can occupy more than one functional category in a network, but the categories cannot be exchanged arbitrarily. This reasoning becomes useful when interpreting an experiment in which an enzyme inhibitor is compared with a receptor mutation.

What a feedback experiment can and cannot establish

An increase in an upstream hormone following removal of a target organ can be evidence of negative feedback. However, surgical removal also changes other physiological variables and may activate stress responses. An especially instructive experiment compares a sham-operated group with a group in which hormone production has been reduced and a group in which the missing hormone is replaced. A rise in the upstream signal after loss of the target hormone, followed by suppression when that hormone is restored, supports feedback by the target product. Direct effects at the pituitary can be tested separately in an isolated preparation. This is a general experimental design rather than a claim about a particular historical data set. The combination of perturbation and rescue is stronger evidence than a simple correlation between two hormones.

Thyroid anatomy and follicular polarity

The human thyroid is composed of two lobes connected by an isthmus near the trachea. It receives a rich blood supply, enabling communication between circulating thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), circulating iodide and the glandular cells. The functional unit is the follicle, a roughly spherical layer of epithelial cells enclosing a lumen. The luminal contents are called colloid; thyroglobulin, a large glycoprotein, forms an important part of the colloid. Follicles have two different cellular surfaces, each serving a different purpose. The basolateral surface faces the interstitial space and nearby capillaries. The apical surface faces the colloid. This polarity permits vectorial movement of iodide from blood into the lumen, followed by recovery of hormone-containing protein from the lumen and hormone delivery to blood.

The thyroid is unusual among endocrine glands because it maintains a substantial extracellular reserve of hormone precursors and mature iodothyronine residues attached to protein. This reserve makes acute changes in iodine supply less disruptive than would be expected if every molecule of hormone released had to be synthesized at that moment. Colloid is not simply a pool of freely diffusible T3 and T4. Much of the hormonally useful iodine is chemically incorporated within thyroglobulin. Release therefore requires a processing step. A histological section with tall follicular cells and evidence of colloid resorption is consistent with a stimulated gland, but morphology alone cannot identify the cause of stimulation.

Parafollicular cells, or C cells, lie within or between follicular structures without directly participating in the iodination of thyroglobulin. They release the peptide calcitonin. The parathyroids are distinct, usually four small glands associated with the posterior thyroid surface in humans, although their number and location vary. Their chief cells are arranged close to capillaries and do not enclose a thyroid-like colloid reservoir. A section showing a colloid-filled lumen is therefore far more informative for identifying thyroid follicles than merely finding a cluster of endocrine cells near the trachea.

Reading a thyroid histological preparation

At low magnification the follicle appears as a roughly circular or irregular enclosed region filled with relatively homogeneous material. At higher magnification, nuclei arranged around the lumen identify the epithelial wall. A section passing tangentially through a spherical follicle will show a smaller cross-section than a section passing through its center, so differences in apparent diameter are not sufficient by themselves to establish differences in follicular size. The appearance also depends on fixation and staining. These elementary geometrical and methodological limitations prevent a student from overinterpreting a single image. If the question concerns functional activity, compare many follicles under equivalent preparation conditions and examine epithelial height, colloid characteristics and independent functional measures.

An active follicular epithelium must carry out protein synthesis, membrane transport and endocytic processing. The rough endoplasmic reticulum supplies the machinery needed to synthesize secreted glycoprotein, and the Golgi apparatus contributes to its modification and delivery. Secretory vesicles must fuse with the correct surface. The basolateral membrane contains transport systems and receptors with functions that differ from those at the apical surface. A cell may synthesize a perfectly functional transport protein, yet fail to use it appropriately if the protein is mislocalized to the wrong membrane domain. Polarity is therefore not merely a feature visible under the microscope; it is a necessary element of the mechanism that converts substrate available in blood into a secreted hormone.

Consider the fate of a newly synthesized thyroglobulin molecule. It enters the endoplasmic reticulum, undergoes folding and glycosylation, passes through the Golgi system and reaches the lumen by exocytosis. At this stage it provides chemically suitable sites for subsequent iodination. A defect in folding or secretion can prevent efficient delivery even if iodide transport and peroxidase activity are normal. Conversely, a cell may export abundant protein that remains poorly iodinated because iodide or peroxide is missing. A biochemical assay detecting thyroglobulin alone would not discriminate between these possibilities. It measures the scaffold, not necessarily the success of every reaction that subsequently occurs on it.

The luminal store is bounded by epithelial cells, yet it is extracellular relative to their cytoplasm. The arrangement provides a useful contrast with peptide-secreting endocrine cells, which generally store processed peptide in membrane-bound intracellular secretory granules. A rapid secretory response of the latter can arise from regulated exocytosis. In the thyroid, the hormone-bearing material must be recovered from the extracellular lumen before liberation of much of its hormone content. The distinguishing event in an experiment is therefore not simply whether endocytosis or exocytosis occurs, but which material moves through which process and on which side of the epithelium.

The richly vascular interfollicular connective tissue also has a functional meaning. Blood delivery supplies iodine and carries away released hormone, while pituitary TSH must reach its receptor through the same general route. However, an increase in perfusion does not necessarily increase hormone synthesis if the limiting factor is an apical enzyme, adequate thyroglobulin or downstream processing. The gland functions as a sequence of linked operations, each of which can become limiting. The histological relationship of blood vessels, polarized epithelium and colloid is a spatial representation of that sequence.

Identifying cell populations through their products

If a histologist uses antibodies against thyroglobulin, calcitonin and PTH on separate tissue sections, the three staining patterns are expected to reflect the distribution of different cell products. Thyroglobulin is associated with follicular cells and colloid, calcitonin with C cells, and PTH with parathyroid chief cells. Appropriate controls are necessary, because nonspecific binding or cross-reactivity can produce misleading staining. Histological evidence of a particular hormone is evidence for its local presence; proof of regulated secretion requires additional functional measurements. A peptide detected within a cell may be stored but not released, and a protein present in a lumen may have been secreted from the surrounding cell layer earlier. This is why anatomical identification and physiological measurement complement one another.

Iodide capture and the chemistry of organification

Iodine consumed in food becomes available to the thyroid predominantly as iodide. A negatively charged ion cannot freely dissolve in the hydrophobic core of a membrane, so it requires a transport system. The sodium–iodide symporter (NIS) on the basolateral follicular membrane uses the energy stored in the inward sodium electrochemical gradient to accumulate iodide. The sodium gradient is maintained by the Na+/K+-ATPase, which hydrolyses ATP. NIS is thus a secondary active transporter rather than an ATP-driven iodide pump. The usual coupling stoichiometry is two sodium ions per iodide ion. If the sodium gradient collapses, the immediate consequence is impaired uptake even if the concentration of NIS protein has not changed.

Iodide must subsequently reach the apical surface and lumen. Pendrin contributes to this apical movement, although other pathways can also participate. Thyroglobulin is translated into the secretory pathway, processed and delivered into the lumen. These two streams bring the inorganic substrate and the protein scaffold together. Thyroid peroxidase (TPO), located near the apical surface, catalyses reactions that require hydrogen peroxide, supplied principally through a dual oxidase system. An iodide ion is not itself an iodinating reagent of sufficient reactivity for the entire process. Oxidative chemistry makes incorporation into selected tyrosyl residues of thyroglobulin possible. The covalent attachment of iodine to these residues is called organification.

One iodine substituent produces monoiodotyrosine (MIT), and two produce diiodotyrosine (DIT). These names refer to residues in the protein at this stage, not secreted thyroid hormones. Oxidative coupling within thyroglobulin subsequently constructs iodothyronine residues. Two DIT-derived residues yield T4; an appropriately oriented MIT donor and DIT acceptor yield T3. The orientation matters: changing which ring lacks iodine changes the chemical identity and biological activity of the product. The hormone remains attached to the scaffold until proteolytic release. A disorder of TPO or peroxide provision may therefore allow iodide uptake while severely limiting effective hormone production. In contrast, a defect in NIS reduces the delivery of the substrate in the first place. The two defects are expected to produce different results in experiments that distinguish iodide accumulation from covalent incorporation.

Iodine availability interacts with feedback. With sustained deficiency, insufficient substrate reduces effective synthesis, and compensatory TSH may increase glandular growth. An acute excess of iodide can transiently suppress organification, a phenomenon known as the Wolff–Chaikoff effect. In a normal gland, adaptation can permit synthesis to resume despite continued exposure. Thus a graph relating iodide supply to hormone production need not be a straight increasing line. The presence of a substrate is necessary but not sufficient to predict the output of a regulated pathway.

Energetics of iodide concentration

A useful way to reason about NIS is to consider the electrochemical potential difference for each transported ion. For a species of charge z moving from outside to inside, the change in molar electrochemical potential can be expressed as RT ln(Cinside/Coutside) + zF(Vinside − Voutside), using concentrations as an approximation to activities. R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, and F is the Faraday constant. The voltage is expressed in volts so that the electrical contribution has units of joules per mole. The term for sodium generally favors entry because intracellular sodium is low and the interior is electrically negative. Iodide accumulation may oppose its own concentration gradient. Coupling the inward movement of two sodium ions to one iodide ion allows the favorable free-energy change of sodium entry to offset an unfavorable contribution from iodide.

For an illustrative calculation, take extracellular sodium as 140 mmol/L, intracellular sodium as 14 mmol/L, and the membrane potential as −60 mV. At 310 K, RT is approximately 2.58 kJ/mol. The chemical contribution for one sodium ion entering is 2.58 ln(0.1), about −5.94 kJ/mol. The electrical contribution is 96.5 kJ/(V·mol) multiplied by −0.060 V, about −5.79 kJ/mol. The total is approximately −11.73 kJ/mol for one sodium ion, and twice this for two sodium ions. For iodide, the negative charge reverses the sign of the electrical term, so entry is electrically opposed by approximately +5.79 kJ/mol. If iodide is accumulated to a hypothetical concentration ratio of 100, the chemical contribution is 2.58 ln(100), about +11.88 kJ/mol. The combined change for the coupled transport cycle is approximately −23.46 + 5.79 + 11.88 = −5.79 kJ/mol. The transport remains thermodynamically favorable in this simplified example.

This calculation does not establish the exact iodide gradient in a living follicle. It neglects activity coefficients, membrane-potential variation, leakage, competing substrates and transporter kinetics. Its purpose is to demonstrate why a pump inhibitor can impair a secondary transporter without binding that transporter. Reducing the sodium gradient reduces the magnitude of the favorable chemical term, while depolarization changes the electrical contributions. A student who has learned membrane transport in cell biology can therefore derive the direction of the endocrine defect rather than memorizing that ATP depletion decreases thyroid function. The total free-energy change refers to the complete stoichiometric transport cycle; analyzing only the iodide ion would incorrectly conclude that accumulation is impossible.

How tracer experiments separate uptake from organification

Iodine tracer studies exploit the fact that entry into a tissue and incorporation into a macromolecule are separate experimentally measurable events. Imagine adding labeled iodide to otherwise comparable follicular preparations. An investigator can first measure cellular tracer accumulation, then separate soluble inorganic iodide from iodine associated with precipitable protein. If a preparation accumulates iodide but produces little protein-associated label, the block lies after initial entry, although further experiments are needed to localize it exactly. If both pools are low, reduced uptake is one possibility, but insufficient viability or an inappropriate sodium gradient could also explain the result. Measuring only total tissue-associated radioactivity cannot establish that hormone synthesis has occurred.

In a simplified pulse-chase experiment, labeled iodide is provided during a short pulse and then replaced by unlabeled substrate. Label moves through successive biochemical pools over time. Early labeling of free iodide followed by labeling of MIT and DIT and later of iodothyronines in thyroglobulin is consistent with a precursor–product sequence. The timing of incorporation matters: simultaneous labeling at one sampling time cannot establish order as strongly as a time series. A control lacking effective TPO activity should show reduced organified label even if initial uptake is relatively preserved. This conceptual experiment follows established biochemical principles of thyroid hormone synthesis; numerical data in an examination scenario must be treated as hypothetical unless tied to a published source.

Perchlorate provides a classic physiological distinction because competition with iodide transport can expose the fraction of accumulated iodide that has not been effectively organified. After iodide trapping, inhibition of continued uptake can allow nonorganified iodide to escape. Iodine chemically incorporated into thyroglobulin is not expected to be discharged in the same manner as freely exchangeable inorganic iodide. A relatively large discharge can therefore support an organification defect. It is not equivalent to measuring TPO concentration, and it does not identify a specific mutation by itself. In a conceptual test, the key is the difference between reversible accumulation of a small ion and covalent fixation in a macromolecule.

Why iodination and coupling require a scaffold

A protein is more than a collection of amino acids in solution. Its folded structure determines which side chains are accessible to enzymes and which residues can approach one another in suitable orientations. Thyroglobulin provides tyrosyl residues in a structural context that permits efficient iodination and oxidative coupling. Not every tyrosine in the protein becomes a hormone, even when iodide is abundant. Selectivity depends on local structure and reaction accessibility. A hormone-producing residue receives an iodinated aromatic contribution from a donor residue; the orientation and iodine substitution pattern determine whether the product is T4 or T3. Thinking of the reaction as a random collision between two freely dissolved DIT molecules obscures this important role of protein architecture.

The iodine atoms have a dual significance. Their covalent incorporation determines the chemical identities of the thyroid hormones, and their subsequent selective removal provides a mechanism for changing hormone activity outside the thyroid. Therefore, iodine is not just an inorganic nutrient required to maintain the gland. It is part of a molecular design that permits the gland to secrete a largely prohormonal product and peripheral tissues to regulate local activation. The presence of four iodine atoms in T4 and three in T3 makes the later enzymatic control chemically possible. Removing the wrong iodine atom, however, can generate reverse T3 rather than an active product. The positional specificity of deiodination is as important as the count of iodine atoms.

Limiting steps and the interpretation of low iodine supply

When dietary iodine is scarce, a functioning feedback system increases the pressure on the gland to recover output. Enhanced trapping and greater trophic stimulation can improve extraction of available iodide, but cannot create iodine atoms. The gland may favor relatively greater T3 production as iodine supply becomes limiting; T3 contains one fewer iodine atom than T4. This adjustment can preserve some biological activity without fully eliminating the consequences of deficiency. It is important not to confuse a relative shift in secreted products with an unlimited capacity for compensation. Long-term substrate limitation eventually constrains output, particularly when physiological demand increases.

Excess iodine illustrates a different regulatory issue. A sudden increase in intrathyroidal iodine can trigger protective reduction of organification, and subsequent adaptation is associated with reduced iodide entry. Not every gland adapts identically, especially when pre-existing disease or autonomous tissue is present. Thus two animals with similar iodine exposure can have different endocrine outcomes. An experimental comparison should control baseline iodine status, time after exposure and the functional state of the gland. For examination purposes, separate the short-term autoregulatory response to excess substrate from longer-term negative feedback through thyroid hormone and TSH. They act at different levels of the system and can operate simultaneously.

Colloid retrieval and circulating thyroid hormones

When thyroid-stimulating hormone activates its receptor on the follicular cell, cAMP-dependent signaling stimulates multiple steps of hormone production and release. Iodinated thyroglobulin is retrieved from the lumen by endocytic processes and delivered to intracellular degradative compartments. Proteolytic cleavage releases T4 and T3, which can then leave the follicular cell for blood through transport pathways. Remaining MIT and DIT can be deiodinated by iodotyrosine dehalogenase to conserve iodide. This recycling matters because an iodine atom placed in an uncoupled residue represents a potentially reusable resource rather than an inevitable urinary loss.

The normal thyroid releases substantially more T4 than T3. In the circulation, thyroid hormones bind reversibly to proteins such as thyroxine-binding globulin, transthyretin and albumin. The bound pool buffers the free concentration and influences hormone clearance. Protein binding is not the same as irreversible inactivation. At equilibrium, free hormone can dissociate from the carrier pool as hormone enters tissues or is cleared. Free concentrations therefore have particular importance for feedback and target-cell exposure, whereas total measurements depend partly on binding-protein abundance.

Consider a rise in the amount of thyroxine-binding globulin. Immediately after the binding change, free T4 tends to fall as more hormone becomes bound. In an intact axis, feedback can stimulate additional secretion until free T4 approaches its previous regulated value. Total T4 can then be higher without indicating sustained thyroid hormone excess. Conversely, a reduction in binding capacity can lower total T4 without producing chronic hormone deficiency. In an examination, a total concentration cannot be interpreted in isolation when the perturbation explicitly concerns binding proteins.

A simple kinetic model also explains why circulating T4 changes slowly. If the circulating amount is A and fractional clearance constant is k, removal is kA per unit time. At constant production P, the steady state satisfies P=kA. After abruptly stopping secretion, the idealized pool follows A(t)=A(0)e^(−kt). The half-life equals ln(2)/k. For an illustrative seven-day half-life and no new secretion, the amount falls to one half after seven days and one quarter after fourteen days. Real thyroid physiology has multiple compartments, changing protein binding and feedback; the calculation isolates the kinetic principle rather than predicting an individual patient's laboratory values.

Storage, release and recycling as distinct rate processes

A stored iodothyronine residue within thyroglobulin cannot act on a nuclear receptor elsewhere in the body while it remains covalently incorporated in the large protein. Proteolysis releases the small hormone molecule, making transport and receptor binding possible. In addition to the correct biochemical processing, hormone export requires movement across the cell membrane toward the interstitial space. A defect in endocytosis might therefore cause accumulation of iodinated material in the lumen, whereas a defect in proteolysis could trap material in the cell after retrieval. These scenarios differ in the predicted distribution of labeled protein. The effect on circulating T4 may be similar, but the intracellular and luminal measurements can distinguish mechanisms.

Uncoupled iodotyrosyl residues contain iodine that has already been concentrated and organified at considerable metabolic cost. Their dehalogenation liberates iodide for reuse. The process is not the same as converting circulating T4 to T3, despite the superficially similar act of removing iodine. The substrate is an iodotyrosine rather than an iodothyronine and the corresponding enzyme is different. A defect in iodine recycling can increase loss of previously incorporated iodine, so normal dietary intake may be less effective at supporting hormone synthesis. This is a concrete example of why conservation mechanisms are important when a nutrient has limited availability.

Binding equilibrium and free hormone

The simplest model of carrier binding treats free hormone H and unoccupied carrier B as reacting reversibly to form a complex HB. At equilibrium the dissociation constant Kd equals [H][B]/[HB]. A lower Kd corresponds to higher affinity, provided the interaction follows this simple model. If the total carrier concentration is Bt and one independent binding site is assumed, the bound concentration is Bt[H]/(Kd + [H]). Total hormone equals free plus bound hormone. The equation explains why increasing Bt at a fixed free concentration raises total hormone. It also demonstrates saturation: when free hormone greatly exceeds Kd, most sites are occupied, so additional hormone cannot all be accommodated by that same carrier pool.

Suppose a hypothetical carrier has a Kd of 1 nmol/L, and the free hormone concentration is 0.1 nmol/L. If total carrier binding sites are 100 nmol/L, bound hormone equals 100 × 0.1/1.1, or approximately 9.09 nmol/L. Total hormone is approximately 9.19 nmol/L. If carrier binding sites double and feedback eventually restores free hormone to 0.1 nmol/L, bound hormone becomes approximately 18.18 nmol/L, with total 18.28 nmol/L. The numbers are intentionally simplified and do not represent clinical thyroid reference intervals. The exercise shows why total hormone can almost double while free hormone and receptor-level signaling remain nearly unchanged.

In a real blood sample, multiple proteins bind thyroid hormone with different capacities and affinities. Binding is reversible and is affected by physiological state, competing molecules and measurement method. The simple equation treats carrier sites as identical and assumes equilibrium. It cannot reproduce every condition of abnormal binding. A total measurement can still be informative if binding conditions are stable or independently assessed. Similarly, a free measurement has limitations related to assay technique and sample conditions. The scientific lesson is not that one type of measurement is universally worthless, but that every quantity must be interpreted in terms of what the assay measures and what biological variable the question asks about.

Half-life and residence time are related but distinct concepts. Under a first-order single-compartment approximation, the average residence time is 1/k while the half-life is ln(2)/k. These are not equal. If half-life is seven days, k is approximately 0.099 per day and the average residence time is approximately 10.1 days. Constant secretion maintains a pool even though individual molecules are continuously removed. At steady state, production and clearance are balanced, not stopped. This interpretation is important when a question describes the gland as secreting a fixed amount every day while measured hormone concentration remains unchanged. Constancy of concentration is evidence that the rates balance at that time, not that there is no turnover.

Deiodinases and tissue-specific hormone action

The thyroid is not the only location where the iodination pattern of iodothyronines changes. Deiodinases remove selected iodine atoms. Type 1 and type 2 deiodinases can catalyse conversion of T4 to T3 by outer-ring deiodination, whereas type 3 deiodinase preferentially inactivates T4 to reverse T3 and T3 to less active metabolites by inner-ring deiodination. These are selenium-containing enzymes, but they do not catalyse the organification reaction performed by TPO. One builds iodinated residues in the follicle; the others modify an existing iodothyronine in tissues. Type 1 also participates in clearance of reverse T3, and type 2 has a particularly important role in determining local T3 availability in selected tissues.

This mechanism means that plasma T4 is partly a source of local active hormone. A cell expressing an efficient T4-activating deiodinase can receive a different intracellular T3 signal from another cell exposed to the same plasma T4. A developing tissue can also restrict local signaling through inactivation. A measurement of circulating hormone consequently provides valuable systemic information, but does not fully describe tissue-specific exposure. Transport across cell membranes is also mediated by specialized transporters; lipid solubility alone does not account for all tissue delivery.

T3 binds nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, which often occupy regulatory DNA sequences in association with other transcriptional regulators. In many positive response elements, ligand binding changes the recruitment of corepressors and coactivators, altering transcription. The effect takes time because the cell must alter RNA and protein abundance. The response is not equivalent to opening an ion channel within milliseconds. Some thyroid hormone effects can occur more rapidly through other mechanisms, but the classical receptor-dependent control of transcription explains much of its enduring metabolic and developmental influence.

A useful experiment is to expose two matched cell cultures to equal T4 while selectively reducing type 2 deiodinase in one culture. A T3-responsive transcriptional reporter should decline in the affected cells if local activation is important. Direct T3 supplementation could restore the reporter if the transport and receptor machinery remain functional. A receptor-deficient culture, in contrast, would not be expected to show the same rescue through T3. This is a hypothetical mechanistic prediction, not a report of measured research data.

The hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis

Thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from hypothalamic neurons reaches the anterior pituitary via the portal circulation and stimulates thyrotrophs to secrete TSH. TSH reaches the thyroid via systemic blood. Thyroid hormones then reduce the upstream drive, providing negative feedback at both pituitary and hypothalamic levels. Local conversion of T4 to T3 contributes to this feedback. The sign of each arrow matters: TRH increases TSH, TSH increases thyroid hormone output, and thyroid hormone reduces the stimulating signals. A feedback diagram is not simply a series of arrows pointing in the same direction.

If thyroid synthetic capacity falls, free T4 tends to fall before the entire system has reached a new steady state. The decrease in negative feedback raises TSH, which attempts to recover production from the remaining functional tissue. An early compensated disorder may therefore show high TSH with free T4 still within its reference range. If reserve is insufficient, free T4 falls despite high TSH. The hormone concentration upstream is consequently not a measure of successful target-gland output. It is partly a measure of corrective effort.

In a central defect affecting pituitary TSH secretion, low free T4 is accompanied by low or inappropriately normal TSH rather than the strong increase expected from a competent pituitary. Some central disorders can yield mildly elevated but biologically less effective TSH. The important reasoning is appropriateness relative to free T4 rather than simply whether one laboratory value is flagged as abnormal. In autonomous thyroid secretion, high thyroid hormone suppresses TSH, but the suppressive message fails to stop the autonomous source. Exogenous hormone can create a similar circulating pattern; the biochemical feedback alone does not distinguish all possible causes.

The axis also responds to physiological context. Severe non-thyroidal illness can change deiodination, hormone binding and hypothalamic–pituitary signaling. An isolated low T3 measurement during illness therefore does not establish a primary thyroid defect. Similarly, circadian variation and the pulsatile nature of endocrine signals limit the interpretation of a single sample. For mechanistic examination problems, identify whether the system is at equilibrium, which gland is perturbed and whether any regulatory pathway is explicitly removed.

Metabolism, development and representative thyroid disorders

The effects of thyroid hormone are distributed among many organs rather than concentrated in one target tissue. In metabolically active cells, changes in gene expression can increase oxidative capacity and alter the abundance of proteins such as ion pumps. ATP hydrolysis and inefficient conversion of chemical energy ultimately contribute to heat generation. An increase in basal energy expenditure therefore has consequences for oxygen consumption, substrate utilization and heat balance. This does not imply that thyroid hormone creates energy or violates energy conservation; it changes the flux through energy-transforming processes.

In the cardiovascular system, thyroid hormone can increase responsiveness to adrenergic signals and influence heart rate, contractility and peripheral circulation. In the developing nervous system, adequate thyroid signaling is required during sensitive windows for normal maturation. In amphibians, thyroid hormone has a major role in coordinating metamorphic changes. Tissue-specific response and timing explain why the same circulating signal can promote different morphological outcomes in distinct organs. These comparative observations connect endocrinology with developmental biology without requiring a separate account of embryonic pattern formation.

In primary hypothyroidism, insufficient thyroid hormone production commonly increases TSH if feedback and pituitary function are intact. Lower metabolic capacity may be associated with reduced heat production, slowing of physiological processes and altered lipid metabolism. Hyperthyroidism refers to excessive hormone production by the gland, whereas thyrotoxicosis is the broader state of excess thyroid hormone action from any source. Graves disease is an example of stimulatory antibodies acting at the TSH receptor; their activity can persist while pituitary TSH is suppressed. Destructive thyroiditis can release stored hormone without an increase in new synthesis. Both can cause excess circulating hormone, yet their underlying mechanisms and iodine uptake patterns differ.

Goitre means thyroid enlargement, not a particular hormone concentration. Increased trophic stimulation can enlarge an iodine-deficient gland; autoimmune stimulation and other mechanisms may also produce enlargement. A gland can be large while hormone concentrations are low, normal or high. This is an important example of the difference between tissue mass, synthetic capacity and regulatory output. A student should not infer thyroid status from gland size alone.

Calcium pools and the controlled variable

Calcium serves both as a mineral constituent and as a signaling ion. Most body calcium resides in the mineralized skeleton, principally in association with phosphate as hydroxyapatite-like crystals. A small extracellular fraction is essential for membrane excitability, neurotransmitter release, muscle contraction, coagulation and cellular signaling. Total plasma calcium is composed of free ionized calcium, calcium bound mainly to albumin, and calcium complexed with anions. The ionized fraction is particularly important for parathyroid feedback and electrical stability.

A decrease in albumin can reduce total calcium while ionized calcium remains near its regulated concentration. Changing blood pH also changes calcium binding to proteins. In alkalosis, greater calcium binding can reduce ionized calcium even when total calcium has not appreciably changed. A total calcium measurement therefore cannot always indicate the physiological status of the controlled pool. This is analogous in logic, although not in chemistry, to the difference between total and free thyroid hormones.

Calcium homeostasis requires both concentration control and mass balance. Suppose an illustrative subject absorbs 250 mg calcium per day from the gut, excretes 200 mg in urine and loses 50 mg through other routes. Net external balance is zero. If absorption falls to 150 mg while external losses initially remain unchanged, net loss is 100 mg per day. Extracellular calcium may nonetheless remain normal for a period through mobilization of skeletal mineral. This distinction explains how a seemingly normal serum concentration can coexist with unfavorable long-term mineral balance. The skeleton is not an inert warehouse; its mineral is continuously exchanged during remodeling.

Phosphate is another important mineral constituent. It participates in nucleic acids, phospholipids, phosphorylation reactions and mineralization. Calcium and phosphate must be regulated together, because increasing one by indiscriminately mobilizing bone may also increase the other. The endocrine system therefore combines skeletal mobilization with selective renal transport and intestinal absorption rather than relying on a single process.

The calcium-sensing receptor and parathyroid secretion

Parathyroid chief cells detect extracellular calcium through the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR), a G protein-coupled receptor expressed at their surface. Higher extracellular ionized calcium activates CaSR and suppresses PTH secretion. A decrease in calcium reduces this inhibitory input and promotes secretion. This sign is the reverse of an intuitive but incorrect assumption that activation of a secretory cell's receptor must always increase hormone release. Here receptor activity communicates that the controlled variable is already sufficiently high.

The response has a steep relationship to calcium concentration in its physiological operating range. In a conceptual graph of PTH versus ionized calcium, PTH declines as calcium increases. A change in the receptor's sensitivity shifts the calcium concentration required to produce a given inhibitory response. A reduced-sensitivity receptor may allow PTH secretion to persist at a higher calcium concentration than usual. The receptor is also expressed in the kidney, where it contributes to local mineral handling; thus a receptor mutation can affect more than the parathyroid gland.

The immediate secretory response uses existing peptide hormone stores. Prolonged changes can alter hormone synthesis and glandular growth. Calcitriol suppresses PTH gene expression and contributes to feedback, whereas severe magnesium deficiency can impair PTH secretion and responsiveness despite hypocalcemia. This magnesium exception illustrates why a single-variable feedback diagram is a useful approximation but not a complete description of real biology.

PTH is synthesized as a precursor that is processed into a mature 84-amino-acid peptide. Unlike thyroid hormone, it acts mainly through a membrane receptor on target cells rather than by directly binding a nuclear DNA-associated receptor. The PTH1 receptor couples to signaling pathways including cAMP and phospholipase C, with effects dependent on the target tissue. The common result of an acute physiological response to falling calcium is to restore the extracellular ionized pool through changes in bone, kidney and intestinal mineral transfer.

PTH actions on bone and renal mineral handling

Bone contains osteoblast-lineage cells that form osteoid and support mineralization, and osteoclasts that resorb mineralized matrix. PTH does not simply bind mature osteoclasts and command them to dissolve bone. Much of its resorptive effect is mediated through osteoblast-lineage and stromal cells, which regulate osteoclast differentiation and activity through signals including RANKL and its decoy receptor osteoprotegerin. An increase in effective RANKL signaling favors osteoclast formation and survival. Osteoclasts create a localized acidic environment to dissolve mineral and use proteolytic enzymes to degrade the organic matrix.

Release from bone supplies both calcium and phosphate. This fact explains the importance of renal phosphate excretion in the overall PTH response. If phosphate were retained to the same extent as calcium, the increase in free calcium could be constrained by complex formation and mineral chemistry. PTH reduces proximal tubular phosphate reabsorption by decreasing the abundance of sodium–phosphate cotransporters at the apical membrane. Consequently, more filtered phosphate remains in tubular fluid and is excreted. At the same time PTH promotes calcium conservation, especially through regulated distal nephron transport. Its renal actions therefore distinguish calcium from phosphate rather than treating both minerals identically.

The quantitative relationship between filtration and excretion is particularly helpful in data questions. The filtered load of a freely filterable substance equals its plasma concentration multiplied by glomerular filtration rate. Excretion equals urine concentration times urine flow. In a simplified example, a filterable phosphate concentration of 1 mmol/L and GFR of 0.12 L/min yield 0.12 mmol/min filtered. A urine phosphate concentration of 20 mmol/L at 0.002 L/min gives 0.04 mmol/min excreted. Fractional excretion is 0.04/0.12, or approximately 33%. If PTH increases fractional phosphate excretion with filtered load unchanged, urinary phosphate loss increases. In real analysis, the filterable fraction and sampling assumptions must be considered.

PTH also stimulates renal conversion of vitamin D to its active endocrine form. This provides a slower mechanism for increasing acquisition of calcium from the diet. The same hormone can therefore produce a rapid change in renal transport and a delayed change in intestinal absorption. These complementary mechanisms reduce reliance on skeletal reserves during persistent dietary challenge. Sustained elevation of PTH and brief intermittent receptor stimulation need not have identical effects on bone remodeling; the temporal pattern matters.

Vitamin D activation, intestinal transport and phosphate feedback

Vitamin D can arise from dietary sources and, in humans, from ultraviolet B-dependent reactions in skin. Cholecalciferol is hydroxylated in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, an abundant circulating precursor. The kidney converts this compound through 1α-hydroxylase (CYP27B1) to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, or calcitriol. Distinguishing these molecules is important because the concentration of the storage-related precursor and the active hormone can change in different directions under endocrine compensation. A fall in precursor availability can initially be accompanied by maintained or even increased active hormone if PTH stimulates its formation.

Calcitriol binds the vitamin D receptor, a nuclear receptor, and regulates genes that facilitate mineral absorption. In intestinal epithelial cells, transcellular calcium movement can involve an apical channel such as TRPV6, intracellular calcium-binding proteins and basolateral extrusion systems. Paracellular transport also contributes, especially when luminal calcium is plentiful. The endocrine effect is therefore not an all-or-none gate through which every absorbed calcium ion must pass. Calcitriol also supports intestinal phosphate acquisition. Mineralization of growing bone requires sufficient amounts of both minerals, which is one reason why vitamin D insufficiency can compromise mineralization despite compensatory PTH secretion.

Phosphate feedback involves fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), secreted largely by osteocytes and osteoblasts. In the kidney it acts with the co-receptor α-Klotho to decrease phosphate reabsorption and reduce calcitriol production. High phosphate burden and calcitriol can stimulate FGF23; the subsequent change in renal transport and vitamin D metabolism helps restrain phosphate accumulation. FGF23 and PTH share a phosphaturic effect but have opposing typical effects on renal calcitriol production. An analytical question that treats all phosphaturic hormones as functionally identical therefore misses an important regulatory distinction.

Chronic kidney dysfunction can impair phosphate elimination and calcitriol production, leading to a stimulus for secondary parathyroid elevation. Reduced functional renal mass means that compensatory increases in phosphaturic signals may not fully restore total phosphate excretion. A high concentration of a regulatory hormone is not proof that its target response is successful. The same principle appeared in the thyroid axis when elevated TSH could not compensate for limited thyroid synthetic capacity.

Calcitonin and integrated parathyroid disorders

Calcitonin is a peptide released by thyroid C cells in response to increased calcium and other stimuli. It can inhibit osteoclast-mediated resorption. Its direction of action on bone therefore differs from the typical resorptive component of sustained PTH elevation. However, a diagram containing arrows of opposite sign does not demonstrate that the two hormones contribute equally to physiological regulation. In adult humans, ordinary calcium homeostasis depends predominantly on PTH, vitamin D, renal function and intestinal mineral availability. Calcitonin's role is relatively limited under many normal conditions, though its importance can vary with species and physiological context.

In primary hyperparathyroidism, parathyroid output is excessive relative to the prevailing calcium concentration. The expected pattern is high calcium with elevated or inappropriately nonsuppressed PTH; phosphate may fall when renal function permits phosphaturia. In hypoparathyroidism, low PTH reduces renal calcium conservation and phosphate excretion and impairs the stimulation of calcitriol synthesis. A typical pattern is low calcium with high phosphate and low or inappropriately normal PTH. In a target-organ resistance state, low calcium and high phosphate can coexist with high PTH, because the feedback response is present but its downstream effect is impaired.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism is compensatory rather than necessarily autonomous. Low calcium availability, vitamin D deficiency or impaired renal function can stimulate PTH. The phosphate concentration depends on the cause: renal failure can lead to retention despite elevated PTH, while a person with intact renal phosphate handling and reduced vitamin D availability may have increased phosphate loss. Thus a table of laboratory patterns is best interpreted as conditional predictions rather than a universal diagnostic checklist.

The organizing principle is to locate the failed component in the feedback loop. If calcium is low and PTH is low, secretion or its regulation is suspect. If calcium is low and PTH is high, substrate availability, vitamin D activation, renal function or tissue responsiveness may be limiting. If calcium is high and PTH is suppressed, the hypercalcemic stimulus may be outside the parathyroid gland. The direction of the upstream response is evidence about feedback integrity, not a complete etiological diagnosis.

Original examination practice

1. Part B — Where is NIS located?

Answer and explanation

Basolateral follicular membrane

It captures iodide from the interstitial side using the sodium gradient.

2. Part B — What is the protein scaffold for hormone formation?

Answer and explanation

Thyroglobulin

Tyrosyl residues are iodinated and coupled within this protein.

3. Part B — Which enzyme catalyses organification?

Answer and explanation

Thyroid peroxidase

TPO uses peroxide-dependent oxidative chemistry near the apical membrane.

4. Part B — Which iodotyrosine combination yields T4?

Answer and explanation

DIT and DIT

Each contributing ring bears two iodine atoms.

5. Part B — Which molecule is predominantly secreted by the thyroid follicular cells?

Answer and explanation

T4

Peripheral activation supplies much of circulating T3.

6. Part B — Which deiodinase inactivates T4 to reverse T3?

Answer and explanation

D3

Inner-ring deiodination produces an inactive isomer.

7. Part B — What hormone stimulates follicular TSH receptors?

Answer and explanation

TSH

Anterior pituitary thyrotrophs supply this trophic signal.

8. Part B — What class of receptor mediates classical T3 transcriptional responses?

Answer and explanation

Nuclear thyroid hormone receptor

Ligand changes the recruitment of transcriptional regulators.

9. Part B — Which calcium fraction is directly relevant to CaSR regulation?

Answer and explanation

Ionized calcium

Protein-bound calcium is not the free ion sensed in this feedback loop.

10. Part B — Where is calcitonin produced?

Answer and explanation

Thyroid C cells

They are distinct from follicular cells and parathyroid chief cells.

11. Part B — Which hormone stimulates renal CYP27B1?

Answer and explanation

PTH

This links hypocalcemic feedback to active vitamin D formation.

12. Part B — Which hormone is the active vitamin D metabolite?

Answer and explanation

1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D

It is calcitriol, distinct from the circulating 25-hydroxy precursor.

13. Part B — Which kidney segment is the main site of PTH-induced reduction in phosphate reabsorption?

Answer and explanation

Proximal tubule

PTH decreases apical sodium–phosphate cotransporter abundance.

14. Part B — What does goitre describe?

Answer and explanation

An enlarged thyroid

Gland size does not specify circulating hormone levels.

15. Part B — What is the principal bone-resorbing cell?

Answer and explanation

Osteoclast

It acidifies the resorptive compartment and degrades organic matrix.

16. Part B — Which cell-surface signal promotes osteoclast differentiation via RANK?

Answer and explanation

RANKL

Osteoblast-lineage and stromal signals regulate osteoclast formation.

17. Part B — Which tissue supplies much circulating FGF23?

Answer and explanation

Bone, especially osteocytes

It links skeletal mineral status to renal phosphate handling.

18. Part B — What is the normal direction of CaSR signaling when extracellular calcium rises?

Answer and explanation

Increased CaSR activation with reduced PTH secretion

The receptor senses sufficiency and suppresses corrective secretion.

19. Part B — Which vitamin D hydroxylation is mainly hepatic?

Answer and explanation

25-hydroxylation

The kidney performs regulated 1α-hydroxylation.

20. Part B — Is calcitonin the dominant regulator of ordinary adult human calcium homeostasis?

Answer and explanation

No

PTH, vitamin D and organ mineral handling are more important under usual conditions.

21. Part C — NIS remains expressed, but Na+/K+-ATPase inhibition collapses the inward sodium gradient. Predict iodide uptake.

Answer and explanation

It declines

Secondary active uptake depends on the electrochemical gradient, not merely transporter abundance.

22. Part C — A TPO-deficient follicle retains NIS function. Which tracer observation differentiates the defects?

Answer and explanation

Iodide entry can occur, but protein-bound iodine formation is impaired

TPO acts after uptake; NIS loss would compromise substrate entry itself.

23. Part C — Free T4 falls while TSH rises. Which component is likely impaired if feedback remains intact?

Answer and explanation

Primary thyroid capacity

The raised upstream signal represents an appropriate compensatory response.

24. Part C — A subject has low free T4 with low TSH. Explain why this is not a typical primary failure pattern.

Answer and explanation

Central signaling may be inadequate

Low free T4 should disinhibit an intact pituitary. TSH is inappropriate for the downstream concentration.

25. Part C — Binding globulin rises and the axis later restores free T4. Predict total T4.

Answer and explanation

It increases

More bound hormone exists at the same free concentration; total concentration includes both pools.

26. Part C — A T3 reporter declines when D2 is knocked down in cells supplied with T4. What rescue distinguishes activation from receptor loss?

Answer and explanation

Direct T3 restores reporter activity if receptor machinery is intact

T3 bypasses the conversion defect but not an absent functional receptor.

27. Part C — TSH is suppressed but a TSH receptor-stimulating antibody remains present. Predict glandular drive.

Answer and explanation

It can remain elevated

The antibody acts at the receptor despite pituitary negative feedback.

28. Part C — A thyroid gland releases stored hormone because follicles are damaged. Is increased new synthesis required?

Answer and explanation

No

Colloid stores can supply transient excess through release without increased organification.

29. Part C — An idealized T4 pool has a seven-day half-life. How much of an initial 80 units remains after 21 days without production?

Answer and explanation

10 units

Three half-lives give 80 × (1/2)^3 = 10. This excludes new production and compartment changes.

30. Part C — Albumin falls while ionized calcium remains unchanged. Predict total calcium and PTH drive.

Answer and explanation

Total calcium may fall without a hypocalcemic PTH stimulus

The bound pool is altered while the regulated free ion is unchanged.

31. Part C — At the same total calcium, acute alkalosis increases protein binding. Predict ionized calcium.

Answer and explanation

It falls

More calcium is bound; the free pool declines and may stimulate PTH.

32. Part C — A CaSR variant decreases calcium sensitivity. At a given ionized calcium, what happens to PTH suppression?

Answer and explanation

It is weaker

The receptor signals less calcium sufficiency, shifting the feedback relation.

33. Part C — PTH rises and proximal tubular NaPi abundance falls. Predict urinary phosphate at unchanged filtered load.

Answer and explanation

It rises

Less reabsorption means greater excretion.

34. Part C — Filtered phosphate is 0.15 mmol/min and excretion is 0.03 mmol/min. Calculate fractional excretion.

Answer and explanation

20%

Divide excretion by filtered load: 0.03/0.15 = 0.20.

35. Part C — A subject absorbs 180 mg calcium daily and loses 260 mg daily externally. What is net balance over five days?

Answer and explanation

−400 mg

Daily net balance is −80 mg; the cumulative value is −400 mg if rates remain constant.

36. Part C — A PTH receptor response is absent despite high PTH and low calcium. Why is hormone replacement alone not predicted to restore the response?

Answer and explanation

The downstream signaling defect remains

Increasing the ligand cannot reliably rescue an absent responsive receptor.

37. Part C — FGF23 rises while kidney responsiveness remains intact. Predict phosphate reabsorption and calcitriol production.

Answer and explanation

Both decline

FGF23 inhibits renal phosphate transport and reduces active vitamin D production.

38. Part C — Renal function declines and phosphate is retained despite high PTH. Explain.

Answer and explanation

Reduced filtration and functional renal mass can limit total phosphate clearance

A high regulatory signal cannot guarantee adequate whole-organ output.

39. Part C — Bone-cell cultures lose osteoblast-lineage RANKL signaling. Predict the PTH-driven osteoclastogenic response.

Answer and explanation

It is attenuated

PTH-mediated osteoclast recruitment depends substantially on indirect signals from osteoblast-lineage cells.

40. Part C — A subject has high calcium and suppressed PTH. Does the result support autonomous PTH as the only cause?

Answer and explanation

No

Suppression indicates a responsive feedback loop and suggests considering non-PTH sources of hypercalcemia.

41. SET — Which statement distinguishes endocrine and exocrine secretion in a thyroid follicle?

Answer and explanation

The lumen stores precursor material internally; final hormone reaches blood.

The follicle is not an exocrine duct. Its colloid is an intermediate extracellular store.

42. SET — Assertion: Goitre can coexist with low thyroid hormone. Reason: Prolonged TSH stimulation may enlarge a substrate-limited gland.

Answer and explanation

Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Trophic drive and hormone output are distinct. A lack of iodide can limit synthesis despite gland enlargement.

43. SET — Why is a rise in TSH insufficient to prove a rise in thyroid hormone output?

Answer and explanation

The gland may have reduced responsiveness or synthetic reserve.

An upstream signal is evidence of drive, not a direct measurement of the target output.

44. SET — Assertion: PTH increases phosphate excretion. Reason: PTH reduces proximal tubular phosphate cotransporter availability.

Answer and explanation

Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Reduced reabsorption increases the fraction of filtered phosphate excreted when other conditions are fixed.

45. SET — Name the primary difference between calcitonin-producing and T4-producing cells.

Answer and explanation

C cells produce calcitonin, while follicular cells synthesize thyroid hormones.

The products have distinct synthesis, signaling and physiological functions.

46. Applied — A simplified tracer sample contains 60 units inorganic iodide and 40 units protein-bound label. What proportion is protein-bound?

Answer and explanation

40%

The denominator is 100 total tracer units; 40/100 is 0.40. It does not by itself reveal the fraction present as T4.

47. Applied — Which measurement better distinguishes substrate capture from covalent fixation: total thyroid tracer alone, or separation of free and protein-bound tracer?

Answer and explanation

Separation of free and protein-bound tracer.

The total pool can be high even if organification is defective.

48. Applied — A simple hormone pool of 200 units is cleared with a fractional rate constant of 0.1 per day. What daily input maintains it at steady state?

Answer and explanation

20 units per day.

At steady state P = kA = 0.1/day × 200 units. The example assumes one well-mixed compartment.

49. SET — Assertion: Severe magnesium deficiency may cause low PTH despite low calcium. Reason: Magnesium is required for normal PTH secretion and responsiveness.

Answer and explanation

Both statements are true; the reason accounts for the exception.

Low calcium is normally a strong PTH stimulus, but sufficiently severe magnesium deficiency disrupts the response.

50. Applied — Why can high PTH in chronic kidney dysfunction coexist with phosphate retention?

Answer and explanation

The remaining functional kidney may not clear enough phosphate.

Fractional handling and total filtration are different. Strong hormonal drive cannot compensate indefinitely for reduced organ capacity.

Revision and active recall

Follow the causal chain from substrate delivery to hormone synthesis and feedback. Separate extracellular calcium concentration from whole-body mineral balance, and identify whether the controlling gland or its target is defective.

  1. Trace iodide from blood to colloid.
  2. Distinguish iodide trapping from organification.
  3. Explain the difference between MIT, DIT, T3 and T4.
  4. Predict TSH after a decrease in thyroid synthetic capacity.
  5. Explain how binding proteins alter total T4.
  6. Contrast D2 with D3.
  7. Identify the calcium fraction sensed by CaSR.
  8. Trace the PTH–RANKL pathway.
  9. Explain PTH-driven phosphaturia.
  10. Trace vitamin D activation from skin or diet to intestine.
  11. Contrast PTH and FGF23 effects on calcitriol.
  12. Interpret high calcium with suppressed PTH.
