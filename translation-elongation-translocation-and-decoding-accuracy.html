1. The biological problem: converting a sequence into a moving reaction

The amino acid sequence of a protein is specified indirectly. The ribosome does not copy the chemical structure of an RNA molecule into another polymer. Instead, it interprets a succession of three-nucleotide symbols and joins a chemically different succession of molecules. The two polymers are related by a code, not by complementary pairing between their backbones. This distinction is important because two separate recognition events are necessary: an amino acid must be assigned to its transfer RNA, and the transfer RNA must be assigned to the proper position on a messenger RNA. An error in either operation can alter the protein, even while the other operation remains entirely accurate.

Imagine that a completed initiation complex has already identified the start codon. The initial methionine, or formylmethionine in a typical bacterium, is attached to the initiator tRNA. That tRNA is in the peptidyl site, called the P site. The first residue of a protein therefore exists before any peptide bond has been formed. There is no need to deliver a second initiator tRNA to begin elongation. What remains is to find the amino acid specified by the following codon, link it to the first residue, and advance the reading apparatus by precisely one codon. The product of this operation must be a ribosome capable of performing the same operation again.

This repeated operation solves three linked problems. First, it must choose a suitable aminoacyl-tRNA from a mixture of many similar molecules. Second, it must form an amide linkage between a selected amino acid and the growing chain without losing the connection between the chain and the ribosome. Third, it must present the next message symbol to the decoding center without shifting the reading frame. The physical arrangement of the ribosome makes these problems interdependent. A chain transferred onto a tRNA in the wrong position will not be ready for the next round. A ribosome advanced before decoding is finished could skip the very codon whose amino acid should be incorporated. Elongation is consequently a coordinated cycle rather than a sequence of independent enzymes acting on freely diffusing products.

The cost of a translation error depends on the resulting protein. Replacing a surface residue by a chemically similar residue may have little effect. A substitution in an enzyme's active site can prevent catalysis, and a substitution in a region that stabilizes a folded core can change the fraction of protein molecules that fold correctly. The position and nature of the error matter. This explains why cellular investment in accurate protein synthesis is beneficial even though some errors are tolerated. Unlike a mutation in genomic DNA, a typical isolated translational mistake is not copied into the DNA of subsequent generations. Nevertheless, when many proteins are synthesized, a modest increase in the frequency of such mistakes can produce a substantial burden of defective molecules.

A useful conceptual distinction is between processivity and fidelity. Processivity concerns how effectively a ribosome remains engaged with a message while making successive residues; fidelity concerns whether the resulting sequence matches the prescribed sequence. A ribosome may synthesize a full-length but inaccurate product, or it may make a short product whose existing residues are all correct. Full-length product measurements alone do not separate these outcomes. An experiment designed to investigate accuracy must therefore include information about the identities of the incorporated amino acids, not simply about the mass of protein produced.

2. Orientation of the message, chain and adaptors

RNA has distinguishable chemical ends because its nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds between the three-prime and five-prime positions of neighboring ribose sugars. The convention for writing an mRNA sequence is five-prime to three-prime. A translating ribosome progresses in that direction relative to the message. If the first translated nucleotide is position 1, the next codons occupy positions 4–6, 7–9 and so forth. Moving the relative register by one nucleotide would replace the succeeding triplets by another set. Therefore a movement of three nucleotides is not an arbitrary property of the machine; it is required by the triplet organization of the code.

Proteins have another form of polarity. A chain possesses an amino terminus, or N terminus, and a carboxyl terminus, or C terminus. During ordinary ribosomal synthesis the first residue ultimately contributes the N-terminal end, and subsequent residues are incorporated at the C-terminal end. The N-terminal portion of a sufficiently long growing chain can emerge from the exit tunnel even while the rest of the protein has not been made. The C-terminal end remains attached to a tRNA until release. These facts connect translation to later folding and processing: the entire protein sequence does not become available simultaneously.

The direction in which a tRNA anticodon pairs with a codon is antiparallel. For example, the mRNA codon 5′-GCU-3′ may be recognized by an anticodon written 3′-CGA-5′, which is 5′-AGC-3′ when written in the conventional orientation. A student who writes an anticodon without indicating its direction can easily mistake the correct complementary sequence. In actual cells, modified nucleotides and permissible wobble pairing expand the set of codons read by certain tRNAs. A wobble pair accepted by the code should not be treated as an error simply because it is not a canonical Watson–Crick pair.

A tRNA links two distant functional regions. Its anticodon provides a sequence-recognition surface at one end of the folded molecule, while its acceptor end carries an amino acid or a growing peptide. The terminal adenosine of the CCA end supplies the ribose hydroxyl group involved in ester attachment. The architecture is effective because recognition at the small ribosomal subunit can position a substrate in the catalytic center of the large subunit through the shape of one adaptor molecule. The two reactions occur at physically separated regions, yet the same tRNA coordinates them.

The amino acid is not recognized by base pairing. An anticodon that normally specifies alanine will still base-pair appropriately if its tRNA has been artificially loaded with another amino acid. The ability of the ribosome to recognize the codon does not automatically guarantee the correct amino acid identity. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases normally establish that assignment upstream of elongation. In a decoding experiment, it is therefore essential to specify whether the tRNA was correctly charged. A mischarged cognate tRNA and a correctly charged near-cognate tRNA can yield the same substituted amino acid in a protein through different molecular causes.

3. The three ribosomal sites as changing functional states

The labels A, P and E describe binding sites, not three permanently distinct types of tRNA. A typical elongator tRNA enters through the aminoacyl site, becomes the carrier of the new peptide, moves toward the peptidyl site and eventually occupies the exit site after losing its acyl group. Its amino acid identity and anticodon have not changed during this journey. What has changed is its location and chemical cargo. This is why one should identify the chemical state of a tRNA independently of its site when solving diagrams.

At the beginning of a canonical elongation cycle, the P site contains the peptidyl-tRNA. For the first round the cargo is a single amino acid rather than a chain containing multiple residues, but the same description of the site is useful. The next sense codon is available at the A site. An E-site tRNA may remain transiently from the previous round; E-site occupancy is not a universal requirement for the chemical reaction. Once an incoming aminoacyl-tRNA is selected and fully accommodated, the ribosome contains a donor and an acceptor positioned appropriately for peptide transfer.

The small subunit binds the mRNA and interacts with the anticodon regions of the tRNAs. The large subunit interacts with their acceptor ends, contains the peptidyl-transferase center and provides the route through which the growing chain emerges. A tRNA therefore spans the interface between two subunits. When a textbook labels one tRNA as occupying the A site, it usually represents a classical state in which the anticodon end and acceptor end occupy corresponding regions of the two subunits. The distinction becomes important after peptide transfer, when the ends can move at different times.

In bacteria, the two principal ribosomal subunits are designated 30S and 50S, while the associated ribosome is designated 70S. For cytosolic translation in a typical eukaryotic cell the corresponding labels are 40S, 60S and 80S. These sedimentation coefficients are not additive masses. A sedimentation coefficient depends on molecular mass, buoyancy and frictional resistance, which changes when the subunits associate. The chemistry of the elongation cycle is highly conserved despite differences in composition, size and the proteins that participate.

The designation of a site also does not imply a rigid hole through which tRNA must pass. A ribosome is flexible, and tRNA conformations change during selection and movement. If all components were locked in place, a tRNA could not be transferred from the decoding configuration to the fully accommodated configuration or from the pre-translocation configuration to the next reading position. A useful mental model is a changing arrangement of contacts among ribosomal RNA, ribosomal proteins, tRNA, mRNA and the elongation factors. Site labels summarize the principal functions of these contacts and make a complicated molecular process tractable at examination level.

4. The repeating elongation cycle

The cycle begins when an appropriate aminoacyl-tRNA arrives at a ribosome with a sense codon in its A site. Delivery is assisted in bacteria by EF-Tu in its GTP-bound form, and in eukaryotic cytosol by the related factor eEF1A. The nucleotide state of the factor affects its interaction with tRNA and with the ribosome. At the first encounter, the anticodon can sample the codon, although the aminoacyl end is not yet in the final position needed for reaction. This arrangement allows sequence discrimination before the cell commits the substrate to peptide formation.

After productive selection, the delivery factor hydrolyzes GTP and undergoes changes that allow its release from the tRNA. The aminoacyl end of an accepted tRNA can then settle into the large-subunit catalytic site. This movement is called accommodation. It provides an additional opportunity to discriminate among substrates, because successful initial pairing and GTP hydrolysis do not guarantee that the tRNA will complete accommodation. Near-cognate substrates can be rejected after the GTP-consuming step. The distinction between initial recognition and final accommodation is central to understanding ribosomal proofreading.

With both reactants correctly positioned, the amino group of the A-site amino acid reacts with the ester linkage attaching the peptide to the P-site tRNA. The peptide is transferred onto the amino group of the incoming residue. The newly elongated peptide remains connected to the tRNA that originally entered the A site, whereas the tRNA that had carried the growing chain becomes deacylated. Neither tRNA disappears from the ribosome at the instant of the chemical reaction. The immediate product is a pre-translocation complex with different chemical states but initially the same codon positions.

The next operation is translocation. In bacteria EF-G facilitates the movement of tRNAs and mRNA, and eukaryotic eEF2 performs the analogous function. A GTP-dependent cycle of binding and conformational transitions promotes a one-codon advance. The peptidyl-tRNA takes the P-site position; the old deacylated tRNA moves toward the E site and subsequently leaves. The next codon becomes available at the A site. If that codon is another sense codon, the entire cycle can begin again.

If the next codon is a stop codon, the same ribosome has reached a different decision point. Ordinary elongator tRNAs do not normally decode standard stop codons as amino acids. Release factors mediate termination instead. Thus the stop codon is not part of a conventional amino-acid addition cycle. It follows the last sense codon, which must itself have been incorporated before termination begins. This timing explains why translation of a message encoding a protein of length n includes n minus one ordinary elongation cycles after initiation, assuming a standard uninterrupted reading frame.

The cycle is best learned as three mechanistically distinct operations with three distinct observables. Selection changes tRNA occupancy and initiates factor turnover. Peptide transfer changes the chemical identity of the tRNA cargo. Translocation changes the reading register of the ribosome. A mutation can disturb one operation and leave the others functional in a prepared experimental system. Accordingly, one should not conclude that any reduction in overall peptide synthesis identifies a defect in peptide-bond catalysis.

5. A complete first round traced through an example

Consider the artificial sequence 5′-AUG GCU UUU GGA UAA-3′. Under the standard code and ordinary bacterial initiation, the first residue is formylmethionine; GCU encodes alanine, UUU encodes phenylalanine, GGA encodes glycine, and UAA is a stop. Ignore subsequent chemical removal of the formyl group for the moment. The chain that is synthesized has four residues. The initial P-site adaptor is paired with AUG and carries formylmethionine. At the A site, GCU presents the recognition problem for the first elongation round.

An appropriately charged alanine tRNA can be delivered by EF-Tu. After selection and accommodation, its amino group attacks the carbonyl associated with the ester-linked formylmethionine in the P site. The resulting dipeptide is formylmethionylalanine. The alanine tRNA now carries the dipeptide; the initiator tRNA is no longer attached to the first residue. This is true even before the ribosome moves along the message. The identity of the first residue has not changed merely because its original tRNA has lost the cargo.

After translocation, GCU occupies the P-site register and UUU occupies the A-site register. Phenylalanine tRNA is selected, the dipeptide is transferred, and the resulting tripeptide is attached to phenylalanine tRNA. In the third cycle GGA supplies glycine. The tetrapeptide remains attached to the glycine tRNA after translocation into the P site. The A site now presents UAA. The ribosome contains the complete sequence but has not yet released the chain into solution. A measurement that detects only free tetrapeptide may therefore underestimate synthesis if termination is selectively blocked.

There are exactly three peptide bonds connecting four residues in a linear tetrapeptide. Each was formed by the transfer of the entire previously made portion of the chain onto a new residue. Nothing in this example requires the old residue-specific tRNAs to stay attached to their corresponding amino acids in the final peptide. The protein is a continuous polymer, not a collection of separate amino acid–tRNA pairs. The number of successful standard elongation rounds is three, not four and not five. The distinction among residue count, bond count, codon count and factor turnovers is the basis of quantitative translation questions.

For a cytosolic eukaryotic ribosome translating the same coding sequence, the initial residue is ordinarily unformylated methionine, and the factors differ by name. The orientation of the template, the order of residue addition and the number of peptide bonds remain the same. In comparing bacteria and eukaryotes, it is therefore important to distinguish universal logical constraints from system-specific initiation details. The same mechanistic reasoning can be applied to most standard examination sequences as long as the assumed genetic code and start position are clear.

6. The delivery factor: recognition through a GTP-dependent cycle

A cell contains a pool of aminoacyl-tRNAs, many of which differ only modestly in their anticodon sequence or nucleotide modifications. Direct movement of all these flexible, reactive molecules into the catalytic center would provide little opportunity to coordinate recognition with chemistry. EF-Tu supplies a controlled route of entry for most bacterial elongator aminoacyl-tRNAs. Its GTP-bound form binds the charged adaptor and produces a three-component complex composed of EF-Tu, GTP and aminoacyl-tRNA. This is commonly called a ternary complex. The aminoacyl end is held by the factor rather than being immediately available for peptide transfer.

The name ternary complex must be interpreted in context. An initiation factor can also participate in a complex of three components, but that does not make it the same molecular assembly. In elongation the incoming tRNA usually carries the next amino acid, not the special initiator role. EF-Tu discriminates against the bacterial initiator tRNA under normal conditions. The first residue has already reached the P site using initiation machinery. The specialization of these pathways ensures that ordinary delivery does not replace the unique event of selecting a start site.

GTP binding stabilizes a conformation of EF-Tu that has high affinity for aminoacyl-tRNA. The factor binds the adaptor in a way that leaves the anticodon region accessible for interaction with the decoding center. The association is not based exclusively on which amino acid the tRNA carries. Contacts with the tRNA body and the aminoacyl moiety together allow a diverse set of correctly charged elongator tRNAs to be delivered. Natural tRNAs have evolved appropriate structural properties for this common route, so one factor can handle substrates associated with many different codons.

At an available A-site codon, the ternary complex initially samples the ribosome. The small subunit can test the potential codon–anticodon interaction before the amino acid enters the peptidyl-transferase center. This early arrangement is often represented as an A/T configuration, where the anticodon region is associated with the A site and the acceptor region is still associated with the delivery factor. The label is a description of the contact pattern, not an additional conventional ribosomal site equivalent to A, P or E.

A favorable codon–anticodon geometry promotes structural rearrangements that couple the decoding center to activation of the factor's GTPase activity. GTP hydrolysis produces GDP and inorganic phosphate. Changes in the nucleotide-dependent conformation and release of phosphate contribute to weakening the interactions that held the aminoacyl-tRNA in the delivery configuration. The factor can disengage, leaving the substrate to complete accommodation or be rejected. Hydrolysis should therefore not be pictured as a small molecule directly pushing the amino acid into a new peptide bond. It changes the direction and timing of a network of molecular interactions.

The delivery factor does not permanently become inactive after one round. EF-Tu in its GDP-bound form must exchange GDP for a fresh GTP molecule before it efficiently delivers another substrate. GDP dissociation can be accelerated by the exchange factor EF-Ts. This factor changes the interaction of EF-Tu with its bound guanine nucleotide, facilitating the replacement step. EF-Ts is not the source of the chemical energy stored in GTP and it does not phosphorylate GDP to synthesize GTP. The cell's nucleotide metabolism maintains the available pool of GTP. EF-Ts assists nucleotide exchange; it is a catalyst for resetting the delivery factor.

This distinction predicts two different phenotypes. If EF-Tu cannot hydrolyze GTP normally, its tRNA complex may bind but fail to progress efficiently into accommodation. If EF-Ts function is lost, pre-existing EF-Tu–GTP can support some successful rounds, but the population of delivery-competent factor can decline as GDP-bound molecules accumulate. A time course may distinguish a failure immediately after substrate recognition from a failure to replenish active factor. In either case the overall synthesis of protein may be reduced, so an endpoint assay of total protein alone cannot determine which step is defective.

In eukaryotic cytosolic translation eEF1A performs the corresponding delivery function and eEF1B provides nucleotide exchange activity through its catalytic component or components. The bacterial and eukaryotic names are important, but their functional correspondence is more useful than a memorized list. Both systems use the nucleotide state of a delivery GTPase to coordinate access of most elongator aminoacyl-tRNAs to the decoding and catalytic centers. Both also require restoration of the GTP-bound population between rounds. A description in which EF-Ts carries amino acids to the bacterial A site, or eEF2 normally delivers charged tRNAs in a eukaryote, exchanges distinct mechanistic roles.

7. Initial selection: from random encounter to codon recognition

A codon is a short sequence with only three nucleotides. Many incorrect anticodons share two of the three expected bases. The decoding apparatus must distinguish these near matches from a much larger pool of unrelated anticodons, and it must do so while using relatively weak, reversible molecular interactions. It would be misleading to assume that a correct tRNA is the only molecule that can ever bind the ribosome. Noncognate and near-cognate tRNAs can encounter the complex and sometimes form temporary contacts. What matters is the probability that a given encounter progresses to the irreversible or effectively committed production of a peptide.

For a specified codon, a cognate tRNA is a tRNA that recognizes it according to the normal decoding rules of the organism. A near-cognate tRNA typically makes a close but incorrect match, for example with a mismatch at one position. A noncognate tRNA has a substantially less appropriate pairing pattern. These categories describe the relationship between a codon and an anticodon in a particular decoding system. A tRNA is not intrinsically cognate to every codon simply because its amino acid has been correctly attached. It can be cognate at one location and a poor competitor at another.

The small subunit does more than allow two strands of RNA to base-pair in solution. Conserved elements of its decoding center make interactions that favor the geometry of appropriate pairing, particularly at the first two positions of the codon. The third position is subject to different constraints, accommodating allowed wobble interactions. This explains the coexistence of accurate discrimination and degeneracy in the genetic code. The code does not require a different rigid decoding geometry for every synonymous codon. Conversely, the existence of wobble does not imply that arbitrary mismatches at the third position are harmless. The structural context of the pair and the particular tRNA are important.

A correct substrate can influence the structure of the binding site itself. This idea is usually called induced fit. It means that the binding event and the conformational response cannot always be separated into a simple model in which a completely rigid receptor accepts or rejects a ligand. Productive codon recognition accelerates subsequent rearrangements that favor GTPase activation and onward progress. Near-cognate interactions may be less stable or may fail to stimulate these transitions efficiently. Thus specificity can arise from both how long a substrate stays bound and how rapidly it enters the productive pathway while it remains bound.

The distinction can be illustrated with a hypothetical encounter that has two possible outcomes: dissociation or forward reaction. A tRNA that dissociates rapidly can still be accepted if its forward reaction is even faster. A tRNA that remains attached for a relatively long time can nonetheless be rejected if productive rearrangement is extremely slow. Consequently affinity alone does not define the final error rate. A measurement of the concentration needed for half-maximal binding is not equivalent to a measurement of the probability that the tRNA makes a peptide bond.

Translation takes place in a crowded environment where the concentrations of potential competitors are unequal. Some tRNA species are more abundant than others, and the fraction that is aminoacylated can change with amino acid availability. The arrival of an incorrect competitor and its conditional probability of being incorporated both influence the frequency of erroneous products. A highly selective ribosome can still be challenged by an altered substrate pool. Equally, a large pool of correctly charged cognate tRNA may support accurate and efficient decoding without any change in the molecular structure of the ribosome.

It is useful to distinguish rejection before GTP hydrolysis from rejection after hydrolysis. Before hydrolysis, an unsuccessful encounter can generally leave with no expenditure of GTP by the ribosome in that delivery attempt. After hydrolysis, rejection is more costly because a guanine nucleotide has already been consumed. The stages do not provide identical information: the first is coupled to codon sampling and activation of the factor, while the later stage tests whether the substrate can continue along the accommodation pathway. The system therefore spends energy on an additional discriminatory step rather than merely on accelerating a single binding reaction.

8. Accommodation and the commitment to peptide transfer

When the aminoacyl-tRNA first participates in codon recognition, its aminoacyl end is physically restrained by the delivery factor. It is not in the same position as a fully accommodated A-site substrate. The transition to accommodation involves a substantial change in the adaptor's contacts. The anticodon region remains associated with the decoding site while the acceptor end moves into the large-subunit catalytic center. In the final state the amino acid is appropriately oriented toward the ester-linked peptide in the P site.

This arrangement separates reading from reacting. If the aminoacyl end of every initially associated tRNA were already positioned to receive the peptide, even a short-lived incorrect pairing could produce a substitution. Delaying access to the catalytic center makes rejection possible while the chain remains safely attached to its preceding adaptor. The movement of the tRNA is not simply a passive drop into an empty cavity. The changing interactions must be compatible with both the decoding complex and the large-subunit environment. Rejected substrates fail to complete this transition and leave before they acquire the chain.

After hydrolysis of GTP, an incorrect tRNA can in principle be released without making a peptide bond. This provides evidence that GTP hydrolysis and chemical incorporation are not equivalent observables. A ribosome may hydrolyze more molecules of GTP through its delivery factors than the number of residues successfully added. In an experiment, the amount of phosphate released can therefore exceed the amount predicted from the simple ideal cycle, even when initiation and translocation have been excluded from the measurement. The excess does not imply that some residues contain multiple peptide bonds to their neighbors; it reflects unsuccessful attempts or other GTPase activity.

A tRNA's acyl group is essential for the standard forward reaction. Deacylated tRNA lacks the amino acid nucleophile required for chain extension. A lack of charged cognate tRNA can slow the arrival of a productive substrate even if the total tRNA concentration has not changed. Thus measuring total tRNA abundance does not necessarily reveal the supply relevant for elongation. Distinguishing charged and uncharged fractions is especially useful when nutrient availability changes. The total quantity of a particular tRNA could remain stable while the effective concentration of its ternary complex decreases markedly.

Accommodation should not be confused with aminoacylation. Aminoacylation is the upstream attachment of an amino acid to tRNA by a synthetase; accommodation is the placement of a charged adaptor into a ribosomal reaction-ready state. A student can separate these processes by asking two questions. Is the tRNA chemically loaded with an amino acid? Is that loaded molecule now correctly positioned for peptide transfer on a ribosome? An affirmative answer to the first question is not sufficient for the second. The separation of these events provides distinct experimental targets and distinct explanations of translational failure.

In a prepared elongation complex, one can distinguish the completion of accommodation from peptide-bond formation by detecting the positions of the reactants or the formation of the chemical product. A conformational probe might report that an adaptor has moved toward the large subunit; peptide analysis is needed to establish that the new covalent bond has formed. In a mechanistic model, these observables correspond to different states. The physical proximity of reactants is necessary for efficient catalysis but does not alone prove that the reaction proceeded. This distinction recurs throughout enzymology and is particularly important for Part C experiments.

9. Why a second selection step improves decoding

A single equilibrium binding event has an intrinsic limitation. Suppose a receptor interacts more favorably with the correct substrate than with an incorrect substrate by a small free-energy difference. At thermal equilibrium the ratio of their occupancies depends on this difference and on their respective concentrations. For closely related substrates, the difference may not yield the specificity needed for highly accurate biosynthesis. Simply allowing the system to equilibrate for a longer time does not create additional discrimination if the final decision still depends on the same equilibrium populations.

The kinetic proofreading concept, developed independently by John Hopfield in 1974 and Jacques Ninio in 1975, addressed this problem. A system can distinguish substrates more effectively by separating recognition into multiple stages and using an energy-consuming transition to keep the process from behaving as one reversible equilibrium. An incorrect molecule that passes the first check can still be eliminated at a later check. If the later decision provides additional discrimination, the combined probability of incorrect incorporation may be much smaller than the probability of surviving either check individually.

The ribosome uses this principle in a chemically specific context. Its initial recognition of the ternary complex can favor the correct codon–anticodon arrangement. GTP hydrolysis separates this recognition stage from further discrimination during accommodation. A mismatch that survives long enough for hydrolysis is not thereby assured of incorporation. After factor release it must still successfully complete the physical rearrangement that places the aminoacyl end in the catalytic center. The extra discriminatory opportunity is maintained by the chemical energy of the nucleotide cycle, not by proofreading the already formed protein sequence backward residue by residue.

The common analogy of a two-door security entrance is useful only if the doors are not imagined as identical and completely independent machines. The second checkpoint may respond to the history of the first and to the conformational state of the same tRNA. In real kinetic schemes different substrates have different association rates, forward rates and rejection rates. The idealized mathematical product of independent survival factors can illustrate the benefit of repeated selection, but it is not a universal formula for all biochemical situations. A proper model must state how the probabilities are defined and whether they are conditional on earlier steps.

The system gains accuracy at a cost. Some correct substrates may also be rejected, so increasing the stringency of selection can slow productive incorporation. A second check that rejects all molecules would have no translation errors among its products because it would make no products at all. Such a system would be biologically useless. The relevant question is how a cell achieves a useful rate of protein synthesis with an acceptable frequency of errors and expenditure of resources. Speed, accuracy and energetic cost are distinct properties that can respond differently to changes in the molecular machinery.

Proofreading is not the same as error correction after product formation. In the normal elongation cycle, the most important opportunity for avoiding a wrong amino acid is before the peptide bond is made. Unlike a DNA polymerase with a proofreading exonuclease, the ribosome does not routinely reverse each last peptide bond and remove an incorrectly incorporated amino acid. Downstream protein quality control can recognize some defective products, but degradation of a whole polypeptide is not the same molecular process as ribosomal selection of aminoacyl-tRNA. Distinguishing prevention, correction and disposal prevents a common misconception about the term proofreading.

10. Quantitative interpretation of substrate discrimination

The idea of repeated selection can be expressed without advanced mathematics. Suppose a newly bound tRNA can either proceed at a rate k_f or dissociate at a rate k_r. Both rate constants have units of inverse time when they describe competing first-order reactions from the same state. Assume that these two processes are the only exits from that state and that their rates remain constant throughout an encounter. The probability density for the molecule to remain in the state until time t and then undergo the forward reaction is k_f multiplied by exp[−(k_f+k_r)t]. Integrating from zero to infinity gives the forward probability k_f/(k_f+k_r). The probability of rejection is k_r/(k_f+k_r). Their sum is one, as expected for two mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes. The average waiting time to either exit is 1/(k_f+k_r).

For example, imagine a cognate substrate whose first-stage forward rate is 90 per second and rejection rate is 10 per second. The probability of passing is 0.90. A near-cognate substrate with a forward rate of 10 per second and a rejection rate of 90 per second passes with probability 0.10. These are hypothetical values, not measured rate constants for a particular ribosome. If the numbers of initial encounters are equal, the ratio of wrong to correct substrates passing this first checkpoint is 0.10/0.90, or one ninth. It is not one tenth because the denominator is the number of correct passes, not the total number of original encounters.

Now assume that, conditional on passing the first checkpoint, a cognate tRNA has a 0.95 probability of completing accommodation while a near-cognate tRNA has a 0.05 probability. For the cognate species the total probability of passing both stages is 0.90 × 0.95 = 0.855. For the near-cognate species it is 0.10 × 0.05 = 0.005. The wrong-to-correct product ratio for equal encounter frequencies is therefore 0.005/0.855, approximately 0.00585, or one wrong product for every 171 correct products. The fraction of all products that are wrong is instead 0.005/(0.855+0.005), approximately 0.00581. The two quantities are similar at low error frequencies, but they are not algebraically identical.

The conditional probability language is essential. Let C represent cognate substrate and W represent wrong substrate. Define p_1,C as the chance that an initial encounter of C passes step one, and p_2,C as the chance that an encounter of C that has already passed step one also passes step two. The overall chance is p_1,C p_2,C by the ordinary product rule for conditional probabilities. No assumption of statistical independence between the two checks is required because the second probability has already been defined conditionally. The same notation applies to W. If the second probability were defined over all initial encounters instead, multiplying it by the first would count some rejection events twice.

An actual ribosome may encounter many varieties of wrong substrate, not only one. Let J_i denote the encounter frequency of substrate i per unit time at an available A site, and let p_i denote its conditional probability of eventual incorporation from one such encounter. In a simplified renewal model with a fixed substrate pool and no persistent memory of failed encounters, its contribution to successful events is proportional to J_i p_i. The fraction of successful events involving wrong substrates is the sum of J_i p_i over the wrong species divided by the corresponding sum over all candidate species. If two incorrect tRNAs have different arrival rates and success probabilities, their contributions must be added as rates, not averaged as percentages without weighting.

Consider a cognate tRNA with an encounter frequency of 50 arbitrary events per unit time and a success probability of 0.8. The effective weight for its correct product is 40. A wrong species encounters at a frequency of 100 and succeeds in 0.002 of encounters, so its effective weight is 0.2. A second wrong species encounters at 10 and succeeds with probability 0.01, contributing 0.1. The wrong-product fraction is 0.3/40.3, or about 0.744 percent. Although the second wrong species has five times the success probability of the first, its smaller encounter frequency makes its total contribution lower. This reasoning is important whenever an examination problem changes both a kinetic discrimination parameter and the abundance of a tRNA.

The use of effective encounter frequencies in this example is a simplification. An A site occupied by a slowly rejected tRNA is temporarily unavailable to others. If some encounters are much longer than others, the overall throughput depends on the durations of unsuccessful attempts as well as on the probabilities of successful attempts. The product composition under a simple independent renewal process can still be computed from event probabilities, but converting the weights into absolute product flux requires a time accounting. This is why a preparation that rejects most wrong substrates can nevertheless show slow elongation in a mixture containing a large amount of a slowly dissociating competitor.

Thermodynamic reasoning gives another view. In a simple equilibrium model with equal substrate concentrations, a difference in standard binding free energy of ΔΔG = G_bind,wrong − G_bind,correct produces an equilibrium wrong-to-correct occupancy ratio exp(−ΔΔG/RT), where R is the molar gas constant and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, RT is about 2.48 kilojoules per mole. A favorable difference of 5.7 kilojoules per mole would give a ratio close to 0.10. A tenfold preference can be useful, but not sufficient by itself for very high fidelity. The energy-consuming sequence of selection transitions enables a system to do more than simply select once from this equilibrium mixture.

It is tempting to claim that kinetic proofreading always squares the equilibrium discrimination factor. That is only a property of certain idealized models and limiting conditions. Real substrates can differ in induced-fit kinetics, rates of accommodation, association frequencies and rejection pathways. The mathematical examples in this chapter illustrate mechanisms and make predictions under specified assumptions; they are not universal quantitative measurements of translation error.

11. Peptide transfer: the chemical reaction and its substrates

A polypeptide is a sequence of amino acid residues connected through amide linkages. During synthesis the relevant carbonyl is at the carboxyl-derived end of the preceding residue, and the relevant attacking group is the amino group of the incoming amino acid. The ribosome must therefore bring two chemically distinct reactive groups close together in a controlled orientation. It must also ensure that the product stays attached to an adaptor so that the next amino acid can be added to the same growing chain.

Consider the ordinary reaction using an incoming amino acid whose α-amino group is represented by H2N–CHR–. Its carboxyl group is linked as an ester to its own tRNA. The donor peptide has its terminal carboxyl group esterified to the P-site tRNA. In simplified chemical notation the donor is peptide–C(=O)–O–tRNA_P and the acceptor is H2N–CHR–C(=O)–O–tRNA_A. The amino group of the acceptor attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the donor ester. The reaction yields peptide–C(=O)–NH–CHR–C(=O)–O–tRNA_A and releases the P-site tRNA with a free hydroxyl group. The new amide bond is the connection between the old chain and the incoming residue.

At the first moment after reaction, the growing chain is therefore attached to the A-site tRNA. This fact is independent of the names or amino acid identities of the substrates. It follows directly from the chemistry: the carboxyl group of the incoming residue was already attached to its own tRNA before the reaction, and that linkage is not the one attacked during peptide transfer. The nucleophile is on the incoming amino acid, and the bond that breaks is the donor peptide's ester linkage to its old tRNA. The product retains the incoming residue's ester linkage, making that residue the next terminal donor when the cycle repeats.

The standard schematic often describes peptide-bond formation as a condensation between two free amino acids with the loss of a water molecule. That reaction is useful for representing the composition of an isolated dipeptide, but it is not the actual substrate-level mechanism of translation. On a ribosome the donor carboxyl group is already activated as an ester, and the chemical reaction is an acyl transfer from oxygen to nitrogen. The leaving group is associated with the deacylated tRNA, not a molecule of water eliminated from two free amino acids. This difference explains why aminoacylation upstream of the ribosome is indispensable for standard translation.

The ribosome does not form a peptide bond by adding a residue to the N terminus of the old chain. If the chain initially reads N–Met–Ala–Phe–C, its last residue Phe has the esterified carboxyl group attached to the current tRNA. An incoming glycine presents its amino group to that carbonyl. The new sequence is N–Met–Ala–Phe–Gly–C. The orientation follows from the chemical arrangement and does not require a separate directional motor to decide which protein end grows. The mRNA-reading direction is enforced by the order of codon exposure, while the chain-growth direction is enforced by the acyl-transfer chemistry.

The reaction is a nucleophilic acyl substitution. Electron redistribution around the carbonyl during attack leads through a tetrahedral configuration along the reaction pathway, and subsequent rearrangement enables departure of the tRNA-linked oxygen group. Appropriate proton transfers accompany the reaction so that the final amide and hydroxyl products have the expected bonding patterns. At examination level the important features are the identities of the nucleophile, electrophile, leaving group and final acyl carrier. One need not assign a single universal detailed proton-transfer route to every ribosome and substrate in order to explain these features.

A special case illustrates why precise terminology matters. Proline has a secondary α-amino group rather than the primary amino group found in many other proteinogenic amino acids. Its incorporation produces a peptide nitrogen with a different substitution pattern, but the fundamental direction of attack and transfer of the growing chain are unchanged. Certain proline-containing sequences are difficult substrates for rapid peptide transfer, which is one reason auxiliary elongation factors can improve synthesis of particular motifs. This exception concerns the rate and substrate geometry of the conserved reaction, not a separate genetic code for proline.

12. The peptidyl-transferase center is a catalytic RNA environment

The ribosome is often introduced as a ribonucleoprotein because it contains both RNA and proteins. That compositional description does not by itself identify which class of molecule forms the catalytic center for peptide synthesis. Structural and biochemical studies established that conserved large-subunit ribosomal RNA is central to the peptidyl-transferase reaction. In bacteria the relevant large rRNA is 23S rRNA; eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes contain the homologous catalytic region within their large-subunit rRNA, called 28S in mammals. The precise nomenclature differs among eukaryotes, but the conserved organization of the catalytic region is the main biological point.

In 2000, structural work by Nissen, Hansen, Ban, Moore and Steitz examined the large ribosomal subunit of an archaeon with substrate analogs. The position of those analogs placed the reaction center among conserved rRNA components rather than a conventional protein enzyme active site. This provided compelling structural evidence for the ribosome as an RNA catalyst. An important distinction is that the study was not an experiment in which all proteins were removed from a functioning modern ribosome and translation proceeded normally. Instead, it located substrate-like molecules in the organized environment of an assembled large subunit and helped identify the chemical nature of the active site.

Catalysis can arise from several contributions. Reactants are brought into a favorable relative orientation; conformations that would be unproductive in solution are constrained; an appropriate environment supports the reaction and its associated proton movements. The ribosome also positions the tRNA acceptor ends so that the product remains covalently attached to the correct carrier. The catalytic environment includes important contributions from the substrates themselves, particularly the terminal ribose of the P-site tRNA. It would be an oversimplification to attribute all catalysis to one specific rRNA base acting exactly like a classical protein general-base catalyst.

A reaction can be accelerated by a catalyst without the catalyst being a source of the chemical free energy driving the reaction. The activated ester at the donor tRNA is a crucial energetic feature of peptide transfer. The ribosome helps convert an existing activated bond into a peptide bond and the released tRNA; it does not hydrolyze GTP at the peptidyl-transferase center as a direct chemical reagent for forming each amide linkage. GTP is consumed at other stages of the standard elongation cycle, supporting substrate selection and directional movement. Separating the sources of chemical activation from the costs of coordinating the machine is essential for correct ATP and GTP bookkeeping.

The classification of the ribosome as a ribozyme has evolutionary significance because it demonstrates that RNA can have both informational and catalytic roles in contemporary cells. The messenger RNA provides sequence information, the transfer RNAs provide adaptors and the ribosomal RNA contributes to a catalytic molecular machine. This combination is consistent with ideas about an early evolutionary role for RNA, but it does not directly prove the exact history of the first biological translation system. A distinction must be maintained between structural evidence about an existing ribosome and a historical inference about ancient biology.

The catalytic role of RNA also does not mean that ribosomal proteins are unimportant. Proteins help assemble and stabilize ribosome structure, influence conformational transitions and provide interaction surfaces within the translation machinery. A mutation in a protein component can alter the behavior of the RNA-based catalytic center indirectly. Therefore, finding a protein mutation that reduces peptide formation does not refute the conclusion that rRNA constitutes the core catalytic environment. The functional phenotype of an assembled complex reflects the contributions of its components to both local chemistry and global organization.

Evidence and further reading: Nissen et al. (2000).

13. Chemical activation and the energetic logic of the peptide bond

The peptide-bond reaction cannot be understood by counting nucleotide hydrolyses alone. An unactivated carboxyl group is not a convenient acyl donor for a rapid controlled reaction with another free amino acid in the aqueous environment of a cell. Before translation, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases use ATP to form an aminoacyl adenylate and transfer the aminoacyl group onto tRNA. The resulting ester is an activated derivative of the carboxyl group. When the residue eventually participates in peptide transfer as the terminal donor, this ester linkage provides a chemically suitable substrate for acyl substitution.

The two steps of charging can be represented separately. Amino acid plus ATP yields aminoacyl-AMP and pyrophosphate. The activated aminoacyl group is transferred to a tRNA hydroxyl, releasing AMP. Pyrophosphate can then be hydrolyzed to two inorganic phosphates. These reactions help sustain directionality in the formation of charged tRNA. The net result is not that ATP remains bound to the tRNA until it arrives at the ribosome; rather, the chemical products of the nucleotide reaction have separated from the charged adaptor. The activation is stored in the acyl linkage itself.

A chain that contains many residues has consumed many such charging reactions, although the ribosome never carries the entire set of original tRNAs at once. The tRNA attached to the newest residue supplies the linkage that persists between successive rounds of elongation. In the next cycle, that linkage becomes the donor ester. Accordingly, the chemical activation associated with an incoming amino acid does not vanish immediately when its α-amino group receives the chain. The incoming amino acid's carboxyl linkage is retained in the product; it will serve as the donor connection in a later round or be hydrolyzed at termination.

This observation also clarifies a subtlety in energy accounting. A protein consisting of n residues contains n−1 peptide bonds but requires n correctly charged aminoacyl-tRNAs if all charging is counted from free amino acids, including the initiator. It is not sufficient to count charging only for residues that arrived at the A site after initiation when the question asks for synthesis from free amino acids. If the initial P-site tRNA and all subsequent aminoacyl-tRNAs are provided as already charged substrates, by contrast, their charging energy has already been spent before the defined experiment begins. Which processes are included in the accounting is part of the problem statement.

The standard energy cost assigned to an ATP-to-AMP conversion is two high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents when pyrophosphate hydrolysis and restoration of ATP from AMP are taken into account. This does not mean that two ATP molecules were consumed by the synthetase itself. One ATP molecule enters each ordinary activation event and is converted to AMP rather than ADP. Restoration of the original nucleotide energy state requires two phosphoryl-transfer equivalents. If a problem asks for ATP molecules, count one per activation. If it asks for high-energy phosphate equivalents, count two per activation under the standard convention.

GTP-dependent factor reactions differ in that each ordinary GTP-to-GDP conversion is counted as one equivalent. A successful elongation round normally includes one GTP hydrolysis associated with aminoacyl-tRNA delivery and one associated with EF-G or eEF2. Neither of these nucleotide molecules supplies a phosphate group that is incorporated into the protein chain. Their turnover organizes binding, selection and movement. In a biological system, additional events, including rejected substrates, maintenance of nucleotide pools and other protein-processing reactions, may increase expenditure beyond the idealized cost.

The energetic contributions thus form a relay of different types. ATP-dependent chemical activation establishes a useful acyl substrate; GTP-dependent conformational cycles help deliver that substrate selectively and reset the ribosome's reading register. The peptidyl-transferase center uses the properly positioned activated substrate for bond formation. These operations are coupled through the state of the translation complex, even though their chemical reactions occur at different sites and times. The student should not assign the entire energetic cost of protein synthesis to the formation of the peptide bond itself.

14. The pre-translocation state and hybrid tRNA configurations

Immediately after peptide transfer, two facts have changed: the old P-site tRNA is deacylated, and the old A-site tRNA now carries the elongated peptide. The message has not necessarily advanced by a complete codon at that instant. The apparent contradiction in describing a peptidyl-tRNA in the aminoacyl site is resolved when the site names are recognized as locations rather than strict chemical definitions. The A site can transiently contain a peptidyl-tRNA, even though an aminoacyl-tRNA normally enters through it.

A tRNA spans two subunits, and its two ends need not move simultaneously. The standard classical-site notation writes two letters separated by a slash to describe the locations of the anticodon end on the small subunit and the acceptor end on the large subunit, respectively. A classical A/A tRNA occupies corresponding A regions on both subunits. A P/P tRNA similarly occupies P regions on both. After peptide transfer, an A/P configuration describes the new peptidyl-tRNA whose anticodon remains in the A register on the small subunit while its acceptor end occupies the P region on the large subunit. The old deacylated tRNA can adopt a P/E configuration. These hybrid states provide a useful intermediate between the chemical step and completed translocation.

The evidence for intermediate configurations was strengthened by work reported by Dmitri Moazed and Harry Noller in 1989. They used chemical probing of ribosomal RNA to investigate contacts made by tRNAs in different elongation states. The patterns of protection showed that after peptide formation, changes associated with the large subunit could occur before the complete shift relative to the small subunit. This supported a staged model of movement. The experiment did not consist of directly watching an entire movie of each atom in a translating ribosome. Rather, differences in chemical accessibility provided evidence about the positions of molecular contacts.

The logic of a protection experiment is that a nucleotide which becomes less accessible to a chemical reagent upon tRNA binding can reveal the region occupied or influenced by that tRNA. Comparisons across known complexes can help assign contact changes to a particular stage. However, a protected nucleotide is not always a direct contact with the tRNA, because a conformational rearrangement can also alter accessibility. Multiple probes, reference complexes and other structural information help interpret protection patterns. The power of this method is in comparing controlled states rather than treating every single protection signal as an independently exact position measurement.

The two principal subunits also undergo relative movements, commonly described as rotation or ratcheting, during the elongation cycle. The head of the small subunit can adopt different orientations as movement progresses. These are not separate ATP-powered machines attached to each subunit. They are conformational transitions of the ribosome itself, coordinated with tRNA interactions and elongation-factor binding. Thermal motion enables fluctuations among accessible states; chemical changes and nucleotide-dependent factor interactions favor particular transitions. A ribosome can therefore use its own flexibility without sacrificing the overall directionality required to preserve the reading frame.

A common examination diagram shows two tRNAs moving by a full site together immediately after peptide transfer. Such a diagram is a useful summary of the net outcome but does not depict all intermediate states. If a question explicitly distinguishes acceptor-end movement from anticodon-end movement, the hybrid-site description is necessary. If the question asks only for final P- and E-site occupancy after a completed translocation event, the simple classical-site model is adequate. One should not apply an unnecessarily complicated intermediate model to a simple endpoint question, nor use an endpoint cartoon to deny the existence of experimentally supported intermediates.

Evidence and further reading: Moazed and Noller (1989).

15. EF-G and eEF2: resetting the ribosome for the next codon

In bacteria the major factor promoting translocation is EF-G, a GTP-binding protein distinct from EF-Tu. It interacts with the ribosome after peptide formation and helps coordinate the rearrangements by which tRNAs and mRNA change their positions relative to the decoding center. Its GTPase cycle is coupled to movement, conformational transitions and efficient progression toward a post-translocation state. In eukaryotic cytosol the corresponding factor is eEF2. Although the factors are not identical in their complete structures, their central elongation functions are analogous.

The role of EF-G is sometimes illustrated as pushing the tRNAs physically from one site to the next. This mechanical analogy is helpful for remembering direction, but it can create an incorrect picture of a rigid motor acting on static objects. The ribosome has already acquired a different chemical and conformational state after peptide transfer. The movement of the acceptor ends can begin without a full EF-G-driven advance of the message. Factor binding and its nucleotide-dependent conformational cycle help bias the transitions and promote productive movement of the mRNA–tRNA complex. Completion and rapid factor turnover are part of the overall GTP-dependent process.

The peptidyl-tRNA must end in the P-site register because its attached terminal residue will be the donor for the next peptide bond. The deacylated tRNA must leave the P position because it can no longer provide a peptide donor. The A-site register must become available to the following sense codon. A successful translocation event accomplishes all three requirements through coordinated movement. If the ribosome were advanced without accompanying the relevant tRNA–mRNA interactions, the connection between the chain and the message's reading register could be lost.

The old tRNA does not need to exit at exactly the same instant that the next codon becomes available. E-site occupancy is a dynamic feature of a translating complex, and the precise timing of dissociation can vary. The E site facilitates release of a spent adaptor as the cycle proceeds, allowing it to return to the soluble tRNA pool for recharging. An empty E site is not itself evidence that elongation has not occurred. Similarly, the presence of a deacylated tRNA in the E region need not mean the ribosome is irreversibly stalled.

GTP hydrolysis and phosphate release are chemically distinguishable events. Descriptions at the level of the complete cycle normally state that EF-G hydrolyzes one GTP per successful standard translocation, and that this energy-dependent cycle promotes forward movement. The detailed timing of hydrolysis, phosphate release and individual molecular motions can depend on the experimental system and the particular state observed. A statement that every elementary movement is physically caused at the exact moment of GTP cleavage is stronger than the usual evidence supports. The safe mechanistic conclusion is that factor binding and nucleotide-dependent transitions enable rapid, ordered movement and recycling.

A useful comparison is with the delivery factor. EF-Tu in its GTP-bound form associates with a charged tRNA and participates in recognition before peptide formation. EF-G acts on a ribosomal complex that has already completed the current peptide-transfer step. Both are GTPases, but the similarity in nucleotide use does not make their substrates or their stage assignments interchangeable. A preparation supplied with abundant EF-Tu but lacking effective EF-G may complete a peptide transfer and accumulate a pre-translocation complex. More delivery factor cannot by itself restore the missing movement. Conversely, EF-G cannot supply a cognate aminoacyl-tRNA when its delivery machinery is defective.

A nonhydrolyzable GTP analog can be useful in experimental analysis because it can stabilize certain nucleotide-bound complexes and interfere with normal progression or factor recycling. The outcome depends on the factor being investigated and the properties of the analog. A complex trapped by such an analog need not be identical to the unperturbed intermediate in every structural detail. This is an important limitation when using trapped states to infer the natural sequence. Nevertheless, comparisons among GTP-bound, GDP-bound and analog-stabilized states can help establish how nucleotide-dependent factors participate in the cycle.

16. Maintaining the reading frame during movement

The ribosome must advance by one codon rather than by an arbitrary distance because a coding sequence contains overlapping potential triplets when no frame is specified. Take the sequence 5′-AUG GCU UUU GGA-3′. Its first selected frame groups the bases into AUG, GCU, UUU and GGA. Starting one nucleotide later produces UGG, CUU and UUG as successive complete triplets. A two-nucleotide shift produces GGC, UUU and UGG. The same chemical message can therefore encode radically different amino acid orders depending on the initial register. The function of translocation is not simply to move forward but to maintain the register established at initiation.

Stable codon–anticodon interactions help couple the mRNA position to the adaptor positions as the complex advances. The ribosome also makes contacts with the mRNA and constrains its orientation through the decoding region. Because the mRNA and tRNA interactions change as the subunits rotate and rearrange, productive movement requires coordinated release and reformation of contacts rather than permanent locking of every molecular pair. The resulting net movement normally exposes the next triplet while preserving the correct chain-to-message relationship.

A decoding error and a frameshift are distinct events. In a missense decoding error, an inappropriate tRNA can introduce the wrong amino acid for one codon, after which the ribosome may still continue in the original reading frame. In a frameshift, the register changes so that subsequent codons are read differently until a compensating change or termination event. A nucleotide insertion or deletion in the coding sequence can alter the frame at the template level, whereas translational frameshifting can occur on a physically unchanged RNA. Neither should be confused with the normal three-nucleotide translocation event.

Certain natural messages exploit programmed ribosomal frameshifting. Sequence context and structures in the mRNA can favor a defined change in the reading register, thereby altering the relative production of different proteins from the same message. Such exceptions are not evidence that ordinary elongation lacks directionality. They demonstrate that the general mechanism can be influenced by local sequence features and dynamics. The details of virus-specific regulatory strategies belong to later gene-expression topics; the relevant concept here is the distinction between a deliberate message-dependent shift and an accidental failure of normal frame maintenance.

A selective block of translocation can reduce full-length protein synthesis even when all codons are correctly recognized and the initial peptide-transfer reaction works. An experimental ribosome might accumulate peptide-bearing tRNA in a pre-translocation configuration. The next codon would not be efficiently presented to the decoding center, and repeated rounds would cease. If a test detects a new peptide bond but fails to detect the expected shift in a footprint or toeprint, the combination supports a movement defect rather than a primary inability to catalyze peptide formation. The result is most convincing when the same complex is shown to initiate correctly and accept the intended tRNA.

In a simple diagram, the message is often drawn as moving from right to left through a fixed ribosome. Another diagram keeps the mRNA fixed and moves the ribosome from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end. These are equivalent relative descriptions if the orientations are labeled consistently. A diagram's page direction is not the biochemical direction. The decisive evidence is the order in which codons enter the A site and the maintenance of the appropriate three-base register.

17. Counting elongation cycles, nucleotides and energy

Quantitative questions on translation become straightforward once the boundary of the calculation is defined. Let n be the number of amino acid residues in a linear polypeptide immediately after synthesis, before any processing removes residues. Assume a normal single reading frame, one initiator residue, no recoding, no failed tRNA selection attempts and a complete translation event. There are n sense codons, including the start codon, and an additional stop codon. The portion of the RNA from the start codon through the stop codon contains 3(n+1) nucleotides. Untranslated regions, introns of the original gene, and the poly(A) tail are not included in this count.

The initiation complex already contains the first residue in its P site. Consequently n−1 further residues must be delivered in ordinary elongation rounds. There are n−1 peptide bonds and n−1 completed ordinary translocations, including the last movement that brings the final peptidyl-tRNA to the P site and exposes the stop codon at the A site. In the special mathematical case n=1, the chain contains no peptide bonds and no ordinary amino-acid-addition cycle; initiation and termination are still conceptually distinct events. This boundary case is a useful check on the expression n−1, because a formula that predicts one elongation cycle for a single-residue product would be counting the initiator incorrectly.

Each of the n−1 ordinary rounds uses one GTP for delivery and one for translocation in the ideal cycle. Thus the elongation-specific GTP consumption is 2(n−1). If the problem begins with precharged tRNAs and an assembled initiation complex, the charging and initiation costs must not be charged again to the elongation phase. If instead the problem asks for synthesis from free amino acids, n ordinary aminoacylation reactions consume n ATP molecules converted to AMP. Expressed in high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents, the charging contribution is 2n. The sum of charging and elongation costs is therefore 2n+2(n−1)=4n−2 equivalents, before any initiation, termination, recycling or additional proofreading costs have been included.

For a 100-residue bacterial protein, the ordinary elongation rounds number 99. They consume 198 GTP molecules in the ideal accounting. Charging consumes 100 ATP molecules, equivalent to 200 high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents. The combined charging and elongation total is 398 equivalents. The coding sequence including the stop codon contains 303 nucleotides. If a question explicitly adds one GTP for initiation and one for termination, the stipulated total becomes 400 equivalents. This last total is not a universal value for every organism and every choice of system boundary; it follows from the additional assumptions of this particular calculation.

A typical bacterial initiation model includes IF2-dependent GTP hydrolysis. Termination and ribosome recycling can involve additional GTPases, including RF3 where applicable and EF-G in bacterial recycling. Eukaryotic initiation has its own additional GTP- and ATP-dependent steps, including nucleotide consumption by initiation factors and ATP-dependent activities associated with scanning. The exact cost of these steps can vary with the definition of the system and the machinery involved. It is therefore safer to specify which events are counted than to memorize a single formula for the total chemical expenditure of all translation-related processes.

The familiar approximation of four high-energy phosphate equivalents per residue is convenient for large proteins because the constant boundary corrections become small relative to n. It hides the fact that the first residue enters by a different route and that the cost of termination is not proportional to the number of residues. If n is very small, the approximation is less useful. If n is large, it can provide a good estimate of the minimum energetic burden of producing a protein when only the main charging and elongation reactions are considered.

Consider a protein of 250 residues. A problem asks for the number of GTP molecules consumed after initiation but before release factors enter. The answer under the ideal cycle is 2 × 249 = 498. The last translocation is included because the stop codon must become accessible before release factors enter. If the experiment instead stops immediately after the final peptide bond forms, without completing the last translocation, the number of delivery turnovers remains 249 while the number of completed translocation turnovers is 248, giving 497 GTP molecules under the simplified counting convention. In real transient intermediates, a factor may have hydrolyzed GTP before translocation has been completed; the question must define whether turnover or completed movements are being measured.

The difference of one GTP in this example may look trivial, but it reflects a major mechanistic distinction. The product can have its complete amino acid sequence without yet being in the correct register for release. By counting events rather than assuming that each stage automatically follows the preceding one, a student can reason about partially completed translation reactions. This ability is more valuable in an analytical examination than memorizing a total for an unspecified endpoint.

18. Translational fidelity at the level of a whole protein

The fidelity of decoding is often described as an error probability per residue or per decoded codon. A long protein involves many opportunities for errors, so the chance of at least one error is not equal to the error probability for a single residue. Let p denote a constant independent probability that each of m decoding events introduces an incorrect amino acid. The probability of a correct event is 1−p. Under the independence assumption, the probability that all m events are correct is the product (1−p)^m. The probability of at least one error is its complement, 1−(1−p)^m. In a standard elongation-only model m=n−1; a problem that includes an independently modeled initiator error may instead specify m=n.

For a hypothetical 301-residue protein in which the 300 elongation decoding events have error probability 0.0001 each, the expected number of errors is 300 × 0.0001 = 0.03. This expected value is not the probability of at least one error, although they are close when errors are rare. The probability of an error-free elongation sequence is 0.9999 raised to the power 300, approximately 0.97044. The probability of at least one error is approximately 0.02956, or 2.96 percent. For 10,000 such independently synthesized proteins, the expected number containing at least one elongation error is about 296. There is no requirement that exactly 296 will be observed in a finite sample.

The expected number of individual errors over m trials is mp, by linearity of expectation. This result does not itself require independent trials when each trial has the same marginal probability. The formula for the probability of no errors, however, uses independence. This distinction matters because molecular errors can have correlated causes. A transient depletion of a specific charged tRNA might affect several occurrences of its codon within a transcript. Some errors or perturbations might also change the probability of later stalls or decoding problems. A simple independent model is a teaching model, not an assertion that all cells and all codons have identical error behavior.

A more general independent model assigns a probability p_i to event i. The probability that every event is correct becomes the product of (1−p_i) across all events. For example, suppose a protein includes 100 ordinary sites with error probability 0.0001 and 20 unusually error-prone sites with probability 0.002 in a hypothetical altered cell. Its error-free probability is 0.9999^100 × 0.998^20, approximately 0.9512. The 20 higher-risk sites contribute an expected 0.04 errors, compared with 0.01 from the 100 ordinary sites. A relatively small subset of codons can therefore dominate the error burden when their probabilities are sufficiently different.

A distinction should be made between an incorrect amino acid sequence and a loss of biological function. Even a protein with a substituted residue may retain its normal activity, depending on the position and substitution. Conversely a completely correct sequence can fail to fold under unfavorable cellular conditions. The probability of an error-free sequence is not automatically the probability of a functional protein. A reporter assay that relies on the activity of one enzyme can be sensitive to some errors and insensitive to others. A direct measurement of error frequency requires an appropriate method and a clear definition of what counts as an error.

The accuracy cost can also be analyzed at the level of repeated GTP consumption. Suppose that each intended successful elongation delivery requires an average of 1.2 delivery-associated GTP hydrolyses because some tRNAs are rejected after hydrolysis. Assume one translocation-associated GTP per successful round and no other expenses. For 200 successful rounds, the expected delivery GTP count is 240 and the translocation GTP count is 200, giving 440 total. The ideal count without post-hydrolysis rejection is 400. The extra expected expenditure is 40 GTP. A fractional average does not imply a single ribosome can hydrolyze a fraction of a nucleotide; it represents a mean over many cycles or molecules.

Under a simplified geometric-trial model, if each GTP-consuming delivery attempt independently succeeds with probability q, the mean number of such attempts per success is 1/q. The mean wasted attempts per successful event are (1−q)/q. This follows because the probability of requiring exactly j attempts is (1−q)^(j−1)q, for j from one upward. With q=0.8, the mean number of hydrolyses per successful delivery is 1.25, and the expected wasted count is 0.25. The model excludes failures before hydrolysis because those do not contribute to this specific energy counter. It also assumes that each failed attempt resets to the same statistical starting condition. Stating these assumptions is important when translating the model into biological predictions.

19. Rates, bottlenecks and the duration of elongation

Elongation rate can be reported as amino acid residues incorporated per second per active ribosome. If a ribosome adds residues at an approximately constant rate v and n−1 additions are required, the idealized elongation duration is (n−1)/v. A 301-residue product with 300 additions at an assumed constant rate of 15 residues per second requires 20 seconds of elongation. Initiation, termination and intervals of inactivity are excluded. The example uses an assigned rate for calculation rather than asserting a universal speed of translation in a particular cell type.

The duration of a single round can be analyzed as a sum of sequential waiting times. If productive delivery averages 20 milliseconds, peptide transfer averages 5 milliseconds and translocation averages 25 milliseconds, the mean duration is 50 milliseconds under the assumption that these are consecutive nonoverlapping contributions to the round. The corresponding throughput for a sustained sequence of such rounds is approximately 20 residues per second. If a mutation doubles only the translocation time to 50 milliseconds, the new total becomes 75 milliseconds, corresponding to about 13.3 residues per second. The overall rate has not fallen by half, because the unaffected operations still contribute to the duration.

The slowest individual step is sometimes called rate limiting. This phrase is most useful when one operation dominates the total duration by a substantial margin. If several operations contribute comparable waiting times, changing any one of them may influence the rate without fully controlling it. In a translating cell the bottleneck can also shift with substrate supply, codon identity, local sequence and factor availability. A loss of a charged tRNA species can make delivery particularly slow at the codons it normally recognizes; a movement defect can affect many codons without the same sequence specificity. A measurement of a mean translation rate can conceal these distinct local behaviors.

A series of codons does not necessarily have one fixed duration for every addition. If 90 additions each require an average of 0.05 second and 10 difficult additions each require 0.20 second, the total expected duration is 4.5 + 2 = 6.5 seconds. The effective mean rate is 100/6.5, approximately 15.38 residues per second. Taking the unweighted average of the two local rates would give a misleading result because the two groups contain different numbers of events and different waiting times. When predicting the completion time of a sequence, sum the times rather than simply averaging instantaneous rates.

The rate of protein production from an mRNA also depends on the frequency of successful initiation and the number of ribosomes simultaneously translating it. A ribosome can take longer to complete one protein without necessarily reducing the steady-state output in exact proportion if initiation is already much slower than elongation and no ribosome traffic interferes. Under different conditions, a long pause may create queues of ribosomes and reduce output. The distinction between transit time and production flux is important. A pulse-labeling experiment that measures the time to complete newly initiated chains answers a different question from a steady-state experiment that measures how many finished molecules are produced per minute.

These quantitative examples connect elongation to broader cell physiology. A growing cell needs enough active ribosomes and charged tRNAs to maintain protein synthesis at a rate compatible with its needs. Increasing initiation alone does not guarantee a proportional increase in completed proteins if the supply of elongation substrates or the capacity for translocation is limiting. Conversely a faster translocation factor will not restore the synthesis of a protein when its mRNA is absent. Mechanistic reasoning therefore requires defining the level at which the system is constrained.

20. Experimental separation of selection, chemistry and movement

A mechanistic experiment should be designed around an observable that distinguishes the proposed step from alternatives. Measuring the amount of a complete protein synthesized by a cell is useful as an overall functional assay, but it is not sufficient to identify the exact defect. Initiation, tRNA charging, decoding, peptide transfer, translocation and termination all contribute to this final product. To study elongation specifically, investigators can begin with a defined initiation or elongation complex whose mRNA position and tRNA cargo are known. Supplying the missing substrate or factor then initiates a much more restricted reaction than adding a DNA template to a cell.

A classical cell-free translation approach uses ribosomes, soluble translation components and a defined message to examine amino acid incorporation. Nirenberg and Matthaei's 1961 experiments with synthetic polyuridylic acid helped establish the relationship between a repeated RNA sequence and phenylalanine incorporation. That experiment is often discussed in the history of the genetic code. Its relevance here is the ability to separate protein synthesis from an intact cell and control the sequence presented to the translational machinery. It did not by itself identify all elongation factors or prove the complete pathway of GTP-dependent proofreading. Later reconstitution and kinetic studies addressed more specific molecular questions.

Imagine three aliquots of identical prepared ribosomes with an initiator tRNA in the P site and a known sense codon in the A site. All receive an appropriately charged incoming tRNA. Aliquot A receives all required factors and GTP. Aliquot B lacks delivery factor. Aliquot C receives delivery factor but lacks the translocation factor. The control is expected to progress through delivery, peptide transfer and movement. Aliquot B should show greatly reduced normal factor-dependent substrate delivery under conditions in which spontaneous entry is negligible. Aliquot C may form a dipeptide but fail to efficiently expose the following codon. The different outcomes would show that peptide formation and message movement can be separated experimentally.

The predictions are conditional, not universal statements that no peptide can ever form without either factor. At sufficiently high substrate concentration or under specially chosen conditions, some normally factor-assisted reactions can occur at a low rate without their usual protein factors. A rigorous experiment therefore measures rates or fractions of product in a specified time window and includes a negative control. Reintroducing a purified functional factor to the corresponding deficient preparation helps establish whether the loss is specific. A heat-inactivated or inactive factor control can help distinguish the effect of adding a protein from the effect of restoring its particular activity.

The detection of peptide products can use a labeled amino acid in a defined experimental system. If the label is associated with the incoming residue, its presence in the isolated peptide reports incorporation, provided unreacted aminoacyl-tRNA and free amino acid are adequately distinguished. A suitable product separation can distinguish the expected dipeptide from longer products. If only total label associated with ribosomes is measured, the signal might instead represent a bound but unreacted tRNA. Thus binding and covalent incorporation must be distinguished in the design, just as they are distinguished mechanistically.

A separate readout can follow mRNA register. Toeprinting uses reverse transcription from a primer on the mRNA; a ribosomal complex can impede the reverse transcriptase and create a characteristic stop pattern. A comparison between properly defined pre-translocation and post-translocation complexes can reveal a shift associated with the movement of the ribosome by one codon. A reverse-transcription stop is an indirect position marker rather than a direct visualization of tRNA chemistry. RNA secondary structure can also influence extension, so a message without bound ribosomes and a reference complex with a known position are important controls.

Consider a hypothetical result in which a control makes dipeptide in 90 percent of complexes and shows an expected downstream toeprint displacement in 85 percent. An experimental sample makes dipeptide in 88 percent but shows the displacement in only 8 percent. The peptide measurement argues that delivery and chemistry remain largely functional. The positional measurement suggests that progression to the post-translocation state is impaired. A factor-addition rescue would strengthen the assignment. However, the exact defective molecule cannot be identified from these two observations alone: altered tRNA–ribosome contacts, an impaired movement factor or an RNA feature that obstructs movement could generate a similar phenotype.

21. Testing fidelity without confusing incorrect charging with incorrect decoding

Accuracy experiments must distinguish the correct product from possible incorrect products. A simple way to frame the problem is to use an mRNA codon whose correct amino acid is known and provide two distinguishable candidate aminoacyl-tRNAs. One is cognate and correctly charged; the other is near-cognate and correctly charged with its own amino acid. If the resulting peptide is analyzed for which amino acid has been added, it becomes possible to estimate discrimination. The assay must account for the concentrations of the candidates because changing their abundance can alter product composition without changing the inherent selectivity of the ribosome.

For example, suppose equal amounts of two appropriately charged candidate tRNAs are supplied in a hypothetical prepared reaction. Out of 1,000 completed single-addition products, 990 contain the cognate amino acid and 10 contain the near-cognate amino acid. The observed wrong-product fraction is 1 percent. If a variant ribosome generates 950 correct and 50 incorrect products under matched conditions, its observed wrong-product fraction is 5 percent. The relative increase is fivefold, and the absolute increase is four percentage points. Both descriptions are correct, but they answer different quantitative questions. Replication and uncertainty estimates would be needed in a real study before drawing a conclusion about a reproducible effect.

An alternative experiment deliberately supplies a mischarged cognate tRNA. Its anticodon remains appropriate for the chosen codon but the attached amino acid is different from the expected one. If that amino acid is incorporated efficiently, the result is compatible with the division of labor between aminoacylation and decoding. The ribosome's sequence check can function as expected while the final peptide is incorrect. A comparison with a near-cognate correctly charged tRNA can reveal two different routes to the same possible sequence substitution. The result should not be interpreted as evidence that all mischarged substrates are delivered equally well; factor binding and other interactions can differ depending on the properties of the tRNA and its attached amino acid.

The distinction can be extended by measuring hydrolysis separately from peptide formation. In a controlled system containing delivery factor, the amount of GTP converted to GDP can be compared with the amount of amino acid incorporated. A situation with elevated hydrolysis and low product formation may indicate that many substrate encounters enter the GTP-consuming pathway but are rejected before completion. It could also arise from nonspecific GTPase activity or uncoupling of nucleotide hydrolysis from the productive conformational transitions. Controls that omit the candidate tRNA, the mRNA or the relevant factor help define the background signal.

A useful hypothetical dataset contains three conditions with identical amounts of starting ribosome. The cognate-only condition gives 100 delivery-associated GTP hydrolyses and 90 peptide products. A near-cognate-only condition gives 60 hydrolyses and 3 peptide products. A mixture with a ribosome variant gives 110 hydrolyses and 75 products, of which 15 are incorrect. The first two observations are consistent with substantial discrimination after some near-cognate encounters have consumed GTP. The variant data suggest altered selectivity or coupling, but do not identify a unique stage. Measuring which tRNAs were bound before hydrolysis and which were accommodated afterward would be informative. This example illustrates why the final product and the nucleotide consumption are complementary observables.

Kinetic studies of ribosomal decoding have shown that appropriate codon recognition can accelerate productive conformational reactions, not merely increase equilibrium binding. This finding explains why both residence time and forward rate matter. A strong experiment compares pre-steady-state changes when a prepared complex encounters cognate and near-cognate substrates, controlling for factor concentrations and charging. The data may show differences in the rates of GTPase activation or accommodation. The mechanistic inference is strongest when multiple independent observables fit a coherent sequence of states. It is weaker when the same endpoint could arise from several changes in reaction kinetics.

Evidence and further reading: Rodnina and Wintermeyer, ribosome fidelity.

22. From molecular mechanisms to biological phenotypes

A defect in elongation affects all proteins that depend on the relevant translation system, but the consequences need not be identical for every gene. A reduced pool of a particular charged tRNA is expected to slow decoding of the codons it normally recognizes. A defect in the peptidyl-transferase center can affect a more general reaction, although the severity may vary with the substrates. An altered translocation factor can slow repeated movements throughout many messages. These perturbations can all reduce cellular growth because protein production is central to metabolism and cellular maintenance, yet they leave different signatures in measurements of translation intermediates.

Some elongation-associated factors have specialized roles. In bacteria EF-P helps overcome inefficient peptide formation at certain sequences, particularly consecutive proline residues. The eukaryotic factor eIF5A, despite its historical name, contributes importantly to elongation, including synthesis through difficult peptide motifs. These factors illustrate that a single general reaction can have substrate-dependent kinetic difficulties. They should not replace EF-Tu/eEF1A or EF-G/eEF2 in a diagram of the ordinary cycle. Their functions supplement that cycle by helping particular peptide-transfer reactions proceed efficiently.

A nucleotide change in a coding region may alter a protein in several ways. A nonsynonymous change alters the amino acid specified by the relevant codon under the standard reading rules. A synonymous change preserves the encoded amino acid but can alter which tRNA species participates and how rapidly an available cognate substrate arrives. Such effects are context-dependent: a synonymous substitution does not automatically speed up or slow down translation. Local RNA structure, tRNA availability, initiation and other sequence features can also influence measured protein output. The essential lesson is that the genetic code specifies sequence, whereas the cellular translation machinery determines how efficiently that sequence is decoded.

The importance of elongation can also be seen in selective inhibition. A compound that interferes with entry of a charged tRNA can prevent a new bond from forming because the acceptor substrate is missing. A compound that interferes with the catalytic center can allow some tRNA binding yet prevent normal transfer. A compound that interferes with movement can allow a chemical product to form but impair progression to the following codon. Classical examples studied in the later inhibitor chapter include tetracycline acting on bacterial A-site tRNA accommodation and diphtheria toxin disrupting eukaryotic eEF2 function. These examples are useful as mechanistic probes, not as a substitute for the separate treatment of inhibitor spectra and exceptions.

Translation in mitochondria and plastids illustrates the need to identify the compartment before generalizing from a eukaryotic cell's cytosolic factors. These organelles retain evolutionarily bacterial-related translation systems with important differences among lineages. They need not have exactly the same ribosome composition or genetic-code assignments as a standard bacterium, and they are not simply copies of the cytosolic 80S system. An experimental inhibitor selective for a bacterial-like translation feature may therefore have different effects on cytosolic and organellar protein synthesis. The relevant inference must be tied to the system being tested.

As the growing N-terminal region emerges from the ribosome, it can begin to form local structures or interact with folding and targeting machinery. The sequence of events along a translating message can influence which parts of the chain are available at a given time. A pause at one region changes the interval during which the already synthesized portion is exposed. Such a pause is not invariably beneficial for folding; it can also create difficulties for a translating complex. Later chapters address protein folding and quality control in their own right. Here the important causal connection is that elongation produces a chain progressively rather than releasing a complete sequence instantaneously.

The completed elongation framework can now be summarized as a set of constraints that an unfamiliar experiment must satisfy. A correctly positioned peptidyl-tRNA supplies an activated donor. A new charged tRNA must be selected according to the current codon and must position its amino group in the catalytic center. Peptide transfer changes which adaptor carries the chain. Relative movement restores the donor to the P register and exposes a new message symbol. When a perturbation appears to disrupt protein synthesis, identifying which of these constraints fails is the first step toward a defensible mechanistic explanation.

The next curriculum node, 3.C.07, examines how a stop codon triggers peptide release and how ribosomes are recycled.

Evidence and further reading: EF-P and polyproline stalling; eIF5A and polyproline motifs.

New cycle = 50+5+20 = 75 ms. Rate 1/0.075 = 13.33/s, not half the initial 20/s.

C8. Numerical

For 120 successful additions, mean delivery GTP use is 1.25 per addition and translocation costs one per addition. Find total expected elongation GTP.

  1. 150
  2. 240
  3. 270
  4. 300
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 270

Delivery 150 plus movement 120 gives 270. Extra GTP use arises from unsuccessful GTP-consuming delivery attempts.

C9. Numerical

A sequence has 80 additions at 0.04 s each and 20 at 0.12 s each. Determine elongation duration.

  1. 4.0 s
  2. 5.6 s
  3. 8.0 s
  4. 12 s
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 5.6 s

80×0.04 + 20×0.12 = 3.2 + 2.4 = 5.6 s. Sum durations, rather than averaging local rates.

C10. Numerical

A translated segment from AUG through the first in-frame stop contains 606 nt. With no recoding, how many elongation cycles occur?

  1. 200
  2. 201
  3. 202
  4. 203
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 200

606/3 = 202 codons including stop. There are 201 residues including initiator; therefore 200 ordinary cycles.

C11. Numerical

Two equal-concentration ligands differ in standard binding free energy by 5.7 kJ/mol in favor of the correct ligand. At 298 K with RT=2.48 kJ/mol, estimate wrong/correct equilibrium occupancy.

  1. 0.01
  2. 0.10
  3. 0.50
  4. 10
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 0.10

exp(−5.7/2.48) ≈ exp(−2.30) ≈ 0.10. It is an equilibrium occupancy ratio, not the final ribosomal error probability.

C12. Analytical

A mutant gives 92% normal dipeptide but only 5% normal downstream toeprint movement. Which stage is most directly implicated?

  1. Charging
  2. Initiation
  3. Translocation
  4. Genetic code assignment
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Translocation

Covalent product can form while register change fails. The result supports a movement defect but does not alone identify a particular mutant protein.

C13. Analytical

A reaction produces no dipeptide and little bound incoming tRNA, although the initiator complex is normal. Adding EF-Tu restores both. What is best supported?

  1. Primary delivery defect
  2. Primary termination defect
  3. Defective stop recognition
  4. Proof that all tRNAs were mischarged
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Primary delivery defect

Rescue by delivery factor and low binding support a delivery problem; termination lies later. This does not establish the charging status of every tRNA.

C14. Analytical

A ribosome mutant produces more GTP hydrolysis but less correct product in a cognate/near-cognate mixture. Which additional measurement best separates rejection from uncoupled hydrolysis?

  1. Total mRNA amount only
  2. Number and identity of accommodated tRNAs, with background GTPase controls
  3. Genomic DNA length
  4. Final protein color alone
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Number and identity of accommodated tRNAs, with background GTPase controls

The state and identity of tRNAs after hydrolysis test passage through accommodation; factor-only and no-tRNA controls identify background turnover.

C15. Analytical

A cognate tRNA carrying an incorrect amino acid is accepted; a correctly charged near-cognate tRNA is usually rejected. What inference follows?

  1. Codon testing alone proves correct charging
  2. Charging fidelity and decoding fidelity are separable
  3. All near-cognates always pass
  4. Amino acids base-pair directly with mRNA
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Charging fidelity and decoding fidelity are separable

The two substrate types distinguish anticodon pairing from amino acid assignment. The ribosome is not a comprehensive synthetase identity checker.

C16. Analytical

A mutation reduces EF-Ts function. Active EF-Tu–GTP is initially abundant. Which time course is plausible?

  1. Normal initial rounds followed by slowdown as EF-Tu–GDP accumulates
  2. Immediate removal of all mRNA
  3. More peptide bonds than residues
  4. Termination at every first codon
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Normal initial rounds followed by slowdown as EF-Tu–GDP accumulates

Pre-existing active factor can support early cycles. Impaired exchange progressively reduces the delivery-competent pool; the other outcomes are not mechanistically implied.

C17. Analytical

The normal message AUG GCU UUU GGA UAA is translated. A movement inhibitor arrests the complex immediately after alanine incorporation. Which tRNA carries the chain before complete translocation?

  2. Alanine tRNA originally at A
  3. Phenylalanine tRNA at E
  4. Glycine tRNA at P

Transfer forms the Met–Ala chain on alanine tRNA. Phenylalanine is not yet added; the former initiator is deacylated.

C18. Analytical

A synonymous substitution causes slower synthesis at a specific codon; the transcript level is unchanged. Which experiment best evaluates a charged-tRNA supply explanation?

Charged cognate supply directly affects productive arrival. RNA abundance and global ribosome counts alone cannot distinguish this specific cause.

C19. Analytical

Two hypothetical products have equal full-length yield, but one shows reduced enzyme activity. What is required to support a decoding-error explanation?

  2. Direct sequence-sensitive product analysis with appropriate folding controls

  4. Only ATP concentration

Answer: B. Direct sequence-sensitive product analysis with appropriate folding controls

Activity can change through misfolding or modification despite correct sequence; sequence-sensitive evidence plus controls is needed.

C20. Analytical

A chemical protection experiment shows A-site acceptor end has moved to large-subunit P but the anticodon remains at small-subunit A. Which interpretation is best?

Revision and active recall

Essential definitions and mechanism

Aminoacyl-tRNA: an amino acid esterified to its adaptor. Accommodation: movement into the reaction-ready A site. Peptidyl transfer: movement of the old chain to the incoming amino group. Translocation: coordinated shift by one codon. Kinetic proofreading: additional substrate discrimination coupled to energy expenditure.

Mechanism map: Charged tRNA + delivery GTPase → initial selection → GTP hydrolysis → accommodation or rejection → peptide transfer → hybrid-state transitions → GTP-dependent translocation → next sense codon or stop.

Formula sheet

  • For n residues: n−1 bonds; n−1 additions; 2(n−1) ideal elongation GTP molecules.
  • Charging: n ATP molecules to AMP, 2n high-energy phosphate equivalents; combined ideal charging and elongation: 4n−2 equivalents.
  • Competitive first-order exit probability: k_f/(k_f+k_r); mean exit time: 1/(k_f+k_r).
  • Independent decoding error: P(at least one)=1−(1−p)^m; expected errors = mp.
  • Conditional two-check acceptance: p₁p₂; wrong fraction = wrong accepted weight / all accepted weights.
  • Mean elapsed elongation time: sum of individual durations; average rate = successful additions / elapsed time.

Examination traps

AUG is not an ordinary A-site addition at initiation.
The initiator residue is already in P before ordinary elongation.
Stop is not a residue.
Count n sense codons and one stop for a chain of n residues.
A site can carry peptide.
After transfer and before complete movement, the incoming adaptor has acquired the chain.
EF-Tu and EF-G are distinct.
Delivery is before chemistry; translocation is after chemistry.
EF-Ts is not a kinase.
It facilitates nucleotide exchange, not GTP synthesis.
Charging differs from accommodation.
The first attaches the amino acid; the second positions a charged tRNA.
GTP hydrolysis does not equal incorporation.
Late rejection can consume GTP without forming a bond.
Peptide transfer is not direct free-amino-acid dehydration.
A donor ester is attacked by the incoming amino group.
Correct anticodon does not guarantee correct amino acid.
Mischarging is a separate error route.
The slash in A/P has meaning.
The anticodon and acceptor ends contact different subunit registers.
Wobble is not automatically an error.
Permitted pairing can implement normal code degeneracy.
One ATP to AMP is not two ATP molecules.
It represents two high-energy phosphate equivalents under standard accounting.
Translocation does not always follow every peptide bond immediately.
A stalled pre-translocation state can retain the newly made peptide.
Error probability is not protein dysfunction probability.
Sequence fidelity, folding and biological activity are separate outcomes.

Active recall

Where is the first residue before elongation?

On initiator tRNA at P.

What is the ternary complex?

EF-Tu or eEF1A, GTP and charged elongator tRNA.

What happens after GTP-dependent selection?

Factor release and accommodation or rejection.

What attacks what during peptide transfer?

The incoming amino group attacks the donor ester carbonyl.

Which tRNA receives the chain?

The original A-site tRNA.

What does EF-G do?

Promotes coordinated translocation and factor turnover.

What is the ideal GTP cost per ordinary round?

Two, one for delivery and one for translocation.

How does a toeprint complement a peptide assay?

It reports a positional marker rather than covalent bond formation.

How can correct pairing give a wrong product?

A mischarged cognate tRNA can pass sequence recognition.

Why does kinetic proofreading cost extra energy?

Post-hydrolysis rejection can occur before peptide formation.
