1. The problem: distinguishing a molecular target from a cellular outcome

A cell can contain a complete genome, abundant messenger RNA and all twenty standard amino acids and nevertheless be unable to make a particular protein. The missing requirement is an operating translation system. Translation converts the order of nucleotide triplets in a messenger into the order of residues in a polypeptide. Its operation depends on several different kinds of molecular recognition: enzymes attach amino acids to transfer RNAs, a ribosome selects transfer RNAs by their anticodons, catalytic RNA positions substrates for peptide formation, and soluble factors coordinate movements of the ribosome and its substrates. Each of these operations is a potential point of interruption. Understanding an inhibitor therefore requires more than remembering that it reduces protein synthesis. The important questions are which molecular component it encounters, which reaction becomes inefficient, which intermediate accumulates and what sort of protein product remains.

Protein synthesis is necessary for both immediate cell function and future growth. A newly divided bacterium must make enzymes, transport proteins and structural components before producing another generation. When synthesis falls below demand, the cell may stop increasing its mass even though previously synthesized enzymes continue to operate. A mammalian cell likewise retains proteins produced before the interruption. Some persist for many hours whereas others disappear rapidly. Hence a decrease in synthesis should not automatically be described as the disappearance of all proteins. A translation inhibitor changes the input to the cellular protein pool; the subsequent abundance of each protein depends partly on its removal rate. This difference between a flux and a pool is central to interpreting experiments.

The distinction between direct and indirect effects is equally important. If an experimental compound abolishes ATP production, aminoacylation and translation eventually fail for lack of energy, but the primary target need not be a ribosome. If transcription is prevented, existing transcripts may continue to direct protein production until they decay. Conversely a direct translation inhibitor can reduce the synthesis of new protein while RNA production initially continues. Measurements of nucleic acids, newly synthesized protein, cellular energy and ribosome-associated intermediates together help discriminate these possibilities. A single endpoint such as reduced growth cannot establish the molecular mechanism.

The seven examples in this chapter illustrate different ways of interfering with the machine. Tetracycline restricts entry of aminoacyl-tRNA; streptomycin disturbs selection at the decoding centre; chloramphenicol inhibits peptide transfer in the bacterial large subunit; erythromycin interacts with the exit tunnel and causes elongation arrest; cycloheximide restricts movement of the eukaryotic cytosolic ribosome; puromycin acts as a deceptive substrate that terminates a growing chain; and diphtheria toxin modifies a soluble elongation factor. Several agents may stop the same overall process but act on entirely different molecular substrates. This is why a classification by the endpoint alone is inadequate.

2. A translation cycle reconstructed from its intermediates

Before studying an interrupted reaction, reconstruct the reaction that would occur in the absence of the inhibitor. The bacterial ribosome is conventionally described as a 70S particle composed of 30S and 50S subunits. The Svedberg values express sedimentation behaviour rather than simple molecular mass and are not additive. The cytosolic eukaryotic ribosome is an 80S particle assembled from 40S and 60S subunits. In each case the smaller subunit contains the decoding machinery while the larger subunit contains the catalytic centre that joins amino acids. The surfaces of the two subunits cooperate to hold transfer RNA molecules whose anticodon ends face the messenger and whose amino acid-bearing ends approach the catalytic centre.

The A, P and E sites describe functional positions of transfer RNA rather than three independent enzymes. During most of elongation the P site carries the transfer RNA linked to the growing chain. The A site receives the next aminoacyl-tRNA selected by the codon. The E site is associated with departure of the deacylated transfer RNA after it has served its purpose. During initiation the initiator transfer RNA is placed directly in the P site, a useful exception to the simplified rule that transfer RNAs first enter at A. An empty A site in an initiation complex is therefore normal; an empty A site in a ribosome whose elongation is blocked may have a different explanation.

Delivery of the next charged transfer RNA in bacteria involves EF-Tu bound to GTP. The corresponding eukaryotic cytosolic factor is eEF1A. A candidate anticodon samples the exposed codon. Productive recognition promotes changes in the ribosome and factor that allow GTP hydrolysis and subsequent accommodation of the amino acid end. Incorrect candidates are preferentially rejected. A successful codon pairing is necessary but does not establish that the attached amino acid is correct: that was the responsibility of the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase and its editing functions. The ribosome normally has no independent means of reconstructing the amino acid assignment from the genetic code.

At the peptidyl transferase centre, the amino group of the A-site substrate attacks the carbonyl group connecting the peptide to the P-site transfer RNA. The growing chain is transferred to the A-site substrate; the old P-site transfer RNA becomes deacylated. The resulting chain is one residue longer, but the messenger has not yet advanced to the next reading position. In the subsequent translocation step the tRNAs and messenger move relative to the ribosome by one codon. Bacterial EF-G and eukaryotic eEF2 are the corresponding GTP-dependent movement factors. The ribosome can now accept another charged transfer RNA. In real elongation complexes, intermediate hybrid tRNA positions occur, so a diagram showing only the three classical sites is a useful teaching simplification, not a complete catalogue of conformations.

When a stop codon reaches the decoding site, normal completion involves a release factor rather than an ordinary aminoacyl-tRNA. Hydrolysis liberates the chain from its transfer RNA. Subsequent recycling makes ribosomal components available for another round. A released full-length polypeptide may still need folding or processing before it becomes biologically active. This distinction is important when evaluating a reporter that measures enzyme activity: a defect in folding can reduce the signal without reducing the number of completed chains. By examining distinct intermediates one can identify an interruption more precisely than by merely detecting a decline in the final active product.

Two kinds of chemical energy contribute to elongation. Charging transfer RNA converts ATP to AMP and pyrophosphate; when the pyrophosphate is hydrolysed, the energetic cost is conventionally counted as two high-energy phosphate equivalents. The ester linkage in the charged substrate then supplies the chemical potential needed for peptide transfer. GTP hydrolysis during delivery and movement coordinates the timing and fidelity of the cycle. There is no separate ATP hydrolysis reaction for each peptide bond at the catalytic centre. A compound preventing translocation is therefore not necessarily a compound that prevents the chemistry of joining amino acids. That distinction will be especially useful when comparing chloramphenicol with cycloheximide.

3. Tetracycline: interfering with productive A-site occupancy

Tetracycline provides a clear example of how a small molecule can interfere with the physical placement of a much larger substrate. In ordinary bacterial elongation a charged transfer RNA must fit into the ribosomal A site. Initial codon recognition and full accommodation are related but separable operations: the anticodon first engages the messenger, then the acceptor arm approaches the large-subunit catalytic centre. Tetracycline associates with the bacterial small subunit near the A-site region. Its presence impedes stable, productive binding of the incoming aminoacyl-tRNA. Structural and functional evidence is consistent with overlap between the drug-binding region and the path needed by the transfer RNA [2].

The immediate defect is not a failure to generate charged transfer RNA in the surrounding cytoplasm. An aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase can still attach its substrate to a transfer RNA if the other necessary reactants are present. Nor does the drug have to hydrolyse the existing peptide or destroy the messenger. The existing peptidyl-tRNA may remain in the ribosome while addition of the next residue is restricted. Even abundant messenger and amino acids cannot remove the obstruction simply by supplying more genetic information.

Imagine a simplified translation system in which a ribosome has just moved so that a peptidyl-tRNA occupies P and a new sense codon occupies A. If productive aminoacyl-tRNA binding is prevented, the next amide bond cannot be formed because its acceptor amino group is not correctly positioned. The peptidyl transferase centre can be chemically intact even when the measured rate of peptide formation approaches zero. The distinction between a catalytic defect and a missing substrate is easy to overlook. A reduced product signal identifies a failed process, not necessarily the failed operation inside that process.

In a comparison experiment, normal charging with reduced ribosomal association of aminoacyl-tRNA supports an entry defect. It is also necessary to consider the state of the ribosome before interpreting the association measurement: a translocation inhibitor could leave a previously used substrate occupying the A-site region and thereby prevent a new transfer RNA from entering secondarily. The most useful evidence combines substrate association with the state of the existing peptidyl-tRNA and tests of earlier and later reactions. For examinations, the standard association remains tetracyclines, bacterial 30S subunit and interference with aminoacyl-tRNA entry. The deeper reasoning is that binding-site geometry links the direct molecular interaction to loss of elongation.

This also explains why inhibition is selective rather than a property of all RNA-containing structures. A bacterial 30S subunit has a particular architecture and particular interactions with the inhibitor. A cytosolic eukaryotic 40S subunit is not simply a larger copy with identical binding pockets. Nevertheless selectivity is not a universal guarantee of harmlessness to all organelles or organisms, because organellar translation systems retain evolutionary relationships with bacterial translation and vary in their sensitivity.

4. Streptomycin: a change in the quality of decoding

Whereas tetracycline makes productive entry of the substrate difficult, streptomycin illustrates a different problem: the ribosome's decision about whether a candidate substrate should be accepted. Accurate decoding depends on recognizing the geometry of a codon–anticodon helix and coordinating this recognition with the progression of delivery and accommodation. Base pairing is a necessary part of the decision, but correct and near-correct pairs must be discriminated by the complete molecular environment. Streptomycin, an aminoglycoside, interacts with components of the bacterial 30S decoding region, including 16S rRNA and the neighbourhood of ribosomal protein S12. It perturbs the normal balance between acceptance and rejection [2,3].

When a near-cognate transfer RNA is accepted at a codon, the amino acid it carries may be inserted even though it does not correspond to the standard assignment. This is a translational error. The nucleotide sequence of the DNA and messenger has not necessarily changed. A second copy of the same messenger may be decoded correctly, and a cell that no longer experiences the perturbation need not transmit the altered protein sequence to its descendants. A genetic mutation and a transient translation error can both produce an incorrect residue, but they differ in their information source and their persistence.

Incorrect decoding can produce proteins that retain partial activity, lose their normal function or fail to fold. The biological outcome depends on which residues are changed and how essential the affected proteins are. It would be misleading to assume that every incorrect amino acid produces a lethal protein. Equally misleading is the assumption that an unchanged number of peptide bonds means translation is functioning normally. Incorporation of amino acids into polypeptides can occur while the fraction of correctly synthesized products declines.

The classic examination contrast is that tetracycline interferes mainly with productive transfer RNA entry whereas streptomycin causes miscoding and perturbs bacterial translation. Streptomycin can also interfere with other ribosomal events, including initiation and elongation, depending on the conditions. Its primary textbook association with decoding does not imply an exclusive effect on error frequency with an otherwise unchanged rate of production. Both the quantity and the quality of synthesized protein may be affected. Structural studies comparing small-subunit complexes with matching and near-matching anticodon substrates help locate the relevant perturbation. Such studies complement, rather than replace, functional measurements of miscoding.

Consider a reporter that produces a normal enzyme when translated with a particular amino acid at an essential position. A loss of enzyme activity after exposure to streptomycin could be consistent with decoding errors, but cannot alone establish that mechanism because decreased reporter synthesis would also lower activity. Measuring reporter abundance alongside enzyme activity distinguishes total production from function per molecule. A fall in activity per unit reporter is stronger evidence for an altered quality of the product; direct assessment of the inserted residue is needed to attribute the change specifically to miscoding. The principle extends to many experiments in molecular biology: the assay must measure the feature that the proposed mechanism actually changes.

5. Chloramphenicol: the chemistry of chain extension

Chloramphenicol directs attention to the bacterial large subunit. Its conventional assignment is inhibition of peptidyl transferase activity associated with the 50S subunit. The central chemical step of elongation requires correct arrangement of the amino group of the incoming aminoacyl-tRNA and the ester-linked chain on the donor transfer RNA. The catalytic centre is formed largely by 23S ribosomal RNA. This is one reason the ribosome is considered a ribozyme: RNA is at the centre of the catalytically active structure even though ribosomal proteins contribute importantly to the assembly and operation of the whole particle.

If both substrates enter the appropriate ribosome sites but transfer of the chain is inhibited, the growing polypeptide remains attached to the donor for longer than usual. The amino acid carried by the incoming substrate cannot be incorporated efficiently at the affected step. This is mechanistically different from preventing productive entry into the A site, even though either obstruction prevents completion of the same new peptide bond. The classical drug association therefore provides a useful experimental contrast between substrate access and catalysis. In interpreting actual experiments, the timing and detailed binding state can be more complex than the elementary scheme.

A useful modern qualification is that chloramphenicol does not inhibit every possible bond with identical efficiency. The sequence of the emerging peptide and the identity of the next residue influence susceptibility. Experiments reported in 2016 found preferential arrest at particular sequence contexts rather than uniform arrest at every position [4]. This result refines the standard textbook model without invalidating its classification of chloramphenicol as a peptidyl transferase centre inhibitor. The location of a drug-binding site remains useful information, but its functional impact depends on the state of the molecular machine carrying the drug.

This distinction matters in questions about different proteins. Suppose two bacterial messages have identical lengths and comparable initiation frequencies, yet one reporter is more strongly inhibited than the other. It is not necessary to conclude that they use different ribosomes or that one reporter was transcribed from a weaker promoter. A difference in the amino acid sequences can change the likelihood of encountering an arrest-prone elongation state. Before accepting this explanation, however, compare the messages' abundances, their translation initiation behaviour and the stability of their products. A sequence-dependent difference in translation inhibition is plausible when these factors are controlled.

Some organellar translation systems are also susceptible to chloramphenicol. The endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria and plastids helps explain why an agent assigned to bacterial translation can affect protein synthesis in a eukaryotic cell without primarily blocking the cytosolic 80S ribosomes. This evolutionary inference is more precise than saying that chloramphenicol affects bacteria but never eukaryotes. A eukaryotic cell contains multiple translation compartments, and its total response depends on their contributions to the measured protein pool.

6. Erythromycin: the exit tunnel and context-dependent elongation arrest

The polypeptide produced at the peptidyl transferase centre must leave the interior of the large subunit through an exit passage. This nascent peptide exit tunnel is not just an empty pipe: it is part of the architecture encountered by the growing chain. Its geometry and chemical surfaces constrain the movement of a peptide while the catalytic centre continues to add residues. Erythromycin belongs to the macrolide class and binds in the bacterial large subunit within the exit tunnel region near the catalytic centre [5].

The traditional explanation states that macrolides obstruct passage of the nascent peptide and thereby inhibit elongation. This is a useful first description of their location and effect, but it should not be interpreted as a universal mechanical plug that stops every chain after exactly the same number of residues. Some nascent chains continue to elongate in the presence of a macrolide, whereas others encounter particular sequence-dependent arrests. The binding of an antibiotic in the tunnel can influence peptide transfer even though the antibiotic is not positioned in the same way as a direct peptidyl transferase centre inhibitor. The distinction between physical binding site and interrupted reaction is essential here.

Ribosome profiling studies provided evidence for this refinement. The underlying concept is to examine the positions occupied by ribosomes along different messages. If many ribosomes encounter difficulty at a particular sequence, their occupancy at that position may increase relative to a control, and the production of downstream completed chains may decline. Studies of erythromycin and other macrolides found that different messages show different arrest patterns [5,6]. A position with elevated ribosome occupancy is consistent with a longer residence time, though altered initiation or other effects may complicate interpretation. The measurement is a snapshot of ribosome distribution rather than a direct movie of every individual translation event.

To reason through an examination example, consider two messages encoding proteins of the same length. In an untreated system each yields approximately the same number of complete molecules. A macrolide produces a strong arrest signal at one position in the first message but not at the equivalent position in the second. If the local peptide sequence differs, context-specific inhibition is a reasonable interpretation. A direct conclusion that every macrolide blocks all protein synthesis equally would contradict the observation. The more accurate model connects the drug, its position in the ribosome and the sequence of the nascent chain.

The two major bacterial large-subunit examples should therefore not be merged into a single memorized category. Chloramphenicol is conventionally associated with the peptidyl transferase centre, whereas erythromycin is conventionally associated with the exit tunnel and elongation. Both can influence the formation of particular peptide bonds. The site of initial binding and the functional consequence of that binding are two different descriptors. An examination asking for the principal binding region expects the distinction; an examination asking whether a tunnel-bound compound can affect peptide transfer permits the more mechanistic answer.

7. Cycloheximide: interfering with eukaryotic ribosome movement

The eukaryotic cytosolic ribosome provides a contrast to the bacterial targets. Cycloheximide is a standard experimental inhibitor of eukaryotic cytosolic translation elongation. Its binding region is associated with the E-site pocket of the large subunit, and structural and biochemical evidence links it to impaired translocation [7]. Because movement of the tRNAs and messenger is coordinated with conformational changes throughout the ribosome, interference at an exit-site region can prevent the next decoding cycle without directly competing with the incoming amino acid at the catalytic centre.

The immediate interpretation is most easily understood by considering a completed peptide transfer step. The growing chain has been transferred to the A-site substrate; before the next bond can form, the chain-bearing substrate must advance toward the P-site position and the messenger must present the next codon. If translocation is restricted, the ribosome cannot repeatedly carry out the sequence of reactions needed for elongation. A failed movement step can therefore cause secondary decreases in aminoacyl-tRNA binding or peptide bond formation. The direct molecular target should not be inferred solely from those secondary measurements.

Cycloheximide is often used conceptually to distinguish translation-dependent processes from changes that can proceed using proteins already present. Suppose a short-lived regulatory protein normally disappears over time because it is continuously degraded and replaced. When new synthesis is inhibited, existing molecules are still removed. The resulting time course can help estimate turnover under suitable assumptions. This is not evidence that cycloheximide itself degrades the protein. Inhibition of synthesis exposes the effect of removal that was already occurring.

Another application of the distinction between movement and initiation concerns polysomes. When a translation elongation inhibitor arrests ribosomes on a messenger, many of the ribosomes may remain associated for an interval. When initiation alone is blocked and previously initiated ribosomes continue elongating, the existing ribosomes can complete their work and leave the message. This gradual depletion of ribosomes is described as run-off. A polysome profile interpreted with time information can distinguish these idealized patterns. Neither pattern is guaranteed indefinitely in intact cells, because stress responses and secondary changes can alter both initiation and elongation.

Cycloheximide and diphtheria toxin both impair movement during eukaryotic elongation, but their primary targets differ. Cycloheximide binds a ribosomal region; diphtheria toxin modifies a soluble factor involved in movement. The distinction allows a student to predict which component would carry the defect in a simplified reconstitution experiment. It also shows why memorizing only the word translocation cannot answer every question.

8. Puromycin: premature release by molecular mimicry

The concept of an inhibitor as a physical obstruction is incomplete. Puromycin demonstrates that a substrate-like molecule can be accepted by the very catalytic machinery it interrupts. Its structure resembles the aminoacylated terminal portion of a transfer RNA. It lacks the complete transfer RNA body and anticodon required for normal codon-directed selection, but possesses a chemical group that can accept a nascent peptide at the large-subunit catalytic centre [1,8].

When the growing chain is transferred from a donor transfer RNA to puromycin, the product is a peptide linked to puromycin rather than a normal peptidyl-tRNA. The linkage cannot participate in the usual next donor reaction. The peptide is consequently released prematurely. The chain need not reach a stop codon; a normal release factor is not required for the puromycin reaction. A prematurely released molecule differs fundamentally from a stalled chain that remains attached to a ribosome. It also differs from an ordinary termination product because the full coding region may not have been translated and the terminal chemical group is not the same as in a normal released peptide.

The peptide transferase reaction is necessary for puromycin's characteristic action. If a ribosome is in a state that prevents access of puromycin to the catalytic site, or if peptide transfer is already inhibited, incorporation may be reduced. Thus an absence of puromycin-labelled chains can have several explanations besides an absence of ribosomes. A ribosome could be present but in an incompatible state. Puromycin illustrates why an experimental probe is informative only if the reaction that generates its signal is understood.

The conserved catalytic chemistry of bacterial and eukaryotic ribosomes explains why puromycin can interfere with translation in both types of system. This is not a claim that every cell takes up the agent equally or that organelles necessarily respond identically. Experimental accessibility and susceptibility must be distinguished from the ability of a purified ribosome to catalyse the reaction.

There is a further consequence of premature release. Suppose a cell normally produces a protein containing a functional domain close to the carboxyl terminus. A chain released before that region has been translated will lack the complete domain. Measuring the number of released polypeptides could therefore overstate the production of useful protein. A labelled short chain demonstrates that some peptide synthesis occurred; it does not demonstrate normal completion. Puromycin is useful as a conceptual probe of nascent chains precisely because its incorporation and the subsequent fate of the labelled product are linked to the chemistry of translation.

9. Diphtheria toxin: modification of a translation factor

Not all translation inhibitors act by occupying ribosomal sites. Diphtheria toxin illustrates interference with the eukaryotic elongation factor eEF2. This protein is required for the translocation step that resets the translating ribosome for the next codon. It contains a modified histidine residue called diphthamide. The toxin catalyses transfer of an ADP-ribose group from NAD+ to this residue, releasing nicotinamide. The modification renders eEF2 unable to support normal translocation [9,10]. NAD+ is the donor of the transferred chemical group, not a substitute for GTP in the ordinary elongation cycle.

The distinction between catalytic modification of a factor and reversible occupancy of a site is conceptually important. Removing free inhibitor from a ribosome-binding system can allow recovery if the inhibitor dissociates and the machine remains intact. Removing a modifying agent does not automatically restore a factor whose covalent state has already changed. Recovery in a cell depends on restoration of a functional factor pool and other consequences of injury. This difference in persistence follows from the molecular mechanism, rather than merely from how much inhibition was initially observed.

At the examination level, the essential association is diphtheria toxin, ADP-ribosylation of eEF2, and inhibition of eukaryotic translocation. Do not replace eEF2 with bacterial EF-G simply because their roles are analogous. The ordinary bacterial translocation factor lacks the characteristic diphthamide target. The biological source of the inhibitory molecule and the cellular system on which it acts are distinct matters: a substance made by bacteria can act on eukaryotic protein synthesis.

A useful comparison is with cycloheximide. Both reduce the ability of the ribosome to progress through elongation, yet one primarily acts on the ribosome and the other on a protein factor. This explains why the same endpoint, stalled elongation, does not identify a unique target. Experiments that distinguish defects retained by the ribosomal fraction from those retained by the soluble factor fraction provide stronger mechanistic evidence than a global measure of protein production. Such comparisons are central to the logic of classical biochemical fractionation and are applicable well beyond translation.

10. Quantitative reasoning: output, error frequency and protein turnover

A short calculation can show why the amount of full-length product is a sensitive measure of successful elongation. Consider an idealized ribosome that must perform n independent elongation transitions, each with probability p of successful completion before an irreversible arrest. Assume all transitions have identical probabilities, initiation has already succeeded, and termination is certain if the last transition succeeds. The probability of completing the chain is then p raised to the power n. This follows from multiplication of independent probabilities, not from a universal physical law of antibiotic action. For a chain requiring 100 transitions with p equal to 0.99, the probability is approximately 0.366. For 200 transitions it is approximately 0.134. A modest failure probability per transition therefore has a much larger cumulative effect on long products. Real sequence-dependent inhibition violates the equal-probability assumption, and reversible delays are not equivalent to irreversible failure. The calculation should be used only when the stated assumptions apply.

A similar argument can quantify decoding fidelity without conflating it with elongation completion. If the independent probability of an erroneous residue is e, the probability of a chain of L residues containing no errors is approximately (1 − e) to the power L. This quantity is not necessarily the fraction of biologically functional proteins. Some errors are tolerated; others are devastating. Initiator residues may have different error rates from internal residues, and codon contexts vary. Nonetheless the model shows why a system that produces many full-length chains can show a marked decline in perfectly decoded chains after a small increase in the per-residue error frequency.

Protein turnover introduces a different mathematical relationship. Define P(t) as the amount of a protein at time t, s as its synthesis rate in amount per unit time and k as its first-order degradation constant in inverse time. In the simplest model, the rate of change of P is s − kP. Under steady synthesis the steady-state amount is s/k. If synthesis is suddenly abolished and the degradation constant remains unchanged, the differential equation becomes dP/dt = −kP. Separating variables gives dP/P = −k dt. Integrating from the initial amount P0 gives ln(P/P0) = −kt, or P(t) = P0 exp(−kt). Setting P equal to half of P0 yields t_half = ln(2)/k. If protein abundance declines from 120 units to 30 units in 6 hours, two half-lives have elapsed and the estimated half-life is 3 hours. The estimated k is 0.231 per hour.

The interpretation requires a verified synthesis block and a degradation process that is reasonably first order over the measured interval. In dividing cells, dilution can contribute to the decline in protein concentration. A treatment that simultaneously alters degradation changes the observed constant. A protein may also have multiple populations with distinct stabilities. A straight line in a plot of log abundance against time is consistent with first-order loss; it does not independently establish the biological route of degradation. This is why the experimental use of a translation inhibitor must be accompanied by a careful definition of what is measured.

These equations describe different quantities. The success-probability model addresses the fraction of initiated chains that reach completion. The fidelity model addresses the fraction without errors under an independent-error assumption. The turnover equation addresses the existing protein pool as a function of time. A drug may influence all three indirectly, but their variables must not be interchanged. The purpose of quantitative reasoning is to sharpen the biological interpretation rather than to assign a single numerical inhibition parameter to a complex cell.

11. Experimental interpretation and compartment-specific selectivity

A mechanistic claim is strongest when different measurements agree. A decline in newly incorporated amino acid label suggests less new protein synthesis, but does not identify whether the interruption occurred during entry, transfer, movement or completion. RNA abundance measurements can help exclude a rapid loss of the message. Measurements of charged transfer RNA can separate a charging defect from some ribosomal defects. The distribution of nascent chains between ribosomal and soluble fractions distinguishes some forms of stalling from premature release. The identity and activity of completed proteins help reveal miscoding. None of these observations in isolation proves a particular drug's binding site.

Consider the following hypothetical comparison. System A shows normal charging, reduced productive A-site occupancy and low peptide transfer. System B shows charged substrate association but reduced chain extension. System C makes appreciable peptide material, but the proportion of correctly decoded product declines. System D releases short chains before normal stop codons. Under appropriately controlled conditions, these patterns are respectively consistent with a tetracycline-like entry defect, chloramphenicol-like interference with peptide transfer, streptomycin-like decoding disruption, and puromycin-like premature release. The patterns are idealized learning examples, not measured drug-response datasets. They illustrate the reasoning chain from state to mechanism and do not imply that other mechanisms cannot generate overlapping signatures.

A separate comparison concerns eukaryotic cytosol and organelles. The endosymbiotic history of mitochondria and chloroplasts explains their bacterial ancestry, but their contemporary ribosomes are not identical to bacterial 70S particles. Mammalian mitochondrial ribosomes, for example, differ substantially in composition and sedimentation from bacterial ribosomes. It is therefore inappropriate to use 70S as a universal label for every mitochondrial ribosome. The defensible inference is that some aspects of organellar translation show bacterial-like susceptibility, not that every bacterial drug affects every organelle.

Imagine that a eukaryotic cell produces most of its measured proteins on cytosolic ribosomes, while a small fraction of its proteins are made inside mitochondria. An inhibitor that selectively lowers mitochondrial translation may have a relatively modest immediate effect on whole-cell amino acid incorporation. Nevertheless the long-term effect could be important because the affected products include components needed for organellar function. Conversely a strong decline in whole-cell incorporation does not by itself reveal the precise behaviour of mitochondrial translation. Compartment-specific measurements are necessary if the question concerns the organelle.

The immediate change in protein synthesis can also have delayed consequences. An inhibitor that interrupts synthesis of a short-lived transcription factor might eventually alter RNA abundance; a drug that produces misfolded proteins might activate protein quality control. These changes are downstream responses, not evidence that the original molecular target was a transcription factor or a degradation enzyme. Time-course reasoning helps separate primary and secondary effects. Early measurements can reveal the first altered process, while later measurements describe how the cell responds.

The next chapter follows the growing or released chain into folding and maturation. Translation inhibitors make the boundary between synthesis and maturation particularly clear: an intact message, correct decoding and completion of the chain are all important, but a functional protein also requires an appropriate structure. A complete account of protein biology must distinguish the quantity of newly synthesized chains, their sequence accuracy, their completion, their folding and their persistence.

12. How classical inhibitor experiments separate the steps of translation

The modern description of translation is sufficiently familiar that it can be difficult to appreciate how investigators established its separate stages. Observing protein accumulation in a growing cell tells very little about the order of events on the ribosome. A useful way to investigate that order is to simplify the system and examine which reactions remain possible when one component or one activity is selectively interrupted. Translation inhibitors became valuable because they allowed some portions of the cycle to proceed while others became inefficient. The logic is related to the use of enzyme inhibitors in metabolic pathways, but a ribosome repeatedly moves through a set of states rather than converting a series of freely diffusible intermediates. An arrest therefore changes the distribution of states occupied by the molecular machine.

A cell-free translation system illustrates this distinction. Such a system can support the conversion of a messenger into protein using cellular components outside the intact cell. The scientific advantage is that loss of product is less readily attributed to changes in cell division, membrane transport or transcriptional regulation. If the messenger has already been supplied, a decrease in translation cannot simply be explained by failure to transcribe the corresponding gene. The preparation still contains interacting molecular components, so additional controls are needed to identify precisely which component is affected. The fundamental reasoning is to hold the remaining inputs constant while examining the consequences of changing a particular component or activity.

There is a difference between supplying an inhibitor before translation begins and examining a system in which translating ribosomes are already present. In the first case a lack of product could arise because initiation never succeeded or because newly initiated ribosomes stalled almost immediately. In the second case the initial population has already passed initiation, so changes in the extension or release of their chains are informative about elongation and termination. This difference in the starting state is particularly important when comparing inhibitors with different stage specificity. A result should always be interpreted in relation to which reactions the experimental population had completed before the perturbation.

In principle, the course of protein synthesis can be reconstructed by following labelled amino acid incorporation into peptide material. Such an experiment exploits the chemical difference between a free amino acid and an amino acid linked into a macromolecule. If label is detected in a peptide-containing fraction, it demonstrates incorporation under the assumptions of the assay. However, the interpretation depends on what the fraction includes. A charged transfer RNA also contains an amino acid covalently attached to a macromolecule. An assay that fails to distinguish labelled aminoacyl-tRNA from labelled peptide can overestimate peptide formation. A comparison between free substrate, charged tRNA and peptide product is therefore more informative than a single total incorporation measurement.

Suppose an investigator detects normal formation of aminoacyl-tRNA in a system treated with an unknown agent, but little labelled peptide accumulates. This is sufficient to argue against a complete inhibition of the relevant charging reaction, but it is not sufficient to locate the defect within elongation. The next question is whether the substrate reaches the ribosome productively. If not, an entry defect is plausible. If it reaches the correct state but its amino acid is not incorporated, an interruption near the chemical transfer step becomes plausible. If a bond forms but the system fails to progress to subsequent codons, a movement defect becomes plausible. Each observation narrows the range of explanations rather than proving a drug identity immediately.

The historical importance of puromycin follows from a related principle. The reaction of the growing peptide with a substrate analogue provides information about the chemistry normally performed by the large subunit. Its incorporation at the end of a growing peptide shows that the ribosomal catalytic environment can recognize and use a suitable aminoacyl-like acceptor without requiring a complete anticodon-bearing transfer RNA for that chemical reaction [1,8]. It is important not to generalize this observation into a claim that ordinary amino acid incorporation is independent of codon reading. Normal translation couples substrate selection to messenger decoding. The analogue reveals the separability of the transfer reaction from the full machinery of ordinary selection.

A different kind of evidence comes from determination of molecular structures. When a structure contains a bound inhibitor, it can identify the region in which the inhibitor resides. For example, a structure of a bacterial small subunit containing streptomycin can reveal changes in the environment used to discriminate between codon–anticodon pairs [3]. A structure of a large subunit containing a tunnel-binding antibiotic can establish that the antibiotic occupies the path of the growing chain. Yet spatial information alone does not establish the rate at which a reaction occurs. The strongest mechanistic conclusions combine a structural model with functional evidence showing how the binding interaction changes translation. Students should recognize the complementary nature of these approaches: one provides localization, the other reveals the dynamic consequences.

An untreated comparison is indispensable for all these arguments. Differences between experimental preparations may arise from variation in ribosome concentration, messenger abundance, the functional state of the factors or the availability of energy. A meaningful comparison holds the relevant variables constant or measures them explicitly. A second type of control is a reaction expected to remain possible in the presence of the inhibitor. For instance, detecting an unaffected charging reaction establishes that the absence of peptide cannot be attributed simply to a failure of the measured synthetase. Such controls help make the interpretation causal rather than descriptive.

13. Decoding versus accommodation: reasoning with an intact transfer RNA

A transfer RNA performs two distinct roles during translation. Its anticodon presents an information-bearing surface to the small subunit, while its acceptor end presents a chemical substrate to the large subunit. A large change in the position of the acceptor end may be necessary between the earliest stage of decoding and the state competent for peptide transfer. This architecture provides a useful way to think about the difference between tetracycline and streptomycin. A perturbation that reduces stable placement of a transfer RNA is not identical to one that alters the discrimination between correct and incorrect anticodons, even when both act in the vicinity of the bacterial small subunit.

The logic is especially apparent if one considers a correctly charged but near-cognate transfer RNA. Its attached amino acid is correct for its own anticodon, but the anticodon does not correctly match the codon currently exposed by the messenger. In an unperturbed ribosome this substrate is less likely to proceed to peptide transfer than a matching substrate. A decoding perturbation can change the relative probabilities of the two outcomes. A restriction on accommodation, by contrast, can reduce the successful passage of correctly matching substrates through the next stage. Both effects decrease the efficiency of producing an appropriate chain, but they operate on different features of substrate recognition.

One source of confusion is the term binding. A transfer RNA can make transient contact with a ribosome without completing the entire entry process. If an experiment measures the first contact, it may detect candidates that subsequently dissociate. If it measures stable association, it emphasizes substrates that progress to a later state. If it measures peptide formation, it measures the success of a still later operation. Therefore the claim that an inhibitor blocks binding should be interpreted at the resolution of the experiment and not automatically taken to mean that no physical encounter between the transfer RNA and the ribosome ever occurs. The standard tetracycline association is best expressed as interference with productive A-site binding or accommodation.

A related distinction concerns proofreading. Before an amino acid is presented to a ribosome, a synthetase has already selected the transfer RNA and attached the amino acid. A mistake at this stage can create a substrate with an anticodon matching the messenger but an inappropriate attached amino acid. The ribosome may accept it because the relevant information in the anticodon is correct. By contrast a ribosomal decoding error can accept a normally charged transfer RNA with the wrong anticodon for the exposed codon. Both routes produce an inappropriate amino acid in the resulting protein. They can be distinguished experimentally by examining the amino acid attached to the transfer RNA before decoding and examining the codon–anticodon interaction during translation.

This comparison explains why a question asking whether streptomycin directly causes an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase to charge the wrong amino acid should be answered in the negative as its standard primary mechanism. A drug that perturbs bacterial decoding can generate incorrect protein sequences without altering the charging specificity of the enzyme. It is possible for stressed cells to exhibit secondary changes in other pathways, but secondary effects do not redefine the established principal target. Conversely a synthetase-specific inhibitor may strongly reduce protein synthesis while leaving the decoding centre structurally unaltered.

The ratio of correct to incorrect incorporation depends not only on the inherent discrimination of the ribosome but also on the relative availability of candidate substrates. Imagine a simplified competition in which a matching transfer RNA is far more abundant than a particular near-cognate species. Even if discrimination becomes less stringent, the total outcome can still favour the matching species. If the near-cognate species is abundant, altered discrimination may have a more visible effect. This is one reason a single percentage of miscoding cannot be assigned to an inhibitor independently of the experimental context. The relevant codon, available transfer RNAs and their interaction with the ribosome all affect the measured outcome.

There is a parallel in genetics examination questions involving an altered ribosomal protein. A change in a ribosomal component may affect sensitivity to a drug and may also affect baseline translation behaviour. To attribute a difference specifically to altered drug sensitivity, compare untreated and treated conditions for both the reference and variant system. A variant showing lower activity under all conditions is not automatically more susceptible; its baseline activity must be taken into account. The same reasoning applies to a variant showing unusually high reporter activity, because increased reporter abundance is different from improved accuracy of each translated chain. This is a general principle of experimental normalization.

14. Peptide transfer and movement are sequential operations, not interchangeable descriptions

A major learning objective of this chapter is to distinguish the formation of a new peptide bond from movement of the ribosome. Both belong to elongation, and failure of either eventually prevents further extension. Nevertheless their substrates, chemical events and immediate intermediates differ. Peptide transfer converts two appropriately positioned transfer RNA substrates into a longer peptidyl-tRNA and a deacylated transfer RNA. Translocation moves the messenger and these products relative to the ribosome so that another decoding event can occur. The first operation changes a covalent bond; the second rearranges a molecular complex without adding an amino acid.

Consider a model growing chain with three residues on a donor transfer RNA and an incoming transfer RNA carrying the fourth residue. After successful peptide transfer, a four-residue chain is attached to the incoming transfer RNA. If movement has not yet occurred, the coding position associated with that incoming transfer RNA remains the currently decoded position. The next sense codon has not yet become available in the proper way for a new elongation cycle. Therefore finding a four-residue product does not demonstrate that the next movement step succeeded. Conversely finding only the original three-residue chain after the incoming transfer RNA has reached a competent state points toward failure before or during transfer.

At the level of the whole protein, an inhibitor that prevents movement can reduce the incorporation of all subsequent residues. If the measurement integrates many cycles, this can make a movement inhibitor look indistinguishable from an inhibitor of peptide transfer. A time-resolved observation that separates the first accessible transfer event from subsequent cycles is more informative. The comparison is useful even when the real system is more complicated than the elementary model because it identifies the earliest reaction whose success differs between the hypotheses.

The energy requirements also distinguish the operations. The charged transfer RNA provides an activated linkage from which the chain can be transferred. The movement factor is a GTPase whose normal cycle is coupled to translocation. A general failure of GTP-dependent progression can therefore leave a previously formed peptide bond intact. It is a mistake to treat all inhibition of elongation as direct inhibition of peptide bond chemistry. Chloramphenicol and cycloheximide illustrate the difference through their standard associations with transfer and translocation, respectively, even though the drugs act in different translation systems.

The ribosomal A, P and E labels are most useful when the stage of the cycle is specified. If a question states that peptide transfer has just occurred, the chain-bearing transfer RNA is associated with the A position in the classical description. If movement has occurred, the same chain-bearing substrate occupies the P position. Without information about timing, a statement that a ribosome contains peptidyl-tRNA does not identify its exact position. Real ribosomes pass through hybrid states, which means that the anticodon end and acceptor end may be associated with different classical positions during movement. At the NET level it is usually sufficient to recognize that the labels in a simple drawing refer to a selected stage rather than to every intermediate.

A common misconception is that a drug acting on the large subunit must inhibit peptide transfer. The large subunit also contributes to the path through which the nascent chain exits and contains regions contacted by moving transfer RNAs. Its architecture participates in translocation as well as catalysis. The site of cycloheximide binding provides an example of how a region associated with transfer RNA exit can influence movement. Erythromycin provides an example of how the tunnel can influence elongation. It is thus not adequate to infer an exact functional mechanism merely from the subunit number.

This relationship between function and structure can be generalized. A binding interaction at one location can affect another operation because the molecular machine acts as an integrated structure. Such an effect is often called allosteric when a perturbation of one region changes the properties of another region. The term should not be used as a substitute for evidence of the relevant coupling. In the case of tunnel-binding macrolides, functional and structural work supports a connection between the sequence of the nascent peptide and events at the catalytic region [5,6]. The appropriate lesson is that physical proximity and functional interaction are related but not equivalent concepts.

15. Ribosome occupancy is not synonymous with protein synthesis rate

One of the most useful Part C principles is that the number of ribosomes bound to a message cannot be interpreted as a direct count of proteins completed per unit time. If all ribosomes move quickly, a message can support substantial output while holding relatively few ribosomes at any instant. If they move slowly, the message may carry many ribosomes but complete fewer products. The same distinction occurs elsewhere in biology, such as when a large amount of a metabolic intermediate accumulates because its downstream consumption is restricted. An abundant intermediate is not necessarily evidence of high pathway flux.

To formalize this reasoning, suppose a message receives translating ribosomes at an average initiation rate J measured in ribosomes per second. Assume a steady state, that every initiated ribosome completes the message, that the mean translation time is T seconds and that ribosomes do not interfere with one another. The average number of translating ribosomes on a message is then approximately N = JT. Each entering ribosome spends an average time T on the message, so a higher input rate or longer residence time increases occupancy. In the absence of losses, the average completion rate is J, not N. This relation has dimensions of a count because a rate in inverse seconds is multiplied by seconds.

For example, suppose a protein-coding region normally takes 40 seconds to translate and initiation occurs once every 10 seconds on average. The predicted average occupancy is four translating ribosomes. If the residence time increases to 80 seconds without any change in initiation or completion, the predicted occupancy doubles to eight. The model does not predict a doubling of completed protein molecules per second. Instead the extra ribosomes represent an increased inventory of ribosomes engaged in the process. In a real system ribosomes have finite sizes and can obstruct one another, so initiation may eventually be limited by crowding. The example is not a claim that drug treatment leaves initiation constant.

A further distinction arises in a transient experiment. Immediately after an elongation arrest, the existing population of translating ribosomes can remain on the message even if no completed chains emerge. In this state the steady-state relation cannot simply be used with an infinite residence time because the assumptions of steady flow and eventual completion no longer hold. Likewise, after a sudden initiation block, the previously engaged population gradually leaves as chains finish. The system is changing over time rather than remaining at steady state. The correct mathematical approach depends on whether the question describes an established steady state or a transition between states.

An idealized run-off example helps clarify the time dependence. Consider a messenger whose translating ribosomes normally complete their chains within 60 seconds of initiation. Suppose the message initially contains a mixture of ribosomes at different positions and no new ribosomes are allowed to initiate from time zero. If all existing ribosomes continue at normal speed and none stalls, all should have finished within 60 seconds. An experimental persistence of a substantial ribosome population much later would contradict at least one assumption of that simple model. It could indicate slowed elongation, continued initiation or retention of inactive complexes. The observation alone does not choose among those explanations.

Ribosome profiling concerns positions as well as total occupancy. A localized increase in ribosome density is consistent with longer dwell time at a position, particularly when the upstream flux remains comparable. A peak can also arise from a queue behind a stalled ribosome, so the location of maximum density is not always identical to the location of the first molecular obstruction. A decrease in downstream ribosome density may support a model in which fewer ribosomes pass the obstructed region. A sound interpretation compares distributions with an untreated control and considers initiation, transcript abundance and local sequence. This is the reasoning behind the use of positional observations to refine the textbook account of macrolide and chloramphenicol action.

Polysome profiles aggregate ribosome-containing complexes rather than reporting the position of each ribosome on every transcript. They can be useful to distinguish broad changes in the number of ribosomes per message. However, a shift towards heavier complexes may reflect slower progress, greater initiation or a change in the transcripts being translated. A shift towards lighter complexes may reflect reduced initiation, premature release or other alterations. The patterns support mechanistic hypotheses most strongly when paired with independent measurements of newly synthesized protein. An exam problem that provides both occupancy and output is deliberately offering two distinct variables, not two redundant ways of expressing the same one.

16. Chemical transfer, premature termination and the fate of a labelled product

Normal termination and puromycin-induced release can be compared by asking which group accepts the growing chain. During ordinary release at a stop codon, a protein release factor promotes hydrolysis of the linkage between the polypeptide and the transfer RNA. The chain is liberated from its carrier. By contrast, the puromycin reaction transfers the chain to a molecule with an accepting amino group, creating a different covalent product. Although both processes release a peptide from the ribosome, one involves hydrolytic release of a chain at the normal endpoint and the other involves transfer to a substrate analogue that terminates ordinary chain extension.

This difference helps explain why measuring a labelled puromycin-containing peptide can be evidence of prior ribosomal catalytic activity. The label is attached through the chemical reaction with the nascent chain. Simply finding a puromycin-related signal associated with a cell does not establish that every detected molecule has undergone the reaction. The interpretation requires distinction between the incorporated product and unreacted material, and between the time of incorporation and the subsequent fate of the released product. The conceptual lesson is that the specificity of a measurement depends on the chemical entity being detected.

A comparison with conventional amino acid incorporation highlights another distinction. A labelled amino acid that is correctly incorporated into an ordinary growing peptide does not necessarily prevent further growth. If the amino acid enters through a normal charged transfer RNA and undergoes peptide transfer, the chain remains on a transfer RNA and can subsequently be moved for another cycle. A puromycin-labelled chain has ordinarily left that route. Thus one label can mark continuing synthesis while the other is coupled to premature release. The two measurements may both provide information about translation but are not interchangeable on an individual chain.

Consider an educational scenario with a message encoding a 300-residue protein. Suppose an experiment detects released chains with a broad range of lengths smaller than 300 residues, and the short chains contain a terminal substrate analogue. This pattern is more directly consistent with an analogue-mediated chain termination mechanism than with an ordinary block in initiation. An initiation block would mainly prevent new chains from starting, while previously initiated ribosomes could still finish under the simple model. A pure translocation block would tend to retain unfinished chains in arrested complexes initially. Detection of short released chains without the terminal chemical signature would be less specific, because abnormal release, proteolysis or sample handling could also produce short products.

A meaningful analysis should distinguish the number of molecules from the amount of incorporated material. A population of 100 peptides, each 10 residues long, contains 1,000 residues. A population of 10 peptides, each 100 residues long, also contains 1,000 residues. An assay measuring total incorporated amino acid may give similar signals for the two populations despite their very different numbers and lengths. A full-length reporter assay could distinguish them if the functional reporter requires the complete chain. When different inhibitors change the length distribution of products, a single measurement of total material cannot be interpreted as a direct measurement of the number of full-length proteins.

The absence of a requirement for a stop codon is particularly important when reasoning about reading frames. Normal termination depends on the identity of the codon presented at the decoding centre. Puromycin-induced release does not mean that the genetic code has been reassigned or that every codon has become a stop codon. It bypasses the usual codon-specific release pathway by providing an alternative acceptor at the catalytic site. The DNA and messenger sequences need not change. Consequently a mutation that introduces a premature stop codon and a puromycin-induced premature release may both shorten a polypeptide, but the mechanisms and chemical endpoints differ.

This comparison also distinguishes the use of substrate analogues from the use of competitive inhibitors in a simple textbook enzyme model. A conventional competitive inhibitor reduces access of a normal substrate without being converted into the normal product. Puromycin itself undergoes peptide transfer and becomes part of the released product. It is therefore misleading to explain all of its effects solely through reversible competitive occupation of a binding site. The distinctive feature is its participation in a chemical reaction that leads away from the normal productive cycle. This general idea of an alternative substrate producing a nonproductive endpoint is encountered in other areas of biochemistry as well.

17. Time courses, protein half-life and the limitations of synthesis-block experiments

A protein concentration measured at a single time point reflects both synthesis and loss. Translation inhibitors are often discussed in experimental biology because stopping or strongly reducing synthesis makes it easier to examine the disappearance of an existing protein pool. The simple exponential model introduced earlier is useful, but the logic deserves careful examination. A decline after treatment demonstrates net loss of detectable protein under the experimental conditions. It does not automatically prove that the protein is normally degraded at the same rate in an unperturbed cell.

Suppose an initially steady-state protein is present at concentration P0 and has first-order degradation rate constant k. Before treatment, synthesis balances degradation, so s = kP0. If synthesis is completely stopped without altering k, abundance follows the exponential curve already derived. If synthesis is only partially inhibited, a nonzero synthesis rate s1 remains. The governing equation is then dP/dt = s1 − kP. Its solution for constant s1 and k is P(t) = s1/k + (P0 − s1/k) exp(−kt). Rather than approaching zero, the protein approaches a new steady state s1/k. Ignoring the residual synthesis can cause the apparent disappearance rate to be underestimated.

For a worked example, suppose P0 is 100 units, k is ln(2)/2 per hour, and treatment reduces synthesis to 20% of its original value. The new steady state is 20 units because the original steady state was 100 units and k remains fixed. After two hours, the transient difference from the new steady state has fallen by half. The predicted amount is 20 + (100 − 20)/2 = 60 units. After four hours it is 20 + 80/4 = 40 units. After six hours it is 20 + 80/8 = 30 units. A student assuming a complete synthesis block would incorrectly expect 50, 25 and 12.5 units at those times. The discrepancy is generated solely by residual synthesis in this hypothetical model.

The model also clarifies why different proteins disappear at different rates after a common translation block. The drug may act uniformly on the translation machinery but the proteins may have different first-order loss constants. If one protein has a half-life of one hour and another a half-life of eight hours, after four hours their relative amounts under an ideal complete block would be one sixteenth and approximately 0.707, respectively. It is not necessary to infer that the inhibitor binds more effectively to ribosomes producing the first protein. The difference can arise from the degradation kinetics of products made before treatment.

A measurement of enzyme activity may give a different time course from a measurement of protein abundance. A protein can become inactive before it is removed, for example through a change in conformation or chemical modification. Conversely a protein detected through an antibody may remain detectable even after losing its function. If the biological question concerns molecular turnover, a measure of abundance is needed; if the question concerns functional persistence, an activity assay is relevant. Comparing both can reveal a change in the ratio of activity to abundance. This distinction links translation inhibition with the following Academy chapters on protein folding and degradation without requiring their mechanisms to be taught again here.

Dilution introduces a further limitation. When cells divide or increase in volume, concentration may decline even if the total number of molecules in a tracked lineage is conserved. Under some simple growth models, a dilution constant adds to the degradation constant in determining the apparent first-order loss of concentration. A treatment that changes cell growth also changes the dilution contribution. Therefore the biological meaning of a half-life estimate depends on the measured variable, whether the population is growing and whether the treatment alters growth. These details matter when the problem compares measurements per cell, per unit total protein or per unit culture volume.

The appropriate conclusion is that synthesis-block experiments can reveal properties of protein persistence, but their quantitative interpretation is conditional. One should verify the extent of synthesis inhibition, use an appropriate normalization method and assess whether the treatment alters other processes that contribute to the observed loss. These requirements are not additional clinical or laboratory protocols; they are ordinary principles of causal interpretation. The examiner may specify ideal assumptions to make a calculation possible, or may deliberately omit one and ask the student to identify why a conclusion is not warranted.

18. Selectivity, sensitivity and apparent resistance: the mechanism-to-effect distinction

The term selective inhibition describes a difference in effect between biological systems, not an absolute division into susceptible and unsusceptible kingdoms. The bacterial 70S and eukaryotic cytosolic 80S ribosomes share a conserved functional core but differ in their molecular environments. A ligand that fits a bacterial ribosomal region may fail to bind effectively to the corresponding eukaryotic region. In other cases, conserved catalytic chemistry permits an interaction in both systems. This explains why the chapter contains bacterial-selective examples, a standard eukaryotic cytosolic inhibitor and a broadly acting substrate analogue.

A cell is not identical to an isolated target. The effect of an inhibitor in an intact organism depends on whether it reaches its target in an active form and how the organism responds. Consequently an organism showing little growth inhibition may possess an insensitive target, may restrict access of the inhibitor to a sensitive target, or may exhibit other protective properties. A comparison of whole-cell effects and effects on purified translation systems can help distinguish these general possibilities. A mechanistic question about the ribosome should not be answered solely with a claim about clinical effectiveness or susceptibility of all species.

The same principle applies to comparing mitochondria, chloroplasts and the cytosol. An organelle may contain a translation machine with ancestral similarities to bacterial ribosomes but also substantial specialized features. A drug that affects translation in one compartment may have different access or binding properties in another. It is important to state the relevant compartment explicitly. An exam statement that cycloheximide inhibits eukaryotic translation is normally intended to describe cytosolic ribosomes. It should not be interpreted as a universal claim that every mitochondrial or plastid translation reaction is inhibited to an identical extent.

Differences in the primary target also influence the interpretation of recovery. If a ribosomal inhibitor binds reversibly, release of the bound molecule could permit the same ribosome to resume its activity, provided that the complex and its substrate remain competent. If a product has already been prematurely released, the interrupted chain cannot simply be extended by the usual ribosome cycle from its former position. If a translation factor has undergone covalent inactivation, removing free inhibitory agent does not by itself restore the original molecular state of that factor. These outcomes follow from the distinct physical changes produced by different inhibitors.

A hypothetical comparison of recovery illustrates the principle. Consider three translation systems with equal initial reduction in completed product. In one, inhibited ribosomes can become functional again once a reversible occupancy effect disappears. In a second, many chains have been prematurely released, so production of those complete molecules requires new translation events. In a third, a soluble elongation factor remains covalently modified. Equal initial reductions therefore do not imply equal recovery kinetics. Nor does a slow recovery necessarily identify a covalent mechanism; delayed cellular responses and other limitations can also slow recovery. The inference requires knowledge of the molecular state after removal of the perturbation.

It is useful to distinguish inhibition of growth from killing. A cell that cannot make sufficient new protein may stop dividing, but whether it loses viability depends on many additional features of the system, including the particular agent and physiological context. Protein synthesis inhibitors are not uniformly bacteriostatic or uniformly bactericidal simply because they act on translation. The Academy's present focus is the direct molecular mechanism, and detailed antimicrobial resistance epidemiology and therapeutic classification belong outside this chapter. For examination purposes, the important point is that a biochemical effect cannot be equated automatically with a population-level outcome.

The seven examples can thus be organized using four different questions. Which molecule contains the primary binding or modification site? Which step of translation first becomes inefficient? What molecular intermediate or product is expected? Which cellular translation compartment contains a susceptible target? These questions are related but each adds information. A student who can answer all four can distinguish a drug binding the decoding centre from one acting at the peptide exit tunnel, even when both produce a decline in total protein accumulation. The same framework also prevents the confusion of a toxin acting on a factor with a small molecule directly occupying a ribosomal region.

19. From measured inhibition to an interpretable concentration-response relationship

An inhibitor is commonly described as strong or weak, but such terms have meaning only when the relevant measurement and conditions are specified. In a translation experiment the measured variable may be the rate of amino acid incorporation, the number of completed reporter molecules, or the fraction of an initially labelled population that remains in a ribosomal state. These variables need not respond in the same way. A compound that decreases the fidelity of decoding may have a particularly large effect on functional reporter activity while having a smaller effect on total peptide material. A compound causing premature release may strongly decrease full-length reporter output without abolishing every peptide transfer event. For this reason a numerical estimate of inhibition should always be tied to the biological quantity measured.

Let v0 be the initial rate of an experimentally defined translation readout without inhibitor, and v be the corresponding rate at a specified inhibitor concentration. If background signal has already been removed, the normalized residual activity is v/v0. The fractional inhibition is 1 − v/v0. These ratios are dimensionless because the numerator and denominator have the same units. If an untreated system incorporates 240 arbitrary units of amino acid label in a minute and a treated system incorporates 90 units per minute, the residual activity is 90/240 = 0.375. The inhibition of this particular readout is therefore 62.5%. It would be inaccurate to report that 62.5% of all ribosomes are bound by the compound unless the relationship between binding and activity has independently been established.

The need for background subtraction can be demonstrated with another hypothetical example. Suppose an untreated sample gives a signal of 220 units, a treated sample gives 100 units, and a blank lacking the relevant translation activity gives 20 units. The corrected untreated signal is 200 units and the corrected treated signal is 80 units. The normalized residual signal is 80/200 = 0.40 rather than 100/220, which is approximately 0.455. The difference arises because the raw signal includes a contribution unrelated to the measured translation reaction. If a student calculates inhibition directly from raw readings without noticing the blank, the resulting estimate can be systematically biased.

A simple binding model provides a useful physical contrast to these activity measurements. Consider a hypothetical target with one ligand-binding site, free inhibitor concentration I, and an equilibrium dissociation constant Kd. Assume that the free inhibitor concentration is effectively constant because the target is much less abundant than the inhibitor and that all target molecules are equivalent. The dissociation constant is defined by the ratio of concentrations of free target and free ligand to the target–ligand complex. If the total target concentration is the sum of the free and occupied forms, substitution gives the occupied fraction f = I/(Kd + I). When I equals Kd, half of the target molecules are occupied; when I equals nine times Kd, the fraction is nine tenths. The form of this relationship follows from the assumed single equilibrium binding interaction.

If, in addition, every occupied target is completely inactive and every free target retains its original activity, the residual activity in this simplified system would be 1 − f = Kd/(Kd + I). Under those unusually restrictive assumptions the concentration producing half of the original activity would numerically equal the dissociation constant. This identity is not a universal property of biological inhibitors. In a functioning ribosome, the affinity of an inhibitor may depend on the particular state occupied by the ribosome, and an occupied complex need not be inactive for every possible substrate sequence. Furthermore a catalytic modifying agent is not described by a simple equilibrium occupancy model alone. The derivation is useful precisely because it exposes the assumptions that must hold before a binding fraction can be inferred from an activity curve.

The concentration associated with half of a defined maximal response is often expressed as an IC50 for an inhibitory measurement, with the exact interpretation depending on how the response is normalized and fitted. An IC50 estimated in one assay need not match an IC50 estimated from a different readout or organism. For example, if one assay measures short-term incorporation and another measures eventual growth, the latter includes many additional biological processes. An IC50 is therefore not an intrinsic label for the primary target in every experimental condition. Comparing two numbers is meaningful only when the measured endpoint and assumptions are sufficiently comparable.

An especially important complication is the dependence of elongation inhibition on sequence. A concentration-response curve obtained with one reporter may not represent the same effect on another reporter, even though both use the same ribosome population. Chloramphenicol and macrolides illustrate this issue: the sequence and state of the nascent chain can alter the efficiency of arrest. A difference between reporter-specific curves is thus not automatically evidence for two different drug-binding sites. The appropriate question is whether a common target produces different functional consequences in different substrate contexts.

A useful examination exercise is to compare two hypothetical agents tested at the same nominal concentration. Agent A reduces corrected total peptide incorporation to 40% of control and functional reporter activity to 35%. Agent B reduces incorporation to 80% but functional reporter activity to 15%. It is tempting to call A the stronger inhibitor because total incorporation is lower. Yet B has the larger effect on the chosen functional reporter. These statements are not contradictory; they refer to different outputs. B could cause miscoding, incomplete chain production or another loss of protein function, and the measurements alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms. A direct claim that B is streptomycin would be premature without evidence concerning decoding or the chemical identity of the products.

Finally, concentration-response reasoning should not be confused with the probability models for successful completion. In the simple binding derivation the fraction refers to target molecules occupied at equilibrium. In the independent-step model the fraction refers to initiated chains that survive a number of transitions. These are different populations, and the two fractions are not interchangeable. If occupied ribosomes can undergo some transitions but fail at particular sequence-dependent states, a single occupancy value will not directly reveal the probability of completing an entire protein. Quantitative clarity starts with identifying the population to which a fraction refers.

20. Reading experimental data: controls, normalization and alternative explanations

Consider a hypothetical teaching dataset in which an unknown compound is studied using a previously prepared messenger in a translation system. The untreated system is assigned a value of 100 for charged transfer RNA abundance, peptide incorporation, full-length reporter abundance and reporter activity. In the presence of the compound the corresponding values are 102, 76, 72 and 18. These numbers are invented for reasoning practice, not empirical observations for any named compound. They show an approximately unchanged charging pool, a moderate decrease in newly made peptide, a moderate decrease in full-length reporter abundance and a much larger decrease in reporter activity.

The first conclusion is that a large decrease in the measured activity of the reporter is not fully explained by the change in abundance. Activity per unit reporter is 18/72 = 0.25 relative to the untreated value of 100/100. Thus the reporter's normalized specific activity has fallen to a quarter of control. That conclusion follows directly from the data if the activity assay is proportional to the amount of functional reporter and the abundance assay measures comparable molecules. The second conclusion, that the compound has caused miscoding, is not established by these data alone. Improper folding or another alteration of the protein could also lower activity per molecule. An examination may offer a direct amino acid sequence measurement that allows the ambiguity to be resolved. Without it, the mechanistically appropriate statement is that the observations support a defect in product quality.

The normal size of the charged transfer RNA pool also deserves scrutiny. A pool size is determined by both formation and utilization. If a drug reduces the use of charged transfer RNA by the ribosome, the pool could increase even if its rate of formation changes. Finding a normal pool size cannot establish that charging flux is exactly normal. To make a direct claim about the charging reaction, one needs a measurement that specifically reports the relevant enzymatic conversion. This distinction between a concentration and a rate is the same principle encountered in ribosome occupancy and protein turnover. It prevents a deceptively simple interpretation of steady-state measurements.

Now consider a second hypothetical dataset. Two translation systems produce equal quantities of full-length reporter in the absence of an inhibitor. In its presence, system X produces 25 units of full-length reporter and system Y produces 75 units. A subsequent observation finds that the sequence of the messenger in Y differs from X only in the region encoding a short internal segment of the protein. This suggests that a sequence-dependent response could account for the difference. But changing the messenger sequence can also alter its structure, stability or translation initiation, and changing the encoded protein sequence can alter its degradation. In a convincing comparison these other contributions should be considered rather than assuming the observed difference must arise from the ribosome's response to the peptide sequence.

A particularly helpful comparison is between a measure of local ribosome occupancy and a measure of full-length output. Suppose X displays a strong localized accumulation of translating ribosomes near the changed region while Y does not. Suppose also that X has reduced downstream occupancy and lower full-length protein. These three observations agree with a model in which more ribosomes fail to progress through a particular region of X. The inference is stronger than the one based on full-length product alone. It is still important to distinguish the actual stalled ribosome from ribosomes queuing behind it, and to recognize that a local density signal reflects the time ribosomes spend in the observed state. This is the appropriate level of interpretation for standard questions concerning sequence-dependent elongation inhibition.

A third kind of experiment uses a conceptual separation of the ribosomal and soluble-factor components. Imagine an unknown impairment that is retained in the isolated ribosomal fraction, while the soluble factors remain competent. In a reconstituted comparison, a functional ribosomal fraction combined with the affected soluble fraction supports normal translation, whereas the affected ribosomal fraction combined with functional soluble factors does not. The result is consistent with a defect associated with the ribosomes. Reverse the pattern and a factor-associated defect becomes more plausible. The assumptions are that the fractions are sufficiently separated, that the component amounts are comparable and that preparation does not introduce a separate injury. This reasoning can distinguish a ribosome-targeting inhibitor from a factor-targeting inhibitor at the conceptual level without depending on an operational protocol.

The apparent rescue of one reaction by provision of a functional component does not identify every detail of the original perturbation. A protein factor could be present but inactive, absent, or sequestered in a nonproductive state. If an exam specifically supplies evidence that the factor carries a covalent ADP-ribose modification and that its role is translocation, the standard diphtheria toxin association becomes relevant. In contrast, if the defect is retained by the ribosomal fraction and is associated with the E-site region of a cytosolic eukaryotic large subunit, cycloheximide is a standard association. The evidential distinction comes from the molecular location of the defect rather than the general statement that elongation is reduced.

The design of a comparison also requires attention to the denominator used for normalization. If a treatment reduces the total amount of cellular protein, normalizing a selected reporter to total protein can sometimes obscure a decline in absolute reporter per cell. If a treatment changes the number of cells in a sample, signal per culture volume and signal per cell can produce different numerical impressions. If a treatment affects the general translation system, an internal reporter translated by the same machinery may itself be altered and may not provide an unaffected reference. These are not reasons to reject normalization. They are reasons to choose the denominator based on the scientific question and to state what remains constant.

When two readouts disagree, the appropriate response is to ask what each readout represents. A high amount of ribosome-associated peptide and a low amount of released full-length product can coexist if the elongation machinery retains unfinished chains. A high amount of total released peptide and a low amount of complete protein can coexist if many chains leave the machinery prematurely. A substantial amount of full-length protein and a low amount of active enzyme can coexist if the proteins are incorrectly decoded or fail to mature. These observations are not errors in arithmetic; they are the expected consequences of measuring different stages of a biological process. They provide a powerful organizing principle for Part C questions.

21. Temporal ordering of primary inhibition and downstream cellular changes

The translation system is closely connected with transcription, metabolism, protein quality control and growth. Consequently, an intervention whose first effect is on a ribosome can produce changes in many other measurements later. It is useful to separate the earliest direct molecular event from subsequent changes in cellular physiology. In bacteria, newly synthesized proteins contribute to the machinery that regulates transcription and RNA stability, and translation itself can influence the fate of a transcript. In eukaryotic cells, synthesis of regulatory proteins participates in many signalling responses. A translation block therefore need not leave the RNA pool unchanged indefinitely. The expectation that RNA synthesis initially continues is a mechanistic contrast, not a claim of permanent independence between transcription and translation.

Consider a hypothetical cell with a transcriptional activator that normally has a short lifetime. When translation is interrupted, existing activator molecules continue to be removed. The activity of genes requiring the activator may decline only after its concentration falls below the level needed for substantial transcription. At an early time point, translation may already be strongly reduced while transcription of these genes remains near normal. At a later time point both processes may be reduced. If only the later point were examined, an investigator might incorrectly infer that the compound directly inhibits both RNA polymerase and the ribosome. The earlier observation helps identify the temporal order, while additional direct assays are needed to establish the molecular target.

The reverse example is equally instructive. An inhibitor that directly prevents transcription can cause a gradual decrease in protein synthesis as the existing messenger population decays. The lag depends on transcript persistence and the translation rates of the messages remaining in the cell. A reduction in protein output after a prolonged transcription block does not convert the transcription inhibitor into a ribosome-targeting drug. A previously supplied messenger in a conceptual cell-free system helps separate the two possibilities, provided that the remaining components of the translation system are competent. This distinction links the present chapter with the earlier Academy chapters concerning RNA polymerase inhibitors.

A more complex situation arises when defective decoding produces proteins that interfere with existing cellular functions. An altered protein may fold inefficiently, be removed more rapidly or lose an enzymatic activity. If these changes affect energy metabolism, the energy available for further translation may decline. The earliest event is an altered fidelity of translation, whereas the later energy limitation can impair many additional reactions. One should therefore avoid treating every secondary defect as another primary molecular target. The distinction is particularly important in interpreting an intact-cell response, where many biochemical processes remain coupled.

A useful way to reason about timing is to distinguish an immediate state-dependent response, a delayed turnover response and a longer-term population response. Immediate responses concern the activity or state of the affected molecular machinery. Delayed responses arise as previously synthesized molecules are depleted, defective products accumulate or regulatory systems adjust. Population responses concern changes in the number or viability of cells and integrate many processes over time. These categories are not defined by fixed times shared by all organisms; they describe the causal order of events. An experiment's temporal resolution determines whether distinct phases can be observed.

Selective inhibition of organellar translation also illustrates temporal separation. Mitochondrial translation contributes a small subset of proteins to the cell's total protein pool. Many components of mitochondrial complexes originate from cytosolic translation, while some are encoded and produced within the mitochondrion. Reduced synthesis of the mitochondrially produced subset can ultimately compromise assembly or maintenance of a larger complex containing products from both sources. At first, previously assembled complexes may retain their function. Later, failure to replace essential components may reduce organellar activity. The magnitude and timing of the outcome depend on the lifetime of those complexes and the cellular context. An immediate global peptide incorporation signal cannot capture all these consequences.

The examination implication is that a time series should be interpreted according to the process each measurement represents. If peptide incorporation declines first, followed by a fall in a short-lived protein and finally by a change in transcription of its target genes, the order supports a translation-first model. It does not by itself exclude an independent effect on transcription. A convincing interpretation considers temporal precedence together with direct biochemical evidence. Causality is stronger when an identified molecular interaction explains the earliest change and the later changes follow from established relationships between the biological variables.

Finally, these distinctions explain why the primary mechanism of a translation inhibitor should not be defined by whether the treated cell eventually stops growing. Many unrelated interventions can lead to the same growth endpoint. Classification by the molecular site and interrupted translation step remains meaningful even when downstream outcomes overlap. The chapter's seven standard examples therefore provide not just a memorization list but a set of contrasting molecular events whose consequences can be followed from the ribosome or factor through the production, quality and persistence of protein.

Part B and structured conceptual practice

Original examination practice; not previous-year questions.

1. Part B — Which drug most directly restricts productive entry of charged tRNA into the bacterial ribosome? A. Chloramphenicol B. Tetracycline C. Cycloheximide D. Puromycin.

Answer and reasoning

B. A interferes with peptide transfer, C with eukaryotic translocation, and D accepts the chain and causes premature release.

2. Part B — Why does a puromycin-released chain lack normal completion? A. It necessarily changes the DNA B. It is released only at UGA C. Peptide transfer to puromycin ends ordinary elongation D. It destroys the ribosome.

Answer and reasoning

C. Its acceptor chemistry permits chain transfer without normal stop-codon recognition.

3. Part B — Which pair correctly distinguishes a ribosome-binding translocation inhibitor from a factor-modifying inhibitor? A. Cycloheximide and diphtheria toxin B. Tetracycline and chloramphenicol C. Streptomycin and erythromycin D. Puromycin and tetracycline.

Answer and reasoning

A. Both disrupt progression, but their direct targets differ.

7. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: Normal transfer RNA charging can coexist with inhibited translation. Reason: Productive binding of charged transfer RNA to the ribosome is a distinct step.

Answer and reasoning

Both true; the reason explains the assertion. Tetracycline is a standard example.

9. Matching — Match chloramphenicol, streptomycin, puromycin, cycloheximide with peptide transfer, miscoding, premature release, eukaryotic translocation.

Answer and reasoning

In the same order. These labels describe principal exam associations and not every possible secondary effect.

11. Multiple statements — (i) Chloramphenicol inhibits bacterial peptidyl transfer. (ii) Every peptide bond is equally sensitive. (iii) Macrolides can act in a sequence-dependent manner.

Answer and reasoning

i and iii. Context-specific inhibition contradicts ii.

13. Part B — Which event is immediately before EF-G-dependent movement in an ordinary bacterial elongation cycle? A. Transcription initiation B. Transfer of the peptide to the A-site tRNA C. Release-factor-mediated hydrolysis D. Initiator tRNA charging.

Answer and reasoning

B. Peptide transfer creates a longer peptidyl-tRNA and a deacylated tRNA, which then move relative to the ribosome. A and D are earlier, distinct processes; C normally occurs at termination.

14. Part B — Which pairing is incorrect? A. Chloramphenicol—bacterial PTC B. Erythromycin—bacterial exit tunnel C. Streptomycin—bacterial decoding centre D. Cycloheximide—bacterial RNA polymerase.

Answer and reasoning

D. Cycloheximide acts primarily on eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes and impairs elongation. The other associations describe standard ribosomal targets.

15. Part B — Which agent is best described as a substrate analogue rather than simply a site-occupying inhibitor? A. Puromycin B. Tetracycline C. Erythromycin D. Streptomycin.

Answer and reasoning

A. Puromycin accepts the nascent chain, producing a covalent nonproductive end product. The other choices primarily disturb normal ribosomal substrate handling or elongation.

16. Part B — The principal catalytic RNA in the bacterial peptide transferase centre is A. 16S rRNA B. 23S rRNA C. 5S rRNA alone D. mRNA.

Answer and reasoning

B. The 23S rRNA of the large subunit forms the central catalytic environment. 16S rRNA is principally associated with small-subunit decoding; mRNA carries sequence information.

17. Part B — Which statement about streptomycin-associated miscoding is correct? A. Each error permanently changes DNA B. A correctly charged near-cognate tRNA may be accepted C. All chains terminate at the first codon D. The drug primarily blocks RNA synthesis.

Answer and reasoning

B. Altered discrimination can permit incorporation from a near-cognate tRNA. The error is made in translation and need not alter inherited nucleic acid sequence. The other statements confuse separate mechanisms.

18. Part B — Which molecule provides the ADP-ribose moiety in the standard diphtheria toxin mechanism? A. GTP B. NAD+ C. ATP D. FAD.

Answer and reasoning

B. NAD+ supplies the transferred group and nicotinamide is released. GTP is used by elongation factors in the ordinary translation cycle, not as the ADP-ribose donor.

19. Part B — Which statement best describes eukaryotic cytosolic cycloheximide action? A. Inhibition of translocation associated with the large-subunit E-site region B. Direct destruction of mRNA C. Inhibition of tRNA charging by all synthetases D. Replacement of stop codons.

Answer and reasoning

A. Cycloheximide binds a ribosomal pocket associated with the E site and interferes with movement. The remaining choices do not describe its principal target.

20. Part B — Which is an appropriate statement about bacterial and organellar ribosomes? A. All mitochondrial ribosomes are identical to bacterial 70S particles B. Organellar translation can show bacterial-like sensitivity, with important differences C. Every bacterial translation inhibitor is harmless to organelles D. All 80S ribosomes are mitochondrial.

Answer and reasoning

B. Endosymbiotic ancestry explains some shared characteristics, but organellar ribosomes have distinct compositions and sensitivities.

21. Part B — Which change distinguishes puromycin release from normal termination? A. Transfer of the peptide to a substrate analogue B. Mandatory recognition of UAA C. Release of the entire mRNA as a polypeptide D. Addition of a new DNA nucleotide.

Answer and reasoning

A. Normal termination uses release-factor-promoted hydrolysis; puromycin accepts the chain and interrupts elongation independently of the ordinary stop-codon signal.

22. Part B — Which observation best fits inhibition of A-site accommodation? A. Productive entry of a charged tRNA decreases despite normal charging B. All DNA is degraded C. Translation continues accurately at the same rate D. The messenger becomes a protein.

Answer and reasoning

A. Charging and accommodation are distinct processes. The other choices either concern unrelated processes or contradict the proposed inhibition.

23. Part B — A macrolide binds in which ribosomal region in the standard bacterial model? A. Small-subunit anticodon recognition site B. Large-subunit nascent peptide exit tunnel C. DNA promoter D. Nuclear pore.

Answer and reasoning

B. Erythromycin occupies the exit-tunnel region. Its functional effects can include sequence-dependent interference with peptide transfer.

24. Part B — Which is the correct direction of ordinary elongation? A. Protein C to N, mRNA 3′ to 5′ B. Protein N to C, mRNA 5′ to 3′ C. Both 3′ to 5′ D. Neither direction is defined.

Answer and reasoning

B. The ribosome reads successive codons from the 5′ end towards the 3′ end; new residues are added to the carboxyl end of the growing chain.

25. Part B — Which factor is the bacterial functional counterpart of eEF2? A. EF-G B. EF-Tu C. RF1 D. IF3.

Answer and reasoning

A. EF-G coordinates bacterial translocation. EF-Tu delivers charged tRNA; RF1 and IF3 participate in termination and initiation respectively.

26. Part B — Which statement about chloramphenicol is most accurate? A. Every sequence is equally sensitive B. It inhibits peptide transfer at the bacterial PTC, with context-dependent efficiency C. It acts primarily on bacterial DNA helicase D. It replaces eEF2.

Answer and reasoning

B. The classical target is correct, but arrest efficiency can depend on the substrates and nascent chain. A is an overgeneralization.

27. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: Protein abundance need not immediately become zero when translation is inhibited. Reason: Proteins present before the inhibition can persist until they are removed.

Answer and reasoning

Both true; the reason explains the assertion. A synthesis rate and the existing molecular pool are different variables.

28. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: A heavy polysome profile proves high protein output. Reason: Slow elongation can increase the time ribosomes remain on a message.

Answer and reasoning

Assertion false, reason true. High occupancy can reflect slow transit and does not prove a high rate of completion.

29. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: Bacterial translation and mammalian cytosolic translation differ in sensitivity to some antibiotics. Reason: Their ribosomal molecular environments differ despite a conserved functional core.

Answer and reasoning

Both true; the reason explains the assertion. Selectivity does not imply absolute insensitivity of all organellar systems.

30. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: Puromycin-mediated premature release requires a normal stop codon. Reason: Puromycin can act as an alternative acceptor at the peptidyl transferase centre.

Answer and reasoning

Assertion false, reason true. The reaction does not depend on ordinary stop-codon recognition.

31. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: A decline in protein synthesis after diphtheria toxin action is associated with impaired translocation. Reason: The toxin modifies eEF2 rather than directly occupying the ordinary bacterial decoding site.

Answer and reasoning

Both true; the reason explains the mechanism in the eukaryotic system.

32. Multiple statements — Evaluate (i) Streptomycin can alter decoding fidelity; (ii) tetracycline primarily blocks tRNA synthesis; (iii) peptide transfer and translocation are separate steps; (iv) cycloheximide primarily inhibits transcription. Which are correct?

Answer and reasoning

i and iii. Tetracycline restricts productive ribosomal entry of aminoacyl-tRNA rather than tRNA production. Cycloheximide is an elongation inhibitor.

33. Multiple statements — Evaluate (i) A large-subunit inhibitor necessarily binds the PTC; (ii) macrolides can cause sequence-dependent arrest; (iii) puromycin can act without normal stop-codon recognition; (iv) eEF2 is a bacterial RNA polymerase.

Answer and reasoning

ii and iii. The large subunit includes other functional sites, including the exit tunnel. eEF2 is a eukaryotic elongation factor.

34. Multiple statements — Evaluate (i) RNA abundance may initially remain constant after translation inhibition; (ii) it must remain constant indefinitely; (iii) a protein pool can decline after its synthesis is stopped; (iv) the decline always proves direct degradation by the inhibitor.

Answer and reasoning

i and iii. Secondary changes may alter RNA later; protein loss can reflect normal turnover and does not imply that the inhibitor is a protease.

35. Matching — Match processes A. aminoacyl-tRNA productive entry B. decoding discrimination C. peptide transfer D. cytosolic eukaryotic translocation with 1. tetracycline 2. streptomycin 3. chloramphenicol 4. cycloheximide.

Answer and reasoning

A–1, B–2, C–3, D–4. The pairing concerns the primary textbook effect rather than all downstream consequences.

36. Matching — Match observations A. terminal substrate analogue on short released chains B. modified soluble eukaryotic movement factor C. bacterial exit-tunnel occupancy D. reduced productive entry of charged bacterial tRNA with 1. erythromycin 2. diphtheria toxin 3. tetracycline 4. puromycin.

Answer and reasoning

A–4, B–2, C–1, D–3. Each observation is matched to the molecule's distinctive action.

Part C analytical and numerical practice

Original examination practice; not previous-year questions.

4. Part C — In a hypothetical assay, reporter abundance is 80% of control and activity is 20% of control. What is its relative specific activity?

Answer and reasoning

0.20/0.80 = 0.25, or 25% of control. The result supports a change in functional quality but does not uniquely identify miscoding; folding or modification defects could also explain it.

5. Part C — A translation inhibitor reduces a protein from 200 to 25 units in 9 hours. Assuming complete inhibition of synthesis and first-order removal, find the half-life.

Answer and reasoning

200 → 100 → 50 → 25, three half-lives, hence 3 hours. If residual synthesis persists, the simple estimate is no longer justified.

6. Part C — Messages of equal length and equal abundance differ in their sensitivity to erythromycin. Why is the observation compatible with a shared ribosomal target?

Answer and reasoning

A shared target may inhibit selected elongation states more strongly depending on the nascent peptide sequence. Equal length does not imply equal sequence or arrest propensity. Initiation and product stability should also be considered.

8. Part C — After initiation is selectively blocked, existing ribosomes continue elongating. Predict the idealized time-dependent polysome pattern.

Answer and reasoning

Ribosomes complete chains and run off the messages; heavy polysomes generally decline. A pure elongation arrest can instead initially retain ribosomes on messages.

10. Part C — A hypothetical agent selectively blocks organellar translation but not cytosolic translation. Why could a whole-cell incorporation assay underestimate biological significance?

Answer and reasoning

Cytosolic synthesis can dominate total incorporation, while the smaller organellar protein pool contains essential proteins.

12. Part C — A chain requires 50 independent elongation transitions, each with 0.98 probability of success. Estimate the fraction completed.

Answer and reasoning

0.98^50 ≈ 0.364. This is an idealized model, not a measured drug effect.

37. Part C — A hypothetical system has 90 units of charged tRNA and 10 units of new peptide, compared with 100 and 100 in the control. Does this prove inhibition of peptide transfer rather than tRNA entry?

Answer and reasoning

No. Both productive entry and transfer defects could produce low incorporation with an appreciable charged pool. A measurement of productive A-site occupancy and the state of the donor chain would distinguish these hypotheses. Pool abundance is also not identical to charging flux.

38. Part C — In a hypothetical synchronized elongation population, the first new peptide bond forms but successive additions are impaired; the pre-movement complex accumulates. Which operation is most directly implicated?

Answer and reasoning

Translocation. The successful first bond argues against a complete block of the initial transfer chemistry, while retention of the pre-movement state supports a movement defect. Later substrate entry could be secondarily reduced.

39. Part C — A control reporter produces 150 units of protein and 120 units of activity. A treated reporter produces 100 units of protein and 20 units of activity. Calculate relative activity per unit protein.

Answer and reasoning

Control specific activity = 120/150 = 0.8; treated = 20/100 = 0.2. Relative value = 0.2/0.8 = 0.25. A quality or maturation defect is plausible, but direct sequence evidence is needed to identify miscoding.

40. Part C — A hypothetical translation readout has control signal 250, treated signal 110 and a common blank of 30. Calculate background-corrected percent inhibition.

Answer and reasoning

Corrected control = 220, treated = 80. Residual fraction = 80/220 = 0.3636; inhibition ≈ 63.6%. Raw signals would give a different, biased estimate.

41. Part C — A one-site equilibrium binding model has Kd = 4 arbitrary concentration units and free inhibitor = 12 units. Calculate occupied fraction. Can this be assumed equal to translation inhibition?

Answer and reasoning

12/(4+12) = 0.75. It equals fractional inhibition only if every bound target is inactive, every free target is normally active and the model's other assumptions hold. Ribosomal state dependence can invalidate the inference.

42. Part C — A message normally receives one initiated ribosome every 8 seconds, with a mean translation time of 48 seconds. Estimate average occupancy under the independent steady-state completion model. If translation time increases to 72 seconds, what is the predicted occupancy?

Answer and reasoning

J = 1/8 s⁻¹. N = JT gives 6 and 9 ribosomes respectively. Output does not automatically increase; crowding, losses or altered initiation could invalidate the model.

43. Part C — A hypothetical message contains ribosomes at different positions when initiation is stopped. The maximum remaining transit time at unchanged speed is 45 seconds. At 90 seconds many ribosomes remain. What follows?

Answer and reasoning

At least one ideal assumption fails: existing ribosomes may progress more slowly, initiation may persist or inactive complexes may remain associated. The data alone do not uniquely identify the cause.

44. Part C — Assume independent errors of 0.002 per residue for a 200-residue protein. Estimate the fraction containing no error; compare with 0.01 per residue.

Answer and reasoning

(0.998)^200 ≈ 0.670; (0.99)^200 ≈ 0.134. A modest increase in error per residue has a large cumulative effect. Error-free fraction is not exactly the functional fraction.

45. Part C — Two hypothetical samples each contain 1,200 incorporated residues. A contains 120 chains of ten residues, B contains 12 chains of 100 residues. Can equal total label establish equal full-length output for a 100-residue reporter?

Answer and reasoning

No. Total residues are equal, but sample A contains no 100-residue product and sample B contains 12 such chains. Chain length and molecular identity are necessary to infer full-length output.

46. Part C — An initially steady-state protein has 80 units and a half-life of 2 h. Synthesis is completely blocked. Predict abundance after 6 h.

Answer and reasoning

Three half-lives have elapsed, so 80 × (1/2)^3 = 10 units. The calculation assumes constant first-order loss, no new synthesis and no independent dilution contribution.

47. Part C — An initially steady-state protein has 80 units, half-life 2 h, and synthesis falls to 25% of normal while degradation is unchanged. Calculate abundance after 4 h.

Answer and reasoning

New steady state = 20 units. After two half-lives, P = 20 + (80−20)/4 = 35 units. Assuming complete synthesis inhibition would incorrectly predict 20 units.

48. Part C — One hypothetical drug reduces RNA synthesis immediately, and protein synthesis decreases only later. A second inhibits protein incorporation immediately with pre-supplied mRNA intact. What is the most appropriate mechanistic distinction?

Answer and reasoning

The first pattern is compatible with transcription-first inhibition and later messenger depletion. The second supports a translation-level effect. Direct molecular assays are needed to establish exact targets.

49. Part C — A cell-free comparison shows that a defect follows an isolated ribosomal fraction rather than soluble factors. Which is more consistent with the pattern: a direct ribosomal inhibitor or a soluble-factor-specific modification?

Answer and reasoning

A ribosome-associated defect. This does not prove the identity of the compound: fraction integrity, carryover and component competence must be considered. A factor-specific modification would more directly follow the affected factor fraction.

50. Part C — An unknown perturbation gives an abundance peak near a particular codon and depletion of ribosome occupancy downstream. What inference is supported, and what ambiguity remains?

Answer and reasoning

Local slowing or arrest is consistent with the pattern, particularly if input flux and message abundance are comparable. Queuing can displace a peak from the first arrested position, and altered initiation or transcript features may influence occupancy.

51. Part C — Two reporters have comparable mRNA abundance and length but one is preferentially arrested by a macrolide. Is this necessarily evidence for different binding sites?

Answer and reasoning

No. A common ribosomal site can yield context-dependent arrest when the nascent peptide sequences differ. The inference is strengthened by positional ribosome and completed-product measurements.

52. Part C — A compound suppresses eukaryotic cytosolic translation, while another suppresses bacterial translation in isolated systems. Is organism selectivity sufficient to predict all intact-cell effects?

Answer and reasoning

No. Compartmental access, target differences, physiological state and organellar sensitivity matter. Isolated-system activity identifies a more direct biochemical effect than a general intact-cell response.

53. Part C — A hypothetical protein population declines from 96 to 24 units over 4 h during a complete synthesis block. Calculate k.

Answer and reasoning

Two half-lives in 4 h give t½ = 2 h. k = ln(2)/2 ≈ 0.347 h⁻¹. If synthesis persists or loss is not first order, this estimate is not justified.

54. Part C — A hypothetical drug causes a 90% decrease in whole-cell mitochondrial translation, which normally contributes 5% of total measured incorporation. Assuming cytosolic synthesis is unchanged, predict the residual whole-cell signal.

Answer and reasoning

Mitochondrial contribution remaining is 0.05 × 0.10 = 0.005. Cytosolic contribution is 0.95, giving 0.955 or 95.5%. A small change in total signal can conceal a large compartment-specific defect.

55. Part C — A treatment leaves 60% of total incorporated peptide but only 20% full-length reporter. What additional result would distinguish premature release from persistent stalling?

Answer and reasoning

Compare whether incomplete chains are released or retained in ribosomal complexes and determine the nature of their termini. Short released chains with an alternative acceptor are consistent with premature release, whereas retained intermediates support arrest.

56. Part C — A student claims that washing away every translation inhibitor restores every unfinished chain immediately. Evaluate this claim.

Answer and reasoning

False. Reversible occupancy may permit competent complexes to recover, but prematurely released chains cannot resume the ordinary elongation cycle and covalently modified factors do not automatically regain their original state. Cell-level recovery also depends on secondary effects.

Examination traps and corrective reasoning

  • A subunit number is not a reaction. The large subunit has multiple functional regions; distinguish PTC, exit tunnel and movement-associated sites.
  • A-site entry is not aminoacylation. A charged tRNA can exist without productive accommodation.
  • Miscoding is not a DNA mutation. A wrong residue can occur without a change in nucleotide sequence.
  • Peptide transfer is not translocation. The first makes a bond; the second advances the complex.
  • Puromycin release is not ordinary termination. An alternative acceptor releases the chain without a stop-codon requirement.
  • Erythromycin is not a universal tunnel plug. Nascent sequence can determine arrest efficiency.
  • Chloramphenicol is not equally effective at every peptide bond. Context modifies arrest.
  • eEF2 is not EF-G. They are functionally related but are distinct factors in different translation systems.
  • A heavy polysome is not proof of high protein output. Occupancy reflects residence time as well as input.
  • A synthesis block is not direct protein degradation. Existing proteins can disappear through their ordinary turnover.
  • Bacterial selectivity is not absolute organelle insensitivity. Translation compartment and access matter.
  • IC50 is not invariably Kd. The relationship requires restrictive binding and activity assumptions.
