1. The biological problem: gaining carbon without losing water

A terrestrial leaf is both a photosynthetic organ and an interface between the hydrated interior of a plant and a usually drier atmosphere. Carbon dioxide must diffuse from the surrounding air to the cells that fix carbon. Water evaporates from wet internal surfaces and escapes along the same gaseous route in the opposite direction. A plant therefore cannot regulate carbon gain independently of water loss simply by sealing its epidermis. The cuticle reduces uncontrolled evaporation, whereas stomata provide adjustable passages. These two adaptations make it possible to maintain a hydrated tissue while periodically exchanging gases with the external environment.

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, principally from its aerial surfaces. It is distinct from the uptake of liquid water by roots, the ascent of liquid in xylem and the exudation of liquid droplets through hydathodes. During sustained transpiration, these processes are often coupled: removal of water from the leaf lowers the water potential of tissues and promotes movement of water from the stem and ultimately the soil. Nevertheless, a change in any one of them does not necessarily produce an instantaneous equal change in the others. An intact plant can store water in living tissues and elastic structures, so water loss can temporarily exceed uptake.

Imagine two identical well-watered plants in a room. One is enclosed in a transparent humid chamber and the other is exposed to moving dry air. Both may receive the same illumination and have similar initial stomatal apertures. The second initially tends to lose water faster because the vapour concentration gradient and external transport conditions differ. As the leaves begin to experience declining water status, their stomata may partly close. The later transpiration rate therefore depends on a physiological response as well as the physical environment. This simple contrast explains why a statement such as “dry air increases transpiration” needs its experimental conditions specified.

Water loss is not an ATP-driven secretion of individual water molecules through stomata. The energy required to evaporate water comes from the surrounding thermal and radiation energy budget. Plant metabolism contributes indirectly, for example through active solute transport in guard cells and roots. A useful distinction is between the energy that changes stomatal aperture and the thermodynamic driving force that moves water vapour. The former can be biologically regulated even when the latter is imposed by weather.

The movement of carbon dioxide and water through stomata is central to plant physiology because it links photosynthesis with hydraulics, temperature and nutrition. Restricting water loss can protect cells against dehydration but also restrict carbon assimilation and decrease evaporative cooling. A high transpiration rate can aid the delivery of dissolved nutrients and help cool a leaf, yet sustained loss beyond the supply capacity of the soil and xylem creates water deficits. Neither maximal nor minimal transpiration is a universally advantageous physiological state. The question is how the leaf adjusts exchange to its carbon requirements and water supply.

How the evidence developed

The early physiology of transpiration faced an identification problem. A change in water loss from a leaf might arise because the surrounding air had changed, because the pores had altered their opening, or because both changes occurred together. Simply weighing a leaf could not isolate the contributions. Francis Darwin's work around the beginning of the twentieth century addressed this problem by using separate measurements of stomatal condition and water loss. His porometer provided a way to infer changes in pore openness from gas passage through leaves. The 1911 report and subsequent studies demonstrate the historical shift from descriptive observations toward experimental separation of physical and biological causes.

In later experiments, Darwin tried to bypass the changing aperture of natural stomata by blocking pores with a surface coating and making openings that connected internal air spaces with outside air. Such artificial openings gave a relatively fixed alternative route and helped investigate environmental effects on evaporation apart from natural stomatal movements. The method was not equivalent to an intact leaf with a normal boundary layer: cutting tissue can disrupt hydration, and a coating can change radiation and surface properties. Its value was the attempt to hold one important variable approximately constant rather than assuming that all environmental effects operated through stomata.

By the late 1960s, the osmotic basis of pore movement was being tested by experiments involving potassium uptake. Fischer and Hsiao studied opening in isolated epidermal strips of broad bean, Vicia faba, with different potassium chloride conditions. Their results linked aperture increase to potassium accumulation of sufficient magnitude, together with accompanying counterions, to explain changes in osmotic state. Later work used additional ways of assessing potassium in guard cells. These observations helped establish the ion-transport basis of aperture control, beyond simply noting that swollen guard cells open the pore.

An isolated epidermis is valuable because it removes much of the influence of mesophyll photosynthesis and the vascular water supply. This isolation is also a limitation. An epidermal strip in an experimental solution has different ion access and water relations from an intact attached leaf. A modern interpretation therefore combines reductionist observations with studies of intact leaves. A transport pathway inferred from isolated cells should predict changes in aperture under controlled treatments and, ultimately, changes in conductance in the entire organ. Convergence of these observations is stronger than any one experimental method.

The historical lesson is especially useful for examination reasoning. If a researcher changes relative humidity and measures only mass loss, they have not directly established that stomata closed, that pores opened, or that guard-cell potassium changed. If they measure aperture as well, they can describe a structural response. To identify an ion mechanism, they need relevant transport measurements or genetic perturbations, preferably with independent checks. The scale of the claim should match the scale of the observation. A result at the whole-plant level can motivate a molecular hypothesis, but it does not independently prove it.

2. The soil–plant–atmosphere continuum

Water movement in the plant must be understood as a connected transport system rather than as a collection of independent events. Liquid enters roots from the soil, moves through root tissues into the xylem, ascends through stems and veins, and reaches living cells and their extracellular walls. At the evaporating surfaces, molecules leave the liquid phase and become vapour. The vapour then diffuses through internal air spaces and out into the atmosphere. The continuous sequence is called the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum, often abbreviated SPAC.

In the liquid segment, water flows down a gradient of total water potential, which includes pressure, osmotic and, where relevant, gravitational and matric components. A root and a leaf can both have negative water potentials relative to pure water yet the leaf can still draw water from the root because its potential is more negative. In a transpiring leaf, the tension generated in the liquid phase is related to curved air–water interfaces in cell walls. Cohesion permits a pressure gradient to be transmitted through the xylem water column. These principles explain how transpiration can promote bulk liquid flow without a pump operating in each vessel.

At the leaf, evaporation and vapour diffusion are separate physical steps. Mesophyll cell walls and other hydrated internal surfaces can supply water to air spaces. The exact distribution of evaporation depends on leaf anatomy, illumination and local temperatures, so the familiar diagram of uniformly evaporating mesophyll walls is a useful introductory model rather than a universal microscopic map. The stomatal pore typically connects a substomatal cavity to outside air. Vapour emerging from that pore must then cross a thin layer of comparatively still air next to the epidermis.

Consider a hydraulic system at a steady flow rate, with gravitational potential included in the water potentials used. Let K be an effective soil-to-leaf hydraulic conductance, expressed in mmol of water per square metre of leaf area per second per MPa. If the soil potential is −0.2 MPa and leaf potential is −0.8 MPa, a conductance of 5 yields a flux of 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Rearranging the expression J = K(Ψsoil − Ψleaf) gives Ψleaf = Ψsoil − J/K. At the same soil potential, doubling the imposed flux while holding conductance fixed requires a larger potential difference. The plant cannot indefinitely maintain this calculation because tissue permeability, stomata and xylem integrity may change as potential declines.

A plant with a large capacity for storing water can delay the decline of leaf water potential after a sudden increase in atmospheric demand. This does not mean that storage creates water. It supplies a temporary deficit until uptake rises, transpiration declines or reserves become depleted. Leaves sometimes begin losing water soon after illumination, before a corresponding sap-flow increase is recorded at the stem base. Such time differences are expected from a dynamic system. In an exam, interpreting an instantaneous measurement as a steady-state flux may therefore lead to an incorrect conclusion.

The atmosphere can have an extremely negative effective water potential at low relative humidity. However, calculating liquid flux by directly using that atmospheric potential and the hydraulic conductance of the root and stem is not valid. The evaporation interface and gas-phase transport add distinct constraints. The next sections use vapour pressure and gas conductance for the external segment and retain water potential and hydraulic conductance for the internal liquid segment.

Hydraulic resistance distributed across the plant

The resistance to liquid transport is distributed among the rhizosphere, root tissues, xylem of roots and stems, leaf veins and the pathways beyond the terminal veins. In a simplified series model, each region carries approximately the same steady flux. The water-potential drop across each region equals the flux multiplied by its hydraulic resistance. The segment with the largest resistance therefore accounts for the largest potential drop under those assumptions. A pressure difference measured from soil to leaf is the combined consequence of all the intervening resistances, not a direct measure of any particular organ.

For example, suppose effective resistances of the root, stem and leaf pathways, on the same leaf-area basis, are 0.08, 0.04 and 0.13 MPa per mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ respectively. Their total is 0.25 in the same units, so the conductance is 4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹. At a steady transpiration rate of 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹, the three drops are 0.16, 0.08 and 0.26 MPa. The leaf pathway contributes the largest drop despite the stem being much longer. If only root resistance doubles, the total resistance becomes 0.33, and maintaining the original flux would require a larger total drop of 0.66 MPa. Such calculations explain why roots or leaves may constrain whole-plant hydraulics even where stem xylem appears anatomically intact.

A decrease in soil water content can affect two different parts of the supply system. First, the water potential of soil often becomes more negative as it dries. Second, the hydraulic connection between soil and the absorbing root can become less effective, raising resistance. If a root draws water faster than the surrounding soil can replace it, a steep local gradient may develop near the root surface. The water potential of bulk soil collected far from the root may then differ appreciably from the potential that actually drives root uptake. This distinction explains why a measured average soil moisture content is not sufficient to describe hydraulic supply.

Leaves within one crown also experience different demands. A branch exposed to the afternoon sun may have a larger local deficit than a shaded branch supplied from the same stem. Their xylem pathways are partly shared and partly separate. Water moving through a common stem divides among leaves, so local flow rates need not equal the total flow in the trunk. The simple series equation remains useful along an individual pathway if the local flux and resistance are defined consistently, but the entire crown is better viewed as a branching hydraulic network.

Hydraulic conductance is frequently normalized by leaf area because gas-exchange rates are expressed per leaf area. If a stem transports twice as much water after the leaf area increases, that does not necessarily indicate a doubling of the supply per unit leaf area. Likewise, a plant may reduce its transpiring leaf area during water limitation and thereby reduce the load on its remaining hydraulic network. A comparison of two plants with different size therefore requires both total transport and area-normalized capacity.

Time-dependent storage adds another important qualification to the series model. Water released from a stem during the morning can supplement root-derived water, while replenishment during the evening can cause continued uptake even when leaf transpiration is relatively low. A sensor placed on the lower stem may therefore record a delayed version of canopy water loss. To estimate daily use from sap flow, measurements need calibration and integration across the relevant stem area and time interval. To identify an immediate physiological response of a leaf, an exchange chamber or aperture measurement is often more suitable than a distant flow sensor.

3. Routes of water loss and the anatomy of the leaf

The epidermis surrounds the mesophyll and usually carries a hydrophobic cuticle containing cutin and associated waxes. This barrier restricts movement of water across the epidermal surface. An entirely impermeable envelope would impair the exchange of carbon dioxide, oxygen and water vapour. Stomata solve part of that problem: each pore is controlled by two guard cells, which change shape when their turgor and wall mechanics change. The pore opens into a cavity that communicates with a network of intercellular air spaces.

Stomatal transpiration is generally the major controllable component of water loss from an actively exchanging leaf. Its magnitude varies with the number and size of stomata, the width of each open pore and the physical length of the diffusion path. Density alone is therefore not a reliable measurement of stomatal conductance. A leaf with many small stomata could have greater, similar or lower total open area than another leaf with fewer larger stomata. Measurements of actual gas exchange and aperture provide more direct evidence about the current physiological state than a count from an epidermal impression.

Cuticular transpiration persists even when stomata close strongly. Waxes do not make a biological cuticle perfectly impermeable, and the residual flux through the epidermis varies among species, leaf ages and growth conditions. In a hydrated plant with widely opened stomata the cuticular component may be relatively small. During drought, as stomatal conductance falls, it becomes a larger fraction of total loss even if the absolute cuticular flux has not changed. It is therefore unsafe to equate closed stomata with a complete absence of transpiration.

Woody stems and other organs can exchange gases through lenticels, specialized regions of relatively permeable tissue in the periderm. Their contribution to water loss depends on anatomy and exposure; they are not the principal adjustable pores of a typical photosynthesizing leaf. Hydathodes have a different function in the release of liquid water under positive pressure. Early-morning droplets along a leaf edge may reflect guttation rather than dew or transpiration. To distinguish them experimentally, establish whether liquid emerges from specific hydathodes and consider whether conditions favour root pressure or condensation.

Stomata may occur primarily on the lower epidermis, on both leaf surfaces, or predominantly on the upper epidermis in floating leaves whose lower surface contacts water. The advantages of each arrangement depend on exposure, access to atmospheric carbon dioxide and the energy environment. An amphistomatous leaf, bearing stomata on both surfaces, may support efficient exchange because it has two gas pathways. It need not lose twice as much water as a hypostomatous leaf, since the two surfaces may have different apertures, wind exposure and boundary-layer conductances.

Sunken stomata, hairs and rolled leaves can alter the local humidity and thickness of the boundary layer. Thick or wax-rich cuticles may reduce residual epidermal loss. These traits cannot be interpreted as absolute indicators of drought tolerance: leaf temperature, stomatal regulation, rooting depth and tissue tolerance also affect performance. A small leaf often exchanges heat efficiently in moving air, whereas a large leaf can maintain a thicker boundary layer at the same wind speed. Both morphology and physiological state matter when comparing transpiration among plants.

Interpreting stomatal distribution and epidermal traits

An epidermal impression can be made from a leaf surface to reveal the pattern of epidermal cells and stomata. To calculate stomatal density, count stomata in a known area, normally reporting number per square millimetre. Sampling must cover multiple positions and leaves because distribution can differ between veins, margins and central regions. If the objective is to compare the two surfaces, collect independent observations from each. Counting guard cells instead of stomatal complexes would approximately double the estimated number of pores in ordinary paired guard-cell stomata.

Stomatal index measures stomata relative to epidermal cell number. In one commonly used convention it is 100 × S/(S + C), where S is the number of stomata and C is the number of other epidermal cells within the same sampled region. The index can help distinguish a change in relative developmental allocation from a change in cell expansion. If both stomatal and epidermal cell densities fall because a leaf expands substantially without forming new cells, the stomatal index can stay similar. Conversely, a changed index suggests that the relative abundance of stomatal complexes has changed. Neither measurement tells whether pores are open at the time of gas exchange.

Suppose two hypothetical leaves have 120 and 180 stomata mm⁻². At first sight the second appears to have a greater capacity for exchange. However, if the mean open area per pore is 10 μm² in the first leaf and 4 μm² in the second, the estimated total open areas per square millimetre are 1200 and 720 μm². These represent fractions of approximately 0.0012 and 0.00072 of the projected surface, since one square millimetre contains one million square micrometres. Even total open area is not a complete predictor of conductance because pore depth and diffusion around the pore entrance affect transfer. The calculation is nevertheless sufficient to refute the claim that a larger stomatal density always produces higher gas exchange.

Leaf traits can change stomatal and boundary-layer components independently. Surface hairs may alter the air flow near the epidermis and protect pores from the direct impact of moving air. A thick waxy cuticle acts at a different position in the transport network, restricting movement through the non-stomatal surface. Reducing leaf size can increase the importance of sensible heat exchange in certain environments, which may lower the leaf-to-air temperature difference. A sunken pore can create a local diffusion path within a depression. These changes are not all interchangeable, and their effectiveness depends on humidity, radiation, wind and the state of the stomata.

A detached leaf coated with an impermeable material on one surface can be used to investigate differences in water loss between surfaces. For a hypothetical hypostomatous leaf, coating the lower surface may greatly reduce the usual stomatal route. A residual mass loss from the exposed upper surface could suggest that other pathways remain. However, the experiment is imperfect because coating alters the thermal and gas-exchange environment and the detached leaf is no longer supplied normally by the plant. A stronger design uses matched leaves, includes a handling control and independently checks stomatal distribution by microscopy. Quantitative separation of cuticular and stomatal flux demands greater care than simply subtracting two treatments.

Water loss from a closed or nearly closed leaf is sometimes described by a minimum conductance. This term should not automatically be identified with pure cuticular conductance. Some pores may fail to close completely, and structural openings, damage or measurement artefacts can contribute. When the goal is to measure cuticular permeability specifically, the method needs to distinguish the cuticle from residual stomatal leakage. In water-stressed plants, minimum conductance becomes biologically important because small losses can accumulate over many hours even after the major stomatal route has been restricted.

4. Vapour pressure, relative humidity and the evaporative driving force

Air is a mixture of gases, and water vapour contributes a partial pressure to that mixture. At a given temperature, air in equilibrium with a flat surface of pure liquid water has a saturation vapour pressure, eₛ(T). This pressure increases strongly with temperature. Relative humidity is the ratio of actual vapour pressure to saturation vapour pressure at the air temperature, multiplied by 100. Thus 50% relative humidity does not specify an absolute amount of water vapour unless the air temperature is known.

The internal air spaces of a hydrated leaf are commonly approximated as saturated at leaf temperature, although severe stress and heterogeneous microenvironments can challenge that assumption. The relevant leaf-to-air vapour pressure deficit is then D = eₛ(Tleaf) − ea, where ea = RH × eₛ(Tair)/100. The deficit is normally expressed in kPa. It measures the difference in water vapour pressure between the internal and external gas environments, not the percentage humidity itself.

At 25 °C, saturation vapour pressure is approximately 3.17 kPa. With the leaf and air at that temperature and RH of 60%, the ambient vapour pressure is 0.60 × 3.17 = 1.902 kPa. The leaf-to-air deficit is approximately 1.27 kPa. If relative humidity drops to 30% without a temperature change, the deficit becomes 2.22 kPa. Assuming unchanged gas conductance, transpiration rises in proportion to that deficit. The assumption is important because stomatal aperture can change in response to the more demanding environment.

A second example shows why measuring leaf temperature is essential. Air at 25 °C and 60% RH contains vapour at approximately 1.90 kPa. If a sunlit leaf is at 30 °C, its internal saturation vapour pressure is about 4.24 kPa. The leaf-to-air deficit is about 2.34 kPa, even though a weather instrument would report an air VPD of only 1.27 kPa. Using air temperature as a substitute for leaf temperature could seriously underestimate the driving force. Conversely, a leaf that is cooler than the air can have a smaller deficit than predicted from weather alone.

Pressure must also be considered when vapour flux is expressed using a mole fraction gradient. For dilute gas mixtures near atmospheric pressure P, the difference in water mole fraction is approximately D/P, provided D and P are in the same units. If P = 100 kPa and D = 2 kPa, the difference is 0.02 mol of water vapour per mol of air. A conductance expressed in mol m⁻² s⁻¹ is multiplied by this dimensionless gradient to obtain a molar flux in mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Multiplication by 1000 converts the flux to mmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

At zero deficit in the simplified model there is no net outward vapour flux, even if the stomata are open. A negative deficit can favour condensation rather than evaporation. Such a condition may occur when a leaf cools below the dew point of surrounding air. Distinguishing a driving force from a pathway is a general principle: opening stomata permits transport but does not by itself determine direction or guarantee a positive transpiration rate.

Humidity calculations under changing weather

Relative humidity is sometimes treated as though it measured the quantity of water in air independently of temperature. An example demonstrates why this causes errors. Air at 20 °C has a saturation vapour pressure of about 2.34 kPa, so 80% RH corresponds to an actual vapour pressure near 1.87 kPa. Air at 30 °C has a saturation vapour pressure near 4.24 kPa; 45% RH corresponds to 1.91 kPa. The second air mass has a lower relative humidity but a similar actual water vapour pressure. A leaf maintained at the same temperature in both air masses could experience nearly the same deficit, assuming comparable pressure and external conditions.

A second comparison uses equal relative humidity instead of equal vapour content. At 20 °C and 50% RH, the air VPD is approximately 1.17 kPa; at 30 °C and 50% RH, it is approximately 2.12 kPa. If leaf temperature follows air temperature and total conductance is unchanged, the warmer condition gives roughly 1.81 times the vapour flux. This predicted factor is not obtained by dividing absolute temperatures or by comparing the percentages of relative humidity. It follows from the ratio of the two vapour pressure deficits.

The assumption of saturated internal air is also connected to water potential. In thermodynamic equilibrium, the relative vapour pressure above a solution with a negative water potential is lower than saturation over pure water. For a typical hydrated leaf, the departure can be small enough for the saturated assumption to be useful in basic gas-exchange analysis. A severely dehydrated tissue may not satisfy the same approximation, and actual evaporation may occur from particular internal regions rather than from every mesophyll wall equally. A model must be judged by its intended level of accuracy.

Dew illustrates the same equilibrium from a different direction. If the air contains water vapour at 2.0 kPa while the surface cools to a temperature whose saturation pressure is 1.7 kPa, condensation is favoured at that surface. Dew on the epidermis should not be counted as liquid water released from the plant. A wet leaf may also change its surface temperature and boundary-layer humidity through evaporation of externally deposited water. Measurements made immediately after rainfall or condensation require care because water loss from a wet surface is not necessarily transpiration from internal tissue.

Atmospheric pressure enters the mole-fraction approximation because a partial pressure difference must be expressed relative to total gas pressure. Suppose two chamber experiments have the same D of 2 kPa and the same stated molar total conductance of 0.15 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, but total pressures of 100 and 80 kPa. In this deliberately simplified comparison, the predicted E values are 3.0 and 3.75 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ respectively. Actual gas conductances may also change with pressure, so the calculation should not be interpreted as a complete prediction of altitude effects. It tests whether the pressure term is used consistently in the provided model.

5. Diffusive conductance and the boundary layer

A conductance quantifies how easily a flux passes through a path under a given driving gradient. For stomatal water vapour conductance, gsw, the path is the stomatal opening. Boundary-layer conductance, gbw, describes gas transfer through the region of air next to the leaf. In a simple model where a molecule must traverse both resistances consecutively, the same flux passes through both at steady state. The total gradient is divided between them, and the resistances add: 1/gtw = 1/gsw + 1/gbw. Here gtw is total gas conductance to water vapour, and internal air-space resistance is neglected.

With gsw = 0.20 and gbw = 0.40 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, the corresponding resistances are 5 and 2.5 m² s mol⁻¹. Their sum is 7.5, giving gtw = 0.133 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. If leaf-to-air D is 1.5 kPa and P is 100 kPa, the approximate transpiration flux is 0.133 × 0.015 = 0.001995 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, or 2.0 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. If one wrongly substitutes gsw for gtw, the predicted flux becomes 3.0 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The discrepancy represents the resistance of the boundary layer, not a numerical error in the pressure deficit.

The boundary layer is a physical region rather than an anatomical covering. Air near the leaf is slowed by friction, and water vapour can accumulate within this region. Increased air movement generally thins the effective boundary layer and raises its conductance. The strongest relative effect occurs when boundary-layer resistance is a substantial part of total resistance. When stomata are already nearly closed, their resistance dominates and removing the external resistance may make little difference. Wind also changes leaf temperature, and prolonged exposure can provoke hydraulic or stomatal responses, so actual results may differ from the fixed-aperture prediction.

Stomatal and cuticular pathways are in parallel in a simplified representation because water can take either route across the epidermis. Their effective conductances add under a common gradient before considering the external boundary layer. The arrangement is different from the consecutive stomatal and boundary-layer resistances. This distinction is frequently tested: adding conductances for consecutive barriers gives a physically impossible result, namely a total conductance greater than either individual conductance.

A leaf with stomata on two surfaces requires attention to the area convention and exposure of each surface. Gas exchange is commonly expressed per projected leaf area. The two surfaces act as alternative pathways, but each has its own epidermal and boundary-layer resistance. One should calculate surface-specific fluxes with consistent area conventions and then sum them, rather than automatically doubling a one-surface conductance. In practice, gas-exchange instruments have models that account for geometry, chamber conditions and water vapour dilution.

The approximate expression E = gtw D/P provides a powerful way to reason about environmental manipulations. It assumes appropriate molar conductance units, a sufficiently small vapour mole-fraction difference, and steady conditions. More exact formulations account for effects of transpiration on bulk gas flow. For basic examination calculations, use the stated model consistently and state whether stomatal conductance alone can stand in for total conductance. It can do so only when other resistances are negligible or already incorporated into the provided conductance.

Deriving flux from gradients and identifying the limiting step

Consider two regions separated by a gas diffusion pathway. In a simple one-dimensional description, Fick's law gives a flux proportional to the concentration difference and inversely proportional to the length of the path. The proportionality depends on a diffusion coefficient, which describes molecular transport in the medium. If a chamber or leaf surface provides a larger cross-sectional area, more molecules can pass at the same gradient. Gas conductance combines the relevant geometry, transport properties and chosen gradient convention. The conductance used in leaf physiology is often expressed using a mole-fraction gradient rather than a concentration gradient, so its units must be matched to that definition.

To derive the series expression, designate the vapour mole fractions at the leaf interior, at the external edge of the stomatal pathway and in the bulk air as χi, χs and χa. At steady state the same flux E passes through both paths, so E = gsw(χi − χs) and E = gbw(χs − χa). Rearranging gives χi − χs = E/gsw and χs − χa = E/gbw. Adding the differences cancels the intermediate mole fraction, yielding χi − χa = E(1/gsw + 1/gbw). The effective total conductance is therefore the inverse of the sum of the resistances. This result does not require the two conductances to be equal.

A limiting case makes the derivation intuitive. If the boundary layer is extremely thin and its conductance is very large, its reciprocal approaches zero, so gtw approaches gsw. If stomata are wide open and gsw greatly exceeds gbw, total conductance approaches gbw. The smaller conductance can dominate the overall transfer, but neither pathway necessarily has zero influence. The limiting-step language describes relative importance rather than an absolute exclusion of all other resistances.

A numerical comparison shows why increasing wind can have different effects in hydrated and stressed leaves. In a well-hydrated illustrative leaf, gsw = 0.40 and gbw rises from 0.10 to 0.40 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Total conductance changes from 0.08 to 0.20, a 2.5-fold increase. In a stressed leaf with gsw = 0.02, the same change in gbw increases total conductance from about 0.0167 to 0.0190, only about 14%. At the same deficit, these factors are also the predicted changes in E. The example isolates the physics of resistance; it does not assume that real wind treatments leave aperture and leaf temperature unchanged.

Parallel transport can be derived in an analogous way. If stomatal and cuticular pathways experience an approximately common internal-to-surface gradient, their individual fluxes are gswΔχ and gcwΔχ. Summing fluxes gives an epidermal conductance of gsw + gcw. If the cuticular conductance is 0.005 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ and the stomatal conductance falls from 0.20 to 0.005, the cuticular share of epidermal conductance rises from about 2.4% to 50%. Absolute cuticular permeability has not changed in the calculation. The increase in relative contribution is a consequence of the shrinking stomatal component.

When comparing carbon dioxide and water vapour transfer through stomata, conductances differ because the gases have different diffusivities in air. In commonly used leaf gas-exchange approximations, stomatal conductance to water vapour is about 1.6 times stomatal conductance to carbon dioxide, with the pore geometry held constant. A stomatal water-vapour conductance of 0.24 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ thus corresponds to an approximate stomatal carbon dioxide conductance of 0.15 in the same units. This conversion is relevant to stomatal diffusion; additional resistances to carbon dioxide within the mesophyll are not eliminated by applying it.

It is tempting to reason that opening a pore must increase photosynthesis and transpiration by the same factor because both gases pass through it. That inference ignores carbon fixation, internal carbon dioxide concentration, biochemical limitations and the different driving gradients. A leaf limited by photosynthetic biochemistry may not greatly increase assimilation when stomata open, even if water loss rises. A low internal carbon dioxide concentration can increase the gradient for inward diffusion, while the water-vapour gradient is largely determined by temperature and humidity. The shared pathway creates a trade-off, not a compulsory equality between the two fluxes.

6. Guard cells: converting membrane transport into pore movement

Stomatal aperture is controlled by the mechanics of paired guard cells. In many dicots the guard cells are kidney-shaped, with walls that differ in thickness and mechanical properties around the pore. Increasing internal pressure changes their geometry so the pore widens. Grasses commonly have dumbbell-shaped guard cells associated with subsidiary cells, which participate in their mechanical and osmotic behaviour. A change in turgor is thus converted into an opening movement by cell geometry and the properties of the cell wall, rather than by a muscular contraction.

An important pathway for opening begins with activation of the plasma membrane proton ATPase. The enzyme exports H⁺ using ATP, making the cell interior electrically more negative. This hyperpolarization can favour K⁺ entry through inward-rectifying potassium channels when the potassium electrochemical gradient permits influx. Anions such as chloride and malate provide countercharge and contribute to osmotic solute accumulation; sugars may also contribute. Solutes stored in the cytoplasm and vacuole lower the osmotic potential of the guard cell. Water enters along a water-potential gradient, turgor rises, and the pore widens.

An increase in turgor does not mean that the guard cell permanently has a much lower total water potential than its surroundings. As water enters, its positive pressure potential rises and offsets the negative change in osmotic potential. Water movement approaches equilibrium when the relevant water potentials converge. The maintained aperture reflects the new osmotic and mechanical state, not continuous unrestricted water uptake. This relationship between osmotic and pressure potentials links stomatal control directly to the earlier water-potential principles.

During closure, changes in signalling favour solute release. Abscisic acid, particularly under water-deficit conditions, can activate anion channels such as SLAC1 through a regulatory pathway. Anion efflux helps depolarize the plasma membrane; outward potassium channels can then promote K⁺ loss. A decrease in osmotic solute content raises the guard-cell osmotic potential, and water exits as the balance of water potential changes. Turgor falls and the pore narrows. Vacuolar transport and membrane water permeability participate in this response. Detailed ABA receptor signalling and blue-light perception belong to the hormone and sensory photobiology chapters; here their transport consequences explain the aperture change.

ABA is not the only influence on the pore. Blue light often promotes opening, carbon dioxide concentration affects stomatal behaviour, and circadian regulation can alter sensitivity over a daily cycle. A high atmospheric demand for water may lead to hydraulic and biochemical feedback. A rapid change in epidermal and guard-cell turgor can even produce a transient response before active regulation dominates. Consequently, the simple phrase “water loss causes stomata to close” should be interpreted as a common regulated outcome, not a universal instantaneous mechanical law.

A useful experimental design compares normal and mutant plants whose guard cells have impaired anion-channel activation. After supplying ABA under standardized light, humidity and leaf-water conditions, measure the change in conductance over time. A mutant with impaired ABA-induced anion efflux may show slower or less complete closure. That result supports a role for the channel in the signalling pathway, but it does not prove that the channel is the sole determinant of aperture. Complementation, ion-current recordings, measurements of starting aperture and viability controls strengthen the interpretation. The link between molecular activity and whole-leaf flux requires both levels of evidence.

Solute balance, voltage and mechanistic experiments

The osmotic consequences of solute uptake can be estimated using the dilute-solution van't Hoff expression Ψs ≈ −RTC, where C denotes total osmotic particle concentration, T absolute temperature and R the gas constant in compatible units. At 298 K, RT is approximately 2.48 MPa L mol⁻¹ when concentration is expressed in mol L⁻¹. An increase of 0.10 mol L⁻¹ in effective osmotic particle concentration would lower osmotic potential by about 0.248 MPa, before considering changes in volume, non-ideal behaviour and pressure. This illustrates how an apparently modest solute change can generate a substantial osmotic effect.

An ion contributes to osmotic concentration as a particle, but a cell cannot accumulate arbitrarily large net electrical charge in its bulk solution. The plasma membrane can maintain a voltage difference with a small separation of charge; the bulk cytoplasmic solutions remain approximately electrically neutral. A potassium increase therefore requires balancing charge, often through accumulation of counterions or changes in metabolic products. If 50 mmol L⁻¹ of K⁺ and 50 mmol L⁻¹ of a monovalent counterion are accumulated, the ideal osmotic particle concentration rises by approximately 100 mmol L⁻¹, not merely 50. For a divalent counterion, the relationship between charge equivalents and particle number is different. In real guard cells, compartmentation and water influx modify the concentration change.

The proton ATPase provides an electrical and chemical driving force for ion movement. Pumping positive charge out can make the interior more negative, but whether K⁺ enters through a particular open channel depends on its electrochemical potential difference. A channel itself is not an ATP-driven pump merely because ATP was used earlier to establish the voltage. Blocking ATPase activity can indirectly reduce ion uptake and aperture even if the channel protein remains functional. This distinction between direct energy coupling and secondary dependence is a recurring principle in plant membrane transport.

Ion-channel recordings can provide evidence at a finer level than aperture observations. If an isolated guard cell exhibits an increased outward current upon a treatment, the current must be interpreted with the ion gradients, voltage convention and identity of the channel in mind. An outward positive current can represent positive ions leaving or negative ions entering, whereas anion efflux represents outward movement of negative charge. A current trace alone therefore does not identify the chemical species. Ion substitution, reversal potential, channel-selective perturbation and genetic evidence can help identify the transport process.

During closure, water leaves the guard cell as osmotic and pressure conditions shift. A small change in turgor can produce different aperture responses depending on wall geometry and the mechanical constraints imposed by surrounding epidermal cells. A plant with impaired guard-cell wall mechanics may accumulate ions normally yet open its pores less efficiently. Measuring only ion accumulation in such a mutant would not reveal the mechanical defect. Conversely, a normal aperture under one condition does not prove normal transport regulation across all environmental conditions.

A comparison between isolated epidermis and an intact leaf helps distinguish autonomous guard-cell behaviour from regulation influenced by neighbouring tissues. If a light treatment induces opening in the isolated epidermis, the epidermal preparation contains mechanisms sufficient to respond under those particular conditions. It does not prove that mesophyll-derived signals never affect opening in an intact plant. If the intact leaf responds differently, possible explanations include carbon dioxide changes, hydraulic constraints, endogenous signals or the altered environment of the isolated tissue. These distinctions prevent overinterpretation of simplified experimental preparations.

7. Environmental regulation: physical forcing and physiological feedback

Light influences transpiration through at least two pathways. It supplies energy that can warm the leaf and promote evaporation, and it alters stomatal behaviour, both through guard-cell photoreception and through the demand for carbon dioxide during photosynthesis. When a dark-adapted leaf is illuminated, stomatal conductance may increase gradually rather than instantaneously. In a short illumination experiment, a measured rise in transpiration could reflect a change in leaf temperature before a large aperture change. To separate these possibilities, measure both leaf temperature and conductance.

Humidity modifies the external vapour pressure. At fixed temperature, lower relative humidity increases the gradient between the moist leaf interior and the atmosphere. This change tends to increase transpiration at unchanged conductance. At longer times, stomatal closure often limits the increase, especially if the supply of liquid water cannot meet the demand. The net relationship between E and VPD is therefore not necessarily linear. A curve may initially rise steeply, flatten or even decline at high VPD, depending on species and conditions.

Temperature changes saturation vapour pressure, metabolic processes and the energy balance. A warmer leaf can have a higher internal vapour pressure, but warming beyond the physiological optimum may restrict photosynthesis or induce stress responses. Temperature also affects the viscosity of liquid water, although it is usually unnecessary to include this in basic leaf gas-exchange calculations. When air temperature increases while RH remains constant, the absolute vapour deficit often rises because saturation pressure is higher. When the water vapour pressure itself remains constant, the relative humidity instead falls as the air warms.

Wind reduces aerodynamic resistance around a leaf, yet its effect on transpiration has no single universal sign in a changing environment. At constant leaf temperature and stomatal conductance, increased boundary-layer conductance increases vapour flux. In sunlight, wind can also cool a warm leaf by enhancing sensible heat transfer, decreasing the deficit. If increased water loss reduces leaf water potential, stomata may close. To interpret a wind experiment, distinguish the immediate physical effect from subsequent changes in temperature and aperture.

Carbon dioxide influences the benefits of aperture. Elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide can permit the same or greater carbon fixation at a smaller stomatal opening in some plants, but response magnitude varies with biochemical capacity and acclimation. High carbon dioxide often promotes closure in C3 plants; low internal carbon dioxide can favour opening. This relationship does not establish a universal quantitative response to every CO₂ treatment. Other environmental factors may be simultaneously limiting photosynthesis.

Soil drying limits the hydraulic supply and can induce ABA-dependent and hydraulic regulation of stomata. A well-watered soil does not guarantee high leaf water potential if atmospheric demand is excessive or xylem transport is impaired. Conversely, reduced transpiration during a period of soil drying may protect leaf water status temporarily. Comparing leaf water potential, soil water potential and stomatal conductance allows a student to distinguish a supply-side restriction from a purely atmospheric increase in evaporative demand.

Separating short-term responses, acclimation and developmental change

Stomatal regulation operates at several time scales. Existing pores can change aperture over seconds to minutes or longer, depending on the stimulus, plant and initial state. Over hours, changes in hydraulic status and metabolism can influence the operating point of the leaf. During the development of new leaves, plants may alter epidermal cell size, stomatal number or other structural characteristics in response to their growth conditions. These are not alternative names for the same process. Aperture is a property of an existing pore, whereas stomatal density reflects the number of pores relative to surface area and cannot generally be changed rapidly by opening or closing them.

A common source of confusion is the comparison of short-term gas-exchange experiments with long-term growth experiments. Imagine plants initially raised under identical conditions and transferred to dry air. A measurement ten minutes later tests rapid responses in existing leaves. A second measurement after several weeks may include acclimation and development of new leaves. If the long-term plants have lower stomatal density, the immediate reduction in transpiration in the first experiment cannot be attributed to that developmental change. It occurred before new epidermal structures could develop. Distinguishing the duration of treatment is therefore essential when interpreting causal explanations.

Daily cycles provide another important example. A morning leaf may have relatively favourable water status and low atmospheric demand. As sunlight increases, photosynthetic activity and conductance may rise. At midday, higher temperature and atmospheric demand can reduce aperture even when light remains abundant. In some species this produces a midday depression in photosynthesis. A late-afternoon recovery is possible if the evaporative demand falls and water supply permits rehydration. These patterns vary with species, soil moisture, canopy position and weather; a midday decline should not be assumed to occur in every plant.

Consider an illustrative daily series in which incident light is high at noon, but assimilation falls from its morning value. Several explanations are possible. Stomatal restriction may limit carbon dioxide entry, biochemical activity may be affected by heat, or another photosynthetic limitation may dominate. A simultaneous decrease in stomatal conductance makes diffusion limitation plausible but does not establish that it is the only cause. Measurements of internal carbon dioxide, leaf temperature and photosynthetic responses to controlled external carbon dioxide can help discriminate among alternatives. The examination skill is to infer what the existing observations support and identify what further evidence is needed.

Circadian regulation may influence stomatal behaviour even if an environmental stimulus is held approximately constant. A circadian rhythm is not established merely by observing a daily pattern outdoors, because light, humidity and temperature cycle simultaneously. To test for endogenous regulation, plants can first be entrained under a regular light–dark regime and then monitored in approximately constant environmental conditions. Persistence of a near-daily pattern would support an endogenous component. The response may change in amplitude or phase, and prolonged constant conditions may alter photosynthetic or water status. Such controls are necessary when separating the internal biological clock from externally driven periodicity.

The effects of light quality can also be separated from total light energy. Guard cells may respond to a small blue-light component superimposed on red background illumination, whereas changes in the red background can influence mesophyll photosynthesis and internal carbon dioxide. If a comparison changes both light spectrum and total irradiance, the experiment cannot identify which variable caused the difference. Keeping the background illumination and environmental conditions stable makes the result easier to interpret. Detailed phototropin signalling belongs to the sensory photobiology node, but the transport consequence is relevant here: a spectral response that changes conductance may change water loss even when the additional photosynthetic energy supplied is relatively small.

Nocturnal water loss is particularly useful for correcting overly simple diagrams of daily regulation. Many non-CAM plants maintain some night-time stomatal conductance, and the cuticular pathway can persist during darkness. Whether that loss is substantial depends on aperture, leaf-to-air vapour deficit and the length of the night. A daytime-only gas-exchange measurement may therefore underestimate daily water use. The presence of night-time transpiration does not prove that the plant uses CAM photosynthesis, which is defined by its carbon fixation and storage physiology rather than by water loss alone.

8. Whole-plant water balance, cooling and hydraulic limits

For a plant considered as a water-storage system over a defined period, the change in water content equals uptake minus loss, with additional terms included if important. In a simple short-term model, dW/dt = U − T, where U is root uptake and T is transpiration, both expressed as mass per unit time. Guttation, growth-associated storage and other exchange terms can matter depending on the experiment. If uptake is 8 g h⁻¹ and transpiration is 11 g h⁻¹ for two hours, stored water declines by 6 g under the constant-rate approximation.

A stable leaf water potential can be maintained only when water loss is compatible with supply and storage. An increase in transpiration may steepen the liquid-phase potential gradient, but greater demand can cause excessive tension and increase the risk of xylem embolism. Embolism blocks conduits with gas and reduces hydraulic conductance. Stomatal restriction of vapour loss can therefore protect the plant before hydraulic transport becomes severely impaired. The vulnerability of xylem and the ability to regulate stomata are linked, but they are not the same trait.

Evaporation requires latent heat. At ordinary leaf temperatures, the latent heat of vaporization is approximately 44 kJ per mol of water, with temperature dependence. A transpiration flux of 4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ corresponds to 0.004 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Multiplying by 44,000 J mol⁻¹ gives a latent heat flux of approximately 176 W m⁻². This is a substantial pathway of energy transfer from a leaf. If stomata close and other fluxes remain unchanged initially, less energy is removed by evaporation and the leaf may warm. The eventual temperature depends on the entire energy balance, including radiation and sensible heat transfer.

Transpiration also influences the movement of minerals in xylem. At a fixed solute concentration, an increase in volumetric sap flow carries a larger amount of dissolved material per unit time. Yet nutrient acquisition involves selective transport at the root, nutrient availability, retention and redistribution. A higher transpiration rate does not necessarily increase the tissue concentration of every mineral. Calcium, for instance, is relatively poorly remobilized through the phloem in many plants, so tissues that transpire little may receive inadequate calcium even when roots acquire substantial amounts.

Whole-plant water balance is shaped by leaf area as well as by flux density. Two plants can have the same E per square metre but very different daily water use because their transpiring areas differ. Canopy shading and humidity also cause heterogeneous exchange across leaves. Multiplying one measured sunlit-leaf rate by total leaf area can be misleading unless it is a reasonable average. A time integral of exchange over leaves and over the day provides a more meaningful description of whole-plant consumption.

The leaf energy budget and feedback between heat and water

Energy arriving at a leaf is partitioned among several processes. Absorbed radiation can increase temperature, contribute to sensible heat exchange with air, drive evaporation, and support photosynthetic chemical energy storage. The relative importance of these terms varies with conditions. In a simplified steady leaf energy balance, net radiation is approximately balanced by sensible heat and latent heat, with additional storage and metabolic terms neglected. The simplification is useful for explaining why two leaves exposed to the same light can attain different temperatures.

Sensible heat is heat transfer associated with temperature differences rather than a change of phase. If a leaf is warmer than surrounding air, sensible heat commonly flows from the leaf to the air; if it is cooler, the direction can reverse. Latent heat is associated with evaporation or condensation. Evaporating water carries energy away from the leaf even when its temperature is below air temperature. As a result, a well-watered transpiring leaf can be cooler than nearby air under appropriate conditions. It is not necessary to imagine that the xylem delivers water colder than the air in order to explain evaporative cooling.

For an illustrative energy calculation, suppose a leaf receives net radiation of 300 W m⁻², transpiration is 4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and the latent heat of evaporation is 44 kJ mol⁻¹. Latent heat transfer is about 176 W m⁻². If other energy terms are negligible and conditions are steady, the remaining 124 W m⁻² is transferred as sensible heat. If stomatal closure reduces E to 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹, latent heat becomes about 88 W m⁻². At unchanged net radiation, the sensible heat term would need to rise to approximately 212 W m⁻² for the new steady balance. In reality leaf temperature and radiation exchange may adjust, so the difference in energy flux should not be converted directly into a temperature change without knowing thermal conductance.

This relationship creates a feedback loop. If the pore closes, evaporation decreases, which may permit warming. The resulting increase in internal saturation vapour pressure increases leaf-to-air VPD, potentially offsetting part of the initial reduction in water loss. A calculation that holds leaf temperature fixed describes one experimentally controlled condition; a freely exposed leaf can respond differently. Similarly, a fan that cools a warm leaf can reduce the driving force while increasing boundary-layer conductance. The combination of energy and mass balance explains many apparently conflicting observations about temperature and wind.

Thermal imaging can reveal temperature differences between leaves or parts of a canopy. Under comparable radiation, wind and water supply, a warmer region may indicate less evaporative cooling and thus lower transpiration. It is not by itself a direct stomatal conductance measurement. Leaf angle alters absorbed radiation; colour and surface properties affect radiative exchange; air flow varies among canopy positions. A suitable comparison uses matched tissues and controls environmental factors or includes reference surfaces and simultaneous gas-exchange measurements. A thermal image becomes much more informative when interpreted as evidence within an energy-balance model rather than as a direct image of stomatal aperture.

Water limitation also interacts with heat stress. On a hot day, an irrigated plant can sustain transpiration and evaporative cooling if its hydraulic capacity allows. A water-limited plant may close stomata, reducing water loss but increasing its exposure to elevated leaf temperatures. In this situation, preserving water and dissipating heat are competing goals. Neither high nor low conductance guarantees better performance. The relevant balance depends on the availability of water, the capacity for transport, heat sensitivity of photosynthetic and cellular processes, and the expected duration of the environmental stress.

The following schematic separates the energy and water budgets. The water budget is expressed in mass or moles per unit time; the energy budget is expressed in joules per unit time. They are linked by latent heat of evaporation. Converting between them requires multiplication by the appropriate latent heat per mass or per mole. Confusing watts with grams of water per hour is a dimensional error, even if both variables respond to the same environmental change.

Mineral delivery, root pressure and changes in tissue water content

The transpiration stream carries solutes in liquid water, and the rate of delivery of a dissolved substance is the product of its concentration and the volume flow. Suppose xylem sap contains 1.5 mmol L⁻¹ of a mineral ion and flows into a leaf at 0.02 L h⁻¹. The delivery rate is 0.03 mmol h⁻¹. If volume flow doubles while concentration falls by half, the delivery rate remains the same. It is therefore insufficient to report increased water flow without measuring concentration if the aim is to infer mineral delivery.

This calculation also clarifies the distinction between supply to a tissue and incorporation into cells. The xylem may deliver a solute to a leaf, but entry into a cell, binding, sequestration and metabolic use depend on other processes. A mineral with relatively limited phloem mobility may accumulate preferentially in strongly transpiring organs. A developing fruit with low transpiration can face a local calcium supply limitation even if mature leaves receive substantial calcium. Such an example links organ water relations with nutrient distribution without replacing the detailed study of root transport and assimilation.

Guttation provides a contrasting case of water movement. When transpiration is low and positive pressure develops in xylem, liquid can emerge through hydathodes near leaf margins or tips. Because that water has travelled through tissues, it may contain dissolved solutes. In contrast, dew condenses from atmospheric water vapour on surfaces that are sufficiently cool. Finding droplets alone is not proof of root pressure; the location, conditions and source of the droplets matter. In a gravimetric water balance, both transpiration and guttation can reduce plant water content, but only the first is evaporation from the plant in the usual definition.

A plant can maintain leaf water content despite a large flow through it. In that steady state, the volume entering and leaving is approximately equal even though individual water molecules are continuously replaced. Tissue hydration is therefore a stock variable, whereas transpiration and uptake are flux variables. A low water content does not necessarily indicate a large current loss: a severely dehydrated leaf may have closed stomata and low transpiration. Conversely, a highly hydrated leaf can sustain a large transpiration flux if the supply is sufficient.

Relative water content is one useful measure of tissue hydration. A commonly used expression is 100 × (fresh mass − dry mass)/(fully turgid mass − dry mass). The numerator estimates water currently in the sample and the denominator estimates the water content when fully hydrated under the chosen measurement procedure. Suppose a leaf sample has fresh mass 0.80 g, turgid mass 1.00 g and dry mass 0.20 g. Its relative water content is 100 × 0.60/0.80 = 75%. The measure is not identical to water potential: two tissues at the same relative water content can differ in solute concentration and mechanical properties.

When comparing treatments, ensure that mass measurements are made consistently. The fully turgid mass can be influenced by incomplete rehydration or changes during sample handling, while dry mass must be measured after adequate drying. External droplets should not inflate the fresh mass. Relative water content provides useful complementary evidence alongside leaf water potential and stomatal conductance. If a treatment lowers conductance but preserves relative water content during a dry period, that is consistent with reduced water loss protecting hydration, although root supply and other differences must also be considered.

A whole-plant comparison may use a covered-pot experiment over several days. At the start, the soil water content and pot mass are standardized. Irrigation additions and drainage are recorded, soil evaporation is estimated using appropriate controls, and mass loss is monitored. Leaves are measured at representative times for conductance and water status. Combining these data helps reveal whether a treatment conserves soil water, changes leaf hydration or simply reduces plant size. A plant with a smaller canopy can use less water in total without having lower transpiration per unit leaf area.

9. Water-use efficiency and C3, C4 and CAM strategies

Water-use efficiency expresses a ratio of carbon gain or biomass production to some measure of water use. At the leaf level, instantaneous photosynthetic water-use efficiency is A/E, where A is net carbon dioxide assimilation and E is transpiration. If A is 15 μmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹ and E is 3 mmol H₂O m⁻² s⁻¹, the ratio is 5 μmol CO₂ per mmol H₂O, equivalent to 5 mmol CO₂ per mol H₂O. Equal ratios do not imply equal carbon gain or equal water loss individually.

Intrinsic water-use efficiency is commonly defined as A/gsw, where gsw is stomatal conductance to water vapour. Using A = 15 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and gsw = 0.20 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ gives 75 μmol CO₂ per mol H₂O. This is not numerically interchangeable with A/E because conductance is not a water flux. A/E depends directly on the vapour gradient as well as on the conductance; A/gsw is useful for comparing carbon gain relative to the stomatal pathway, though it remains dependent on physiological and environmental conditions.

Suppose A and gsw are temporarily unchanged while VPD doubles. With negligible boundary-layer resistance, E approximately doubles, so A/E halves while A/gsw remains unchanged. A student who calls both ratios simply “water-use efficiency” could mistakenly conclude that the two measurements contradict one another. The calculation reveals that each ratio measures a different aspect of the carbon–water trade-off.

Over longer intervals, whole-plant biomass water-use efficiency relates dry biomass accumulation to cumulative water use. Some agricultural measures instead relate harvestable yield to irrigation supplied or evapotranspiration. These denominators include different processes and may account for soil evaporation, drainage or unproductive water loss. A physiological improvement in leaf A/gsw does not guarantee increased crop yield or reduced total irrigation if plant growth, leaf area or season length changes.

C4 photosynthesis concentrates carbon dioxide around Rubisco, allowing many C4 plants to attain high assimilation at lower stomatal conductance than comparable C3 plants in favourable warm, bright conditions. This can improve leaf-level water-use efficiency, although C4 metabolism has an additional energetic cost and the comparison depends on environment. CAM plants often open stomata at night and store carbon temporarily as organic acids. At night, lower temperature and often higher relative humidity reduce the evaporative driving force. Daytime decarboxylation supplies carbon dioxide internally while stomata are relatively closed. CAM is not a universal pattern of complete nocturnal opening and zero daytime exchange; the extent and timing of its phases depend on species and conditions.

Comparisons of water-saving strategies should distinguish drought avoidance from tolerance and productivity. Closing stomata early can conserve soil water for later growth but may sacrifice immediate assimilation. Keeping stomata open longer can favour photosynthesis when water is available but increase hydraulic risk under sustained drought. The best strategy depends on duration and predictability of water limitation. The appropriate examination response explains this conditional trade-off rather than assigning an absolute ranking to all photosynthetic pathways or plant types.

Carbon diffusion and the meaning of efficiency ratios

The inward diffusion of carbon dioxide depends on the difference between atmospheric carbon dioxide mole fraction and the internal intercellular mole fraction. If external boundary resistance is negligible and net assimilation is considered at steady state, an introductory approximation is A = gsc(Ca − Ci), with compatible molar units. Here gsc is stomatal conductance to carbon dioxide. A carbon dioxide mole-fraction difference of 100 μmol mol⁻¹ and conductance of 0.15 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ give an assimilation flux of 15 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The example treats the stomatal pathway explicitly; movement from internal air spaces to chloroplasts and biochemical fixation determine the internal concentration and can impose additional limitations.

If gsw is approximately 1.6gsc, the intrinsic ratio A/gsw in this simplified diffusion model becomes approximately (Ca − Ci)/1.6. The expression explains why intrinsic water-use efficiency is associated with the drawdown of carbon dioxide across the stomatal pathway. A larger drawdown can reflect effective biochemical consumption relative to stomatal supply. But an increase in drawdown is not always desirable: very low Ci can indicate carbon starvation at restricted aperture. To interpret a ratio, examine assimilation and conductance separately as well as the environmental conditions.

Suppose leaf X has A = 18 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and gsw = 0.30 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Leaf Y has A = 12 and gsw = 0.15 in corresponding units. Intrinsic efficiency rises from 60 to 80 μmol mol⁻¹ in leaf Y, yet its assimilation is one-third lower. If the objective is rapid carbon gain with sufficient water, leaf X might be favourable. If water is limited over an extended period, leaf Y might preserve water for future activity. The ratio cannot alone select the better ecological or agricultural strategy.

The difference between leaf and whole-plant efficiency is also a question of integration. Leaf A/E is a ratio at one time and location. Over a full day, one should integrate total carbon gain and total water loss separately and then form the ratio. The mean of several instantaneous ratios generally differs from the ratio of total fluxes. For example, one interval with A = 10 and E = 1, followed by an equal-duration interval with A = 10 and E = 4, gives instantaneous ratios of 10 and 2.5 in consistent units. Their arithmetic average is 6.25, whereas the ratio of integrated totals is 20/5 = 4. This is a weighted average problem: the interval with greater water flux contributes more to total water consumption.

Net carbon gain over long durations is not identical to the sum of daytime leaf assimilation values because respiratory losses, allocation and turnover of tissues affect retained biomass. Whole-plant biomass water-use efficiency can therefore differ even when two plants have similar daytime gas exchange. For agricultural comparisons, soil evaporation, interception of rain, and irrigation or drainage losses may enter the denominator depending on how efficiency is defined. Crop yield per unit evapotranspiration and yield per unit irrigation applied are different quantities and need not move together.

A comparison of CAM and conventional daytime carbon fixation illustrates temporal integration. Under a simplified night-time deficit of 0.7 kPa and a daytime deficit of 2.8 kPa, the same total conductance would give a fourfold higher vapour flux by day. Moving atmospheric carbon acquisition to the night can thus reduce the cost of gas exchange. But the capacity to store organic acids, the availability of metabolic energy and the amount of carbon fixed over the day also matter. CAM is not a guarantee of high absolute growth rate, nor does its water-saving property imply that it is superior under every environment.

For C4 plants, the carbon-concentrating mechanism can support effective carboxylation even when stomatal conductance is relatively low, especially in environments favouring C4 metabolism. A fair comparison with C3 plants controls irradiance, temperature, CO₂ concentration and leaf age. The difference cannot be attributed solely to stomatal density or directly inferred from the photosynthetic pathway name. Because transpiration depends on D as well as conductance, comparing leaves measured under different vapour deficits can obscure the contribution of carbon-concentrating physiology.

The efficiency terminology should be retained throughout the examination. If a problem gives A and E, compute instantaneous photosynthetic WUE. If it gives A and gsw, compute intrinsic WUE. If it gives dry mass and cumulative water consumption, use the biomass ratio. Do not introduce an arbitrary conversion to compare numerical values with different denominators. When a question asks for improved water use, first determine whether it means less water consumed, more carbon gained per unit water, more yield per irrigation, or survival through a dry period. These goals overlap but are not equivalent.

10. Measurement methods and experimental reasoning

A bubble potometer estimates uptake of water by a cut shoot. As the shoot takes up water, an air bubble in a calibrated capillary moves. For a uniform circular capillary, the displaced volume is the cross-sectional area multiplied by the distance travelled. If the bore radius is 0.4 mm and the bubble moves 30 mm in five minutes, the displaced volume is π × 0.4² × 30 = approximately 15.1 mm³. Since 1 mm³ equals 1 μL, the uptake rate is approximately 3.02 μL min⁻¹. To compare shoots with different leaf areas, divide by the measured area.

The instrument does not directly measure water vapour leaving a leaf. At steady water content, uptake may approximate transpiration, but storage, tissue expansion, leakage and the physiological effects of cutting can create differences. Cutting a shoot under water helps avoid air entry, and a watertight apparatus is required. A control without a transpiring shoot can reveal leakage or temperature-related bubble displacement. Allow the plant to acclimate to each experimental treatment and avoid interpreting a single short measurement as a universal rate.

A gravimetric method estimates water loss from a change in mass. An intact potted plant can be weighed at the beginning and end of a measurement interval. Covering the soil and pot surface reduces evaporation not attributable to the plant; a similarly covered soil-only control can estimate remaining non-plant loss. For instance, a plant system loses 18 g over two hours while the control loses 2 g. The corrected rate is 8 g h⁻¹. Over long experiments, irrigation, drainage and carbon-related mass changes may require additional accounting.

A porometer measures leaf conductance by analysing exchange between a leaf and a chamber whose humidity conditions are known. It is especially useful for comparing stomatal behaviour, but the device model, calibration, equilibration and leaf surface sampled influence the result. A conductance measurement alone cannot determine E without the vapour driving force and the appropriate total conductance. Rapid changes in environment during measurement may also mean that the measured state differs from the original field state.

An open gas-exchange system supplies air of known composition to a chamber enclosing part of a leaf. Gas analysers compare incoming and outgoing water vapour and carbon dioxide concentrations. With flow rate and leaf area, these differences can be used to calculate water and carbon fluxes, applying necessary corrections for changing gas composition. For a simple teaching calculation, suppose 0.0005 mol air s⁻¹ passes a leaf area of 0.0005 m² and the outlet water mole fraction exceeds the inlet by 0.003. Neglecting dilution corrections, E = 0.003 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, or 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. A real instrument applies the appropriate mass-balance equations, and inaccurate leaf area or chamber leakage will bias the result.

A microscopy-based aperture measurement gives a structural observation. It can show whether individual stomata have widened, but it does not by itself measure the aggregate gas flux. Combining epidermal images, porometry and gas exchange makes it possible to test whether a treatment acts on aperture, the physical driving force or both. Sampling multiple leaves from multiple plants is important: repeated measurements on one leaf are not independent biological replicates.

A particularly informative VPD experiment uses attached leaves at constant irradiance, air carbon dioxide and controlled leaf temperature. Vary humidity in steps, measure E and gsw after a specified equilibration period, and record leaf water potential where feasible. An initial increase in E accompanied by a decrease in gsw demonstrates that the physical driving force and stomatal response are acting in opposing directions. A later decrease in E at high VPD suggests that the reduction in effective conductance outweighs the increased deficit. Without conductance and leaf temperature data, the same E pattern has several possible explanations. This is why multiple measurements and controlled variables are central to Part C reasoning.

Analysing an illustrative controlled dataset

Suppose a researcher measures gas exchange on attached leaves at constant illumination and 100 kPa atmospheric pressure. The chamber controls leaf temperature and supplies the same carbon dioxide concentration throughout. Boundary-layer conductance is assumed high enough to neglect for this example. At VPD values of 1, 2 and 3 kPa, the measured stomatal conductances are 0.30, 0.22 and 0.12 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ respectively. Predicted transpiration rates from the simplified formula are 3.0, 4.4 and 3.6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. This dataset is invented for teaching and is not a published experiment.

The response illustrates that E can initially rise even as stomatal conductance falls. Between the first and second treatments, the deficit doubles while conductance declines to about 73% of its original value. The product rises to about 147% of its original value. Between the second and third treatments the deficit rises by 50%, but conductance drops to about 55% of the earlier value; the product falls to approximately 82%. The increase in atmospheric demand is outweighed by the reduction in the pathway's conductance.

If assimilation rates are 18, 15 and 9 μmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹, instantaneous water-use efficiencies are 6.0, about 3.41 and 2.5 μmol CO₂ per mmol H₂O. Intrinsic water-use efficiencies are 60, approximately 68.2 and 75 μmol CO₂ per mol H₂O. The two measures move in different directions because E contains the vapour deficit and stomatal conductance does not. Carbon assimilation declines, so the rising intrinsic ratio does not mean carbon productivity is increasing.

Treatments must be randomized or appropriately ordered to avoid mistaking gradual dehydration over the experiment for an immediate response to each VPD step.

Repeated measurements also require interpretation of uncertainty. Three leaves from one plant provide information about variation within that plant but do not constitute three fully independent plants. A biological replicate is an independently sampled experimental organism or relevant independently treated unit. If the chamber is used in one fixed sequence, carryover effects may occur; a return to the initial VPD can test reversibility. Slow recovery could indicate that the leaf's water status or regulation has not returned to baseline. The shape of a response curve alone does not reveal the cause of hysteresis.

Choosing a measurement that can answer the hypothesis

A rigorous experiment begins with an operational definition of the quantity to be measured. For the hypothesis that wind increases transpiration by reducing the external boundary resistance, a treatment that changes air speed is appropriate. However, the prediction assumes constant aperture and leaf temperature. A chamber with controlled leaf temperature, together with conductance measurements and known boundary-layer conditions, allows those assumptions to be examined. A potometer without measurements of temperature and conductance can demonstrate a change in water uptake but cannot by itself determine why it occurred.

If the hypothesis instead concerns stomatal responses to ABA, the appropriate dependent variable could be conductance or aperture as a function of time. A solvent control is necessary when applying ABA in a vehicle that may influence the tissue. Comparing treated and untreated leaves at the same initial hydration and light conditions reduces confounding. If the treatment is applied to an isolated epidermis, the result tests behaviour in that preparation; if applied to an intact plant, transport of the hormone and whole-plant hydraulic responses may also contribute. The choice of preparation therefore changes the precise claim that the data support.

To compare cuticular water loss between two species, one may first attempt to restrict stomatal opening and measure residual loss. The interpretation depends on whether pores are actually closed to a comparable degree. A species with more residual stomatal leakage could appear to have a more permeable cuticle even if its epidermal wax barrier is effective. Direct evidence about closure, matched temperature and humidity, and consistent normalization by leaf area are needed. A minimum whole-leaf conductance measurement is useful but should not automatically be renamed cuticular conductance.

The potometer is commonly used because it makes water movement visible at modest scale. Its calculation begins with the internal radius or calibrated capillary volume, not the outer diameter of the glass. A measurement of distance alone cannot be compared between tubes of different bore. The volumetric formula assumes that the cross section is uniform and that bubble motion reflects liquid displacement in a sealed system. Leaks or changes in gas-bubble volume due to temperature or pressure violate that assumption. A calibration using a known displaced volume can improve confidence.

Water uptake by a cut shoot can be expressed per unit time or per leaf area. Consider two shoots taking up 12 and 18 μL min⁻¹. Their leaf areas are 60 and 120 cm². The normalized rates are 0.20 and 0.15 μL cm⁻² min⁻¹, respectively. The second has greater total uptake but lower uptake per area. Depending on the question, either comparison might be relevant. If the aim is to estimate water use of the entire shoot, use total uptake. If the aim is to compare the intensity of exchange at leaf surfaces, area normalization is more informative.

A porometer and a gas-exchange system also require attention to which area is enclosed. If only half the chamber aperture is occupied by the leaf, using the full chamber area in the calculation underestimates flux per actual leaf area. A small leaf might require a corrected area measurement after the experiment. If gas exchange is recorded from one side only, the instrument's convention must be understood before comparing with values expressed per total projected area. Even a scientifically correct equation produces a misleading result when inappropriate geometric information is supplied.

A gravimetric experiment with detached leaves can reveal rates of dehydration under controlled conditions, but its physiological interpretation differs from an intact-plant experiment. The supply of water from roots has been removed. The leaf may change turgor, conductance and metabolism during the measurement interval. The initial rate may be more representative of the prior state than a later rate after severe dehydration, although cutting can cause immediate responses. Reporting a mass loss over a long interval without recording the time course obscures these changes. When studying intact-plant water use, a covered-pot design is generally closer to the original physiological system.

For temperature-based estimates, distinguish a proxy from the measured quantity. Thermal imaging measures radiation from a surface and is used to estimate temperature. A leaf temperature difference can be related to transpiration using an energy-balance framework, but only if other relevant variables are considered. A surface that absorbs more light may be warmer despite similar water loss. A cooler shaded leaf may transpire less than a warmer sunlit leaf. Temperature patterns can guide the choice of leaves for detailed gas-exchange measurements; they are not a substitute for measuring the flux when quantitative transpiration is required.

A plant's water potential can be assessed with a pressure chamber in suitable tissues by finding a balancing pressure under defined measurement conditions. Such a measurement is not a direct measurement of flow. The potential of a leaf and the soil, together with steady flux, may be used to estimate effective hydraulic conductance. When water storage is changing or the measured potentials are not representative of the relevant flow path, this estimate becomes less reliable. Combining potential and flow measurements is valuable precisely because each alone describes a different aspect of transport.

Measurements often differ in temporal resolution. A mass balance over two hours gives an integrated loss. A gas-exchange instrument may record changes every few seconds but may require equilibration to obtain a valid steady measurement. A microscopy image captures a structural state at the moment of observation, subject to disturbance during preparation. A daily biomass ratio integrates many processes over time. When comparing these outputs, align the relevant time windows rather than expecting a snapshot of aperture to predict exactly a day's water loss. This principle is particularly useful in questions that juxtapose a potometer, a porometer and an infrared thermometer.

Integrated predictions and evidence limits

Consider a well-watered plant suddenly moved from still humid air to dry moving air. At the earliest stage, external vapour pressure falls and boundary-layer conductance increases. If aperture and leaf temperature initially remain close to their previous values, the physical model predicts a larger vapour flux. The increased evaporation can lower leaf temperature and increase the demand on liquid transport. If the plant can supply sufficient water, the new conductance and flux may stabilize at relatively high values. If supply is limited, declining water status can trigger stomatal restriction. Thus an observed later low flux does not contradict the initial prediction of enhanced diffusion.

To test the sequence, record gas exchange and leaf temperature at short intervals and measure water potential at selected points on comparable leaves. The control group should experience the same handling and chamber changes without the humidity and wind treatments. A factorial design that separately varies humidity and wind permits estimation of their individual effects and their interaction. If both variables are changed together in only one treatment, their separate contributions cannot be estimated from the data. The plant's initial state and leaf area must also be comparable.

A related question involves an aquaporin perturbation. If a treatment reduces root or leaf hydraulic conductance while the evaporative environment remains constant, maintaining the original flux would require a larger potential drop. The plant may respond by decreasing stomatal conductance, which lowers vapour loss and reduces the hydraulic demand. This is a plausible consequence of reduced supply, not evidence that aquaporins actively pump vapour through stomata. To establish the causal chain, one would need to confirm the hydraulic perturbation, examine stomatal behaviour and exclude unrelated toxic or developmental effects.

Genetic comparisons can strengthen interpretation but do not eliminate confounding automatically. A mutation affecting stomatal development may also alter leaf morphology or plant size. A lower whole-plant water use in that mutant might result from a smaller canopy rather than from altered regulation of each pore. Compare stomatal density, aperture, leaf area, per-area exchange and total water use. If a genetic rescue restores both stomatal traits and the exchange phenotype under matched conditions, the case for a causal contribution becomes stronger. The conclusion should still be limited to the conditions and traits actually measured.

When analysing a graph with VPD on the horizontal axis and transpiration on the vertical axis, a positive slope can indicate that the increased driving force exceeds any reduction in conductance. A plateau is consistent with a conductance decrease roughly compensating for the increased deficit. A falling branch can indicate stronger compensation. These descriptions assume that the plotted deficit is the relevant leaf-to-air value, that area and pressure conventions are consistent, and that the relationship is not being confounded by unreported changes in temperature or time. From the curve alone it is not possible to identify a particular receptor or hormone.

A graph with assimilation on the vertical axis and stomatal conductance on the horizontal axis tests a different relationship. At very low conductance, the supply of carbon dioxide may constrain assimilation. At higher conductance, photosynthetic biochemistry can become the dominant limit, so further opening may have little effect on carbon gain while increasing potential water loss. This is why stomatal regulation is often discussed as a balance of carbon benefits and hydraulic costs. A plant does not consciously calculate those quantities; the observed pattern arises through physiological regulation and evolution.

Finally, distinguish prediction under stipulated assumptions from a universal empirical claim. If an examination problem explicitly states that leaf temperature, pressure and conductance remain constant, use the proportionality between E and D. If the question describes an intact plant under sustained water stress without fixing conductance, consider feedback and avoid an unconditional proportional prediction. The ability to switch between the simplified model and the biologically regulated system is a central learning objective of this chapter. It is the difference between calculating a flux correctly and explaining why a real plant may not sustain that flux.

12. Original examination practice

Part B-level conceptual practice

  1. Which process is transpiration?

    1. Water entering root hairs from the soil
    2. Loss of water vapour from aerial plant surfaces
    3. Liquid release through hydathodes
    4. Water moving along xylem vessels
    Answer and explanation

    Loss of water vapour from aerial plant surfaces. Liquid guttation, root uptake and xylem bulk flow are distinct processes, although they can be coupled.

    A is uptake, C is guttation and D is liquid transport; none is itself vapour loss.

  2. What is the usual driving force for vapour loss?

    1. ATP hydrolysis at the stomatal pore
    2. A leaf-to-air water vapour mole-fraction gradient
    3. A positive root pressure in every plant
    4. The osmotic potential of the atmosphere alone
    Answer and explanation

    A leaf-to-air water vapour mole-fraction gradient. ATP-dependent guard-cell transport controls aperture, but does not propel individual vapour molecules.

    A is incorrect because vapour diffusion is passive; C is not required during normal transpiration; D confuses liquid water potential with the gas-phase model.

  3. What is the function of the boundary layer?

    1. It generates root pressure
    2. It actively pumps water out
    3. It contributes resistance to transfer between the leaf and surrounding air
    4. It seals the leaf permanently against all gas exchange
    Answer and explanation

    It contributes resistance to transfer between the leaf and surrounding air. A thicker layer of slowly moving air commonly reduces boundary-layer conductance.

    The boundary layer is a gas-phase transport region, not a biological pump or impermeable seal.

  4. Which term is commonly negative in a transpiring xylem conduit?

    1. Pressure potential
    2. Gravitational potential referenced to the base
    3. Turgor in every living leaf cell
    4. Saturation vapour pressure
    Answer and explanation

    Pressure potential. Negative pressure represents tension; it is not synonymous with negative osmotic potential.

    Gravity can be positive above the reference; living turgid cells generally have positive pressure; vapour pressure is a positive partial pressure.

  5. What is the direct function of the guard-cell proton ATPase in opening?

    1. It pumps water out of the pore
    2. It imports potassium using ATP
    3. It exports protons and hyperpolarizes the plasma membrane
    4. It directly hydrolyses wall cellulose
    Answer and explanation

    It exports protons and hyperpolarizes the plasma membrane. The resulting electrochemical conditions can favour potassium entry; water movement follows potential gradients.

    The pump transports H⁺, not water or K⁺; wall properties convert turgor into motion, not direct wall hydrolysis by the ATPase.

  6. What process does a bubble potometer directly estimate?

    1. Stomatal density
    2. Water uptake by a cut shoot
    3. Carbon dioxide assimilation
    4. Leaf water potential
    Answer and explanation

    Water uptake by a cut shoot. Uptake approximates vapour loss only under suitable steady-state conditions with negligible leakage or storage change.

    A potometer measures displaced liquid; microscopy, gas analysis and pressure chamber measurements address the other quantities.

  7. What defines intrinsic water-use efficiency?

    1. A/E
    2. Dry mass per irrigation volume
    3. E/gsw
    4. Net CO₂ assimilation divided by stomatal conductance to water vapour
    Answer and explanation

    Net CO₂ assimilation divided by stomatal conductance to water vapour. A/E instead divides assimilation by water flux and depends directly on vapour deficit.

    A/E is instantaneous photosynthetic WUE; dry mass per irrigation describes a whole-system ratio; E/gsw is related to the driving gradient, not carbon gain.

  8. Why may a CAM plant open its stomata at night?

    1. Atmospheric CO₂ can be stored temporarily in organic acids while vapour demand is often lower.
    2. Its Calvin cycle is absent
    3. Its cuticle becomes fully permeable every night
    4. Root pressure forces the pores to open regardless of water status
    Answer and explanation

    Atmospheric CO₂ can be stored temporarily in organic acids while vapour demand is often lower. Internal CO₂ released in daylight permits assimilation with restricted daytime aperture; the exact timing is flexible.

    CAM uses a Calvin cycle, and its characteristic temporal carbon fixation cannot be explained by the cuticle or universal root pressure.

  9. What is a likely consequence of cuticular water loss when stomata close?

    1. The cuticle becomes a water pump
    2. Water can leave only as liquid
    3. The plant must immediately dehydrate completely
    4. Some residual water vapour loss can persist.
    Answer and explanation

    Some residual water vapour loss can persist. A cuticle is a barrier, not a perfectly impermeable layer.

    Cuticular diffusion is neither pumping nor liquid exudation; residual loss need not cause immediate dehydration.

  10. What does anion release during ABA-induced closure tend to do to membrane voltage?

    1. Depolarize it
    2. Always hyperpolarize it
    3. Remove all membrane voltage permanently
    4. Make the exterior electrically neutral by eliminating its ions
    Answer and explanation

    Depolarize it. Efflux of negative charge makes the interior less negative and can favour outward potassium-channel activity.

    Anion efflux removes negative charge from the interior, favouring depolarization. It does not eliminate ion gradients or permanently remove voltage.

  11. Which cell property directly produces the mechanical opening of an ordinary stoma?

    1. Increased guard-cell turgor acting through wall geometry.
    2. Increased phloem sugar loading alone
    3. Complete loss of guard-cell turgor
    4. Active contraction of a muscle in the pore
    Answer and explanation

    Increased guard-cell turgor acting through wall geometry. Ion accumulation changes osmotic potential; turgor then changes shape. ATP is not a direct mechanical actuator of the pore.

    Phloem loading is not the direct actuator; turgor loss usually promotes closure, and stomata have no muscle.

  12. Which structure releases liquid water during guttation?

    1. Sieve plates
    2. Guard cells
    3. Hydathodes
    4. Root hairs
    Answer and explanation

    Hydathodes. Guttation differs from water-vapour escape through stomata and from condensation of atmospheric water.

    Sieve plates are phloem structures, stomata regulate gaseous exchange, and root hairs absorb soil water.

  13. In the same leaf, which pathway remains when stomatal aperture is strongly restricted?

    1. Only xylem bulk flow
    2. Only liquid guttation
    3. Cuticular water transfer and possibly residual stomatal leakage.
    4. No pathway for any water movement
    Answer and explanation

    Cuticular water transfer and possibly residual stomatal leakage. The cuticle decreases rather than abolishes water permeability.

    The non-stomatal gas pathway can remain. Xylem and guttation are liquid processes, not the sole vapour routes.

  14. What does stomatal index normalize stomatal number against?

    1. Leaf dry mass
    2. The combined number of stomata and other epidermal cells in the sample.
    3. Total number of veins
    4. Total root surface area
    Answer and explanation

    The combined number of stomata and other epidermal cells in the sample. Density instead normalizes against area; leaf expansion may change density without changing index.

    Index is a relative cell-number measure; none of the other denominators is its definition.

  15. Which pressure is used to estimate ambient vapour pressure from relative humidity?

    1. Saturation vapour pressure at leaf temperature only
    2. Saturation vapour pressure at air temperature
    3. Root pressure
    4. Total atmospheric pressure alone
    Answer and explanation

    Saturation vapour pressure at air temperature. Leaf saturation pressure is needed separately for leaf-to-air deficit.

    Relative humidity is referenced to air temperature. Leaf temperature is used separately for internal saturation pressure, and atmospheric pressure converts partial pressure difference to mole fraction.

  16. Under constant aperture and leaf temperature, what is the usual direct effect of wind?

    1. Increasing boundary-layer conductance.
    2. Making vapour pressure of saturated water zero
    3. Stopping all water uptake
    4. Changing stomatal density instantly
    Answer and explanation

    Increasing boundary-layer conductance. The direct aerodynamic effect lowers resistance; feedback or cooling may alter actual flux.

    Wind modifies the external resistance directly, not saturation pressure at fixed temperature or the development of new pores.

  17. Why do counterions accumulate with guard-cell potassium?

    1. To stop osmosis
    2. To make all ions electrically uncharged
    3. To eliminate the need for ion channels
    4. They help preserve bulk charge balance and contribute to osmotic effects.
    Answer and explanation

    They help preserve bulk charge balance and contribute to osmotic effects. A membrane voltage involves relatively small charge separation, not unlimited net charge throughout the cell.

    Counterions remain charged and osmotically active. Their accumulation does not remove the need for regulated transport.

  18. Which measurement directly supplies a structural estimate of stomatal aperture?

    1. Gravimetry
    2. Potometer
    3. Pressure chamber
    4. Microscopy of stomatal pores
    Answer and explanation

    Microscopy of stomatal pores. A porometer estimates gas conductance; a potometer estimates water uptake.

    The other methods measure mass change, uptake and water potential, respectively.

  19. Which physical process accounts for the latent heat flux of transpiration?

    1. Nitrate assimilation
    2. Evaporation of liquid water.
    3. Root cell division
    4. Sensible heat transfer without a phase change
    Answer and explanation

    Evaporation of liquid water. Sensible heat transfer instead involves a temperature gradient without requiring a phase change.

    Latent heat accompanies phase change; metabolism and sensible heat are different energy terms.

  20. What does biomass-based water-use efficiency describe?

    1. Dry biomass gained relative to a defined cumulative water-use measure
    2. VPD divided by wind speed
    3. Stomatal density multiplied by leaf area
    4. Latent heat divided by root pressure
    Answer and explanation

    Dry biomass gained relative to a defined cumulative water-use measure. It differs from instantaneous assimilation divided by conductance or vapour flux.

    Only the first expression relates biological production to water use; the other combinations do not measure biomass efficiency.

Part C-level analytical practice

  1. Leaf and air are at 25 °C, saturation pressure is 3.17 kPa, and RH is 40%. Calculate the leaf-to-air VPD.

    Answer and explanation

    1.902 kPa. Ambient vapour pressure is 0.4 × 3.17 = 1.268 kPa. Subtract it from 3.17 to obtain 1.902 kPa.

  2. A leaf has gsw = 0.20 and gbw = 0.20 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. At D = 2 kPa and P = 100 kPa, estimate E.

    Answer and explanation

    2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Serial resistance is 5 + 5 = 10. Total conductance is 0.10; multiply by 0.02 to obtain 0.002 mol m⁻² s⁻¹.

  3. VPD doubles and total conductance falls to 40% of the initial value. What happens to E in the simple flux model?

    Answer and explanation

    It becomes 80% of the initial rate. The product changes by 2 × 0.4 = 0.8. The stomatal response more than offsets the atmospheric forcing.

  4. A plant loses 12 g h⁻¹ and takes up 9 g h⁻¹ for three hours. Estimate the storage change.

    Answer and explanation

    −9 g. The net rate is −3 g h⁻¹, and the duration is three hours. Storage decline does not imply zero root uptake.

  5. An attached leaf becomes cooler when wind rises, while gsw stays constant. Can the direction of change in E be inferred from wind alone?

    Answer and explanation

    No. Wind raises boundary-layer conductance but cooling reduces the internal saturation pressure. The net effect depends on both terms.

  6. A potometer shows greater uptake after an increase in light. What extra evidence would separate heating from stomatal opening?

    Answer and explanation

    Measure leaf temperature, humidity and stomatal conductance while controlling other conditions. Light can raise the vapour deficit and can open stomata; uptake alone cannot discriminate the mechanisms.

  7. A plant's assimilation remains 12 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ while E falls from 4 to 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. What happens to A/E?

    Answer and explanation

    It rises from 3 to 6 μmol CO₂ per mmol H₂O. Carbon gain is unchanged and the water flux halves. A/gsw cannot be calculated without the conductance.

  8. A mutant retains high stomatal conductance after ABA treatment. What would complementation of an anion-channel gene help establish?

    Answer and explanation

    Whether the mutation contributes causally to the impaired closure phenotype. Rescue under matched conditions supports the gene's role; it does not show that other transport or signalling proteins are unnecessary.

  9. A gravimetric plant system loses 11 g in one hour; a matched soil-only control loses 3 g. Estimate corrected transpiration.

    Answer and explanation

    8 g h⁻¹. Subtract the estimated non-plant evaporation. This assumes similar evaporation from the two systems and negligible additional mass changes.

  10. A leaf has E = 5 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and latent heat of evaporation 44 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate its latent heat flux.

    Answer and explanation

    220 W m⁻². Convert E to 0.005 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, multiply by 44,000 J mol⁻¹. This is not the entire leaf energy balance.

  11. A leaf has 80 stomata and 320 other epidermal cells in a counted area. What is stomatal index?

    Answer and explanation

    20%. 100 × 80/(80 + 320) = 20. Density would require the sampled area.

  12. Two identical-temperature leaves have deficits of 1.0 and 2.0 kPa. Their total conductances are 0.20 and 0.10. Compare E at the same pressure.

    Answer and explanation

    Equal. Both gD products equal 0.20 in consistent units; a stronger gradient is exactly offset by lower conductance.

  13. A leaf stays at 30 °C (saturation pressure 4.24 kPa) in air at 20 °C (saturation pressure 2.34 kPa) and 50% RH. Estimate D.

    Answer and explanation

    3.07 kPa. Ambient vapour pressure is 1.17 kPa, so D = 4.24 − 1.17 = 3.07 kPa.

  14. Root, stem and leaf resistances are 0.1, 0.2 and 0.2 MPa per unit flux. What is total conductance?

    Answer and explanation

    2 units of flux per MPa. Series resistance is 0.5; its reciprocal is 2. Use a consistent area basis.

  15. A cuticular conductance of 0.01 and stomatal conductance of 0.09 share one gradient. What fraction of epidermal flux uses the cuticle?

    Answer and explanation

    10%. Parallel conductance sums to 0.10, giving 0.01/0.10. This neglects different local gradients.

  16. A capillary of radius 0.5 mm has bubble movement of 20 mm in four minutes. Estimate uptake rate.

    Answer and explanation

    About 3.93 μL min⁻¹. Volume = π × 0.5² × 20 = 15.71 mm³, equivalent to 15.71 μL. Divide by four.

  17. At 298 K, an ideal solute increase of 0.05 mol L⁻¹ occurs at fixed volume. Estimate ΔΨs using RT = 2.48 MPa L mol⁻¹.

    Answer and explanation

    −0.124 MPa. The negative sign represents lower osmotic potential. Real water influx may dilute the added solutes.

  18. A plant has high morning leaf transpiration before basal sap flow increases. What mechanism can explain this transient difference?

    Answer and explanation

    Release of water stored within tissues. Storage contributes to the flux difference; the result does not disprove cohesion–tension or indicate water generation.

  19. Assimilation is 20 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and gsw is 0.25 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Find intrinsic WUE.

    Answer and explanation

    80 μmol CO₂ mol⁻¹ H₂O. Divide A by gsw, not by vapour flux. Without E the instantaneous A/E cannot be determined.

  20. In an illustrative dataset, E is 4.4 at D = 2 and 3.6 at D = 3. Calculate the effective conductance ratio g3/g2 at fixed P.

    Answer and explanation

    Approximately 0.545. Since g = EP/D, ratio = (3.6/3)/(4.4/2) = 1.2/2.2. Conductance fell substantially.

  21. A leaf initially has gsw = 0.30 and gbw = 0.15 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, D = 1 kPa, P = 100 kPa. A fan increases gbw to 0.60; feedback lowers gsw to 0.15 and cooling lowers D to 0.8 kPa. Which result is correct?

    1. E rises from 1.0 to 3.6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    2. E falls from 1.0 to 0.96 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    3. E stays at 3.0 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    4. E rises from 1.0 to 1.2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    Answer and explanation

    B. Initial total conductance is 0.10; E = 0.10 × 0.01 × 1000 = 1.0. New total conductance = 1/(1/0.15 + 1/0.60) = 0.12, giving 0.12 × 0.008 × 1000 = 0.96. A and C confuse stomatal conductance with total conductance; D omits the decrease in deficit.

  22. A leaf receives 260 W m⁻² net radiation. Assume steady state, latent heat 44 kJ mol⁻¹, and negligible storage and metabolism. Before treatment E = 4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹; after treatment E = 1.5. Which statements are correct? I: latent heat flux decreases by 110 W m⁻². II: sensible heat must increase by 110 W m⁻² in the stated model. III: leaf temperature necessarily rises by 110 °C.

    1. I only
    2. II and III only
    3. I and II only
    4. I, II and III
    Answer and explanation

    C. The latent heat difference is (0.004 − 0.0015) × 44,000 = 110 W m⁻². In the stated two-term budget, the sensible heat increases by the same amount. A flux in W m⁻² is not a temperature; III requires a thermal transfer relation and is dimensionally incorrect.

  23. A hypothetical plant is measured in two equal one-hour intervals: A = 18 and 9 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹; E = 3 and 1 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. What is the two-hour integrated carbon-to-water ratio?

    1. 7.5 μmol/mmol
    2. 6.75 μmol/mmol
    3. 9 μmol/mmol
    4. 6 μmol/mmol
    Answer and explanation

    B. Equal durations cancel when summing fluxes: (18 + 9)/(3 + 1) = 6.75 μmol/mmol. The arithmetic mean of the two instantaneous ratios, 6 and 9, is 7.5, which weights unequal water fluxes incorrectly. C and D are individual ratios.

  24. Two genotypes have the same leaf temperature and VPD. X has E = 4, A = 16, leaf area 0.50 m²; Y has E = 3, A = 12, leaf area 0.90 m², using mmol H₂O and μmol CO₂ per m² per second. Assume uniform exchange. Which statement follows?

    1. Y has lower total water loss and higher leaf WUE.
    2. X and Y have identical total water loss.
    3. X has higher per-area transpiration, equal instantaneous leaf WUE and lower total water loss.
    4. Y has greater WUE solely because its leaf area is larger.
    Answer and explanation

    C. Both A/E ratios are 4 μmol/mmol. X loses 4 × 0.50 = 2 mmol s⁻¹; Y loses 3 × 0.90 = 2.7 mmol s⁻¹. Per-area flux alone therefore predicts neither whole-plant water use nor efficiency.

  25. In a VPD-step experiment at constant pressure and measured leaf temperature, E doubles immediately, then declines to its baseline value at the same elevated D. A pore-imaging measurement confirms gradual closure. Which statements are supported? I: the initial response can reflect increased driving force. II: effective conductance later declined. III: the experiment alone proves ABA is the only signal.

    1. I and II only
    2. I and III only
    3. II and III only
    4. I, II and III
    Answer and explanation

    A. The fixed elevated deficit coupled to a falling flux implies lower effective conductance in the simplified model, consistent with the pore images. The design provides no direct evidence identifying ABA as the sole cause, so III is not supported.

  26. A mutant has half the effective soil-to-leaf hydraulic conductance of its control at the same soil water potential. The control maintains J = 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ with K = 6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹. Assuming the mutant has the same leaf-area basis and cannot tolerate a potential drop greater than 0.5 MPa, what steady flux should be its approximate upper bound?

    1. 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    2. 6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    3. 0.5 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    4. 1.5 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹
    Answer and explanation

    D. Mutant K = 3; multiplying by the largest permitted difference, 0.5 MPa, gives 1.5 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Maintaining the control flux would require a drop of 1 MPa. Stomatal closure could reduce demand, but does not increase the stated hydraulic conductance.

  27. A guard-cell experiment compares wild type, a loss-of-function anion-channel mutant, and a complemented mutant. Initial aperture is matched. After ABA, only wild type and the complemented line show strong closure. Which conclusion is most defensible?

    1. ABA has no other targets.
    2. The anion channel contributes causally to normal ABA-induced closure under these conditions.
    3. Potassium flux is unnecessary in all plants.
    4. The channel actively pumps water out of the pore.
    Answer and explanation

    B. The loss of response and genetic rescue support causal involvement of the channel. A and C overgeneralize the result to untested processes; D confuses ion movement and passive water movement.

  28. A porometer shows a 50% decrease in gsw in a dry-air treatment. In a gas-exchange chamber D rises from 1 to 3 kPa; assume negligible boundary resistance, constant pressure and unchanged leaf area. If assimilation declines from 12 to 8 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹, what happens to E and intrinsic WUE?

    1. E falls by half; intrinsic WUE falls by one-third.
    2. E rises 50%; intrinsic WUE rises by one-third.
    3. E triples; intrinsic WUE is unchanged.
    4. E remains unchanged; intrinsic WUE doubles.
    Answer and explanation

    B. E changes by 0.5 × 3 = 1.5. A/gsw changes by (8/12)/0.5 = 4/3. The decrease in gsw is not identical to a decrease in E, and intrinsic WUE uses conductance rather than actual transpiration.

Assertion–reason, experimental design and SET application

  1. Assertion–reason: Stomatal closure can decrease leaf cooling. Reason: Evaporation consumes latent heat. Are both true and is the reason explanatory?

    Answer and explanation

    Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion under comparable conditions. Closure usually limits water vapour loss, reducing latent heat removal. Other energy-balance terms determine the actual temperature.

  2. Assertion–reason: Stomatal density and stomatal index are equivalent. Reason: Leaf expansion can lower cell density without changing the relative cell counts.

    Answer and explanation

    Assertion false; reason true. Index concerns the ratio of cell types and density concerns the number per area.

  3. Assertion–reason: A potometer always directly measures transpiration. Reason: Leaf and stem tissues may store water.

    Answer and explanation

    Assertion false; reason true. The ability to store water is one reason uptake need not equal evaporation during a short measurement.

  4. A researcher coats only the lower epidermis and sees a decrease in water loss. What should be checked before inferring stomatal distribution?

    Answer and explanation

    Use microscopy and controls for coating and thermal effects. A reduced loss is consistent with blocking pores but can also result from changed surface permeability or leaf temperature.

  5. An investigator records humidity but not temperature while comparing two leaves. Why is the experiment incomplete?

    Answer and explanation

    Relative humidity alone cannot define leaf-to-air VPD. Saturation pressure depends on temperature; the internal leaf and external air temperatures may differ.

  6. An ABA-treated leaf loses less water than a control. Does this establish SLAC1 as the sole cause?

    Answer and explanation

    No. A whole-leaf flux response alone does not resolve individual signalling components; additional genetic or ion-current evidence is required.

  7. A leaf is exposed to high VPD for ten minutes and then returned to baseline. It does not immediately recover its initial conductance. Give one plausible explanation and one measurement.

    Answer and explanation

    Delayed hydraulic or physiological recovery; measure leaf water potential over time. A lag may reflect depleted storage or regulatory state. The time course can help separate transient and steady-state effects.

  8. A chamber experiment treats five leaves from one plant and calls them five independent plants. What is the design error?

    Answer and explanation

    Pseudoreplication at the plant level. Leaves may be separate subsamples but share genetics and whole-plant environment; independent plant replication is needed for plant-level inference.

  9. A C4 leaf has a higher A/gsw than a C3 leaf. Does it necessarily use less total water over a season?

    Answer and explanation

    No. Seasonal water use depends on leaf area, VPD, duration and whole-plant regulation as well as leaf-level carbon gain.

  10. A xylem-delivered solute has concentration 2 mmol L⁻¹ and water flow 0.05 L h⁻¹. What is the delivery rate?

    Answer and explanation

    0.10 mmol h⁻¹. Multiply concentration by volume flow; this is delivered amount, not necessarily the rate of uptake into target cells.

13. Revision and mastery

Separate the liquid hydraulic gradient from the gas-phase vapour gradient. Distinguish flux, conductance, pressure deficit and storage. Stomatal regulation changes resistance and coordinates water loss with carbon assimilation. Compare methods by their actual measured variables.

Active recall

  1. Trace water from soil to ambient air.
  2. Distinguish evaporation, transpiration, uptake and guttation.
  3. Explain why leaf temperature is required for VPD.
  4. Derive serial conductance from additive resistance.
  5. Predict the response to humidity at fixed conductance.
  6. Explain guard-cell opening using electrochemical and osmotic gradients.
  7. Explain why closure cannot always eliminate loss.
  8. Distinguish A/E from A/gsw.
  9. Calculate storage change when uptake lags behind transpiration.
  10. Design a gas-exchange test of stomatal response to VPD.
