1. Why a genome is not a fixed arrangement

A chromosome is more than a long molecule that can be faithfully copied. Its sequence also records changes in the position, number and arrangement of genetic sequences. Imagine two individuals of the same species with a gene at the same chromosomal locus. One individual carries a block of additional DNA inside that gene, whereas the other does not. The inserted block may have originated from another position in the genome. If it belongs to a family capable of movement through a characteristic molecular mechanism, it is called a transposable element (TE). An insertion can therefore change a chromosome without the chromosome itself moving to another nucleus.

The term mobile refers to the ability of a sequence, or a copy of it, to become established at a different DNA site. It does not mean that every physical copy is moving in every cell. A large fraction of transposon-derived DNA in many genomes consists of inactive remnants. Mutations may have destroyed the coding sequence for the relevant enzyme, removed an essential recognition signal, or prevented expression. Even an intact element may be silenced by its host. A sequence can thus be a recognizable member of a transposable family without being presently mobile.

Three earlier ideas are essential. A locus is a position on a chromosome; a gene is a sequence whose expression produces a functional RNA or protein; a repeated sequence is one found at multiple locations. A transposable element is defined by its history and mechanism of movement, not simply by its gene content or copy number. A single active element can be a TE before it becomes repetitive, and a thousand tandem copies of a satellite sequence need not transpose. Some mobile elements contain one or more genes of their own, while others contain no protein-coding gene at all.

The smallest useful description of a transposition event identifies a donor site, the element and a target site. The donor is the original location and the target is the DNA position where a new insertion becomes established. The target may lie in the same DNA molecule or another DNA molecule present in the cell. For example, movement from a bacterial chromosome into a plasmid is possible without implying that the plasmid has been transferred to another bacterium. Transposition within a cell and transfer between cells are distinct processes.

The key question is whether the original DNA segment is physically excised and inserted elsewhere, or whether a new copy is made. Copying can occur entirely at the DNA level or through an RNA intermediate. These alternatives explain the characteristic changes in element copy number, molecular intermediates and genomic organization. They also explain why superficially similar insertion patterns can arise from different mechanisms. In this chapter we will follow the molecular evidence rather than classify an element by the name of its host organism.

2. From unstable maize loci to mobile elements

In the 1940s, Barbara McClintock investigated maize chromosomes using genetic crosses and cytological observations. At the time, a common working picture was that a gene occupied a stable chromosomal position unless a conventional mutation or chromosome rearrangement occurred. Her maize material displayed reproducible changes in chromosome breakage and the behavior of genetic markers that could not be explained simply by assigning every determinant a permanently fixed location.

She identified a chromosome-breaking locus called Dissociation (Ds) whose behavior depended on another genetic determinant called Activator (Ac). Ds-associated breakage occurred in the presence of Ac, including when Ac was elsewhere in the genome. The changing positions of these determinants in genetic maps and their effects on marker expression supported the idea that certain genetic elements themselves could relocate. A crucial conceptual advance was to distinguish the mobile DNA sequence from the enzyme-producing capacity required for its mobility. Modern molecular work established Ac as an autonomous DNA transposon and many Ds derivatives as nonautonomous elements related to it.

Consider the standard teaching model of kernel pigmentation, simplified from the genetics of maize controlling elements. Suppose an allele of a gene required for pigment synthesis is functional when uninterrupted. Insertion of a Ds element can inactivate that allele. In a genetic background where no other allele supplies the function, the affected cells lack pigment. If Ac is present and expressed, the transposase it encodes can act on the recognizable ends of Ds. Excision of Ds may restore pigment-gene function, provided the repaired site retains the sequence required for expression. A descendant cell population then develops pigment.

Why is the result a spot rather than uniform coloration? Plant tissues develop through successive divisions of precursor cells. An event occurring in one precursor is inherited by its daughter cells; it need not occur in other precursors. The pigmented sector therefore marks a cell lineage in which a functional pigment pathway was restored. Under comparable growth and sampling conditions, early restoration generally allows a larger descendant sector than late restoration. Spot area is not an exact clock, because cell division rates, tissue geometry and pigment accumulation vary.

The control in the simplified genetic comparison is an otherwise comparable genotype carrying the same disruptive Ds insertion but lacking active Ac. The prediction is little or no Ac-dependent restoration in this background. Restoring functional Ac permits the Ds-dependent switching. A pigment phenotype alone is not proof of molecular transposition: an ordinary sequence mutation, epigenetic change or loss of a chromosome could also change pigment. The compelling historical case came from concordant mapping, cytological evidence and inheritance patterns across crosses. Modern sequence comparison of donor and insertion junctions distinguishes excision, reinsertion and repair more directly.

An excision event is not guaranteed to restore the original allele. Excision can leave an altered donor junction, sometimes called a footprint. If this change disrupts the reading frame or a critical regulatory motif, the gene may remain nonfunctional. Consequently, the number of colored spots measures a subset of biological outcomes rather than the total number of movement events. McClintock's discovery was recognized with the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The Ac/Ds system remains valuable for examination reasoning because it connects a visible phenotype, genetic dependency, molecular movement and developmental timing without requiring specialist laboratory methods.

3. Classify by the intermediate, not by the final product

All standard chromosomal transposon insertions ultimately consist of DNA. That fact is not sufficient to classify an element. The decisive question is the nature of the nucleic-acid intermediate that carries the element sequence to its new position. In the usual modern classification, Class II elements are DNA transposons and Class I elements are retrotransposons. DNA transposons move through DNA intermediates; retrotransposons use an RNA intermediate that is copied into DNA by reverse transcription. Older textbook subclass numbering sometimes differs, so follow the mechanism if the labels conflict.

A DNA transposon typically possesses recognition sequences near its ends and a source of transposase, the enzyme that catalyzes the essential DNA breakage and strand-transfer reactions. A conventional cut-and-paste DNA element can move without duplicating its body. Other DNA transposons undergo replicative transposition, in which DNA synthesis creates an additional element copy. Replicative movement is therefore not synonymous with retrotransposition. In both cases a copy may remain at the donor, but in one the additional DNA is generated during a DNA-based recombination reaction and in the other RNA is used as a template.

A retrotransposon is generally transcribed from its genomic DNA. Its transcript is used directly or indirectly to produce proteins and to provide the information for a new DNA copy. Reverse transcriptase is a DNA polymerase that can synthesize DNA using RNA as a template. Unlike ordinary transcription, which produces RNA from DNA, reverse transcription makes DNA from RNA. This does not reverse the flow of sequence information from protein into nucleic acid. The new DNA becomes part of a target site and the original chromosomal element is normally retained.

A second distinction is independent of the first: autonomous versus nonautonomous. An autonomous element encodes the element-specific machinery needed for its own movement, subject to host factors and appropriate expression. A nonautonomous element lacks some or all of that machinery but retains the cis-acting sequences needed to be recognized by a compatible source of proteins. Cis-acting means physically located on the DNA or RNA molecule being acted upon. A trans-acting protein can be produced from a separate genetic locus and act on another molecule. Both Class I and Class II contain autonomous and nonautonomous families.

The classification is a framework, not a claim that every element obeys one idealized cartoon. Certain DNA elements use rolling-circle-like movement and may lack conventional terminal inverted repeats. Some non-LTR retrotransposons recognize unusual targets. Some transposon-derived elements have lost mobility but contribute to normal genome function. For examination questions, identify the supplied evidence and select the most specific supported mechanism. Do not infer a reverse transcriptase simply from copy-number gain, or infer autonomous movement simply from the existence of intact terminal repeats.

4. Recognition sequences, insertion junctions and target-site duplications

Before following the movement reactions, it is necessary to understand the sequence signatures at a typical insertion. A DNA transposon frequently has terminal inverted repeats (TIRs), short sequences at its two ends that are reverse complements when written on the same reference strand in the 5′ to 3′ direction. For instance, 5′-AGTC-3′ and 5′-GACT-3′ are reverse complements. Complementarity follows from A pairing with T and G with C; reversing the order is necessary because the paired strands are antiparallel. The TIRs belong to the mobile element and provide binding or recognition information for its movement apparatus.

An insertion frequently produces a second kind of repeat outside the element: the target-site duplication (TSD). Unlike a TIR, a TSD is copied from the original host target sequence. The two copies normally appear in the same orientation as one another on the reference strand. If the original host sequence is left flank–ACGA–right flank, a four-base-pair duplication gives left flank–ACGA–element–ACGA–right flank after insertion. ACGA was present once before insertion and twice afterward; it is not an intrinsic part of the element's terminal repeats.

How can a short segment of host DNA become duplicated? In many transposition reactions the enzyme attacks the two target DNA strands at positions offset by a few base pairs. The insertion ends become covalently joined to the target strands at these staggered positions. At that stage, the resulting DNA is incomplete: gaps or discontinuities remain at the two junctions. Host repair enzymes synthesize DNA across the missing stretches using the intact complementary strands as templates. DNA ligase seals the remaining nicks. Because the stagger exposed complementary information from the same original target region on opposite sides, completion produces direct repeats of the target sequence flanking the insertion.

The stagger length is often characteristic of a transposon family and is a useful signature in exam problems. It is not universal across all elements. TSDs may subsequently accumulate mutations, and some insertion pathways can produce target deletions or no conventional TSD. Therefore a TSD strongly supports a particular insertion history when compared with the empty allele; its absence does not prove that transposition never occurred. An intact TSD at a newly discovered locus also does not prove which exact enzyme made it, because unrelated transposition systems can generate similar junctions.

The difference between the two repeat types is best established by comparing sequences. Consider two insertions of the same element, one flanked by CGTA and another by AATC. If their terminal element sequences match but their flanking duplicated sequences differ, this is consistent with a common element inserting at different host targets. If a sequence is retained when the element moves, it belongs to the element or to transferred cargo; if it corresponds to the recipient locus, it is host-derived. The previous chapter on repetitive DNA dealt with copy arrangements at the genome scale. Here the repeats are evidence of an individual DNA integration reaction.

A long terminal repeat (LTR) is yet another feature. LTRs occur at both ends of a typical complete LTR retrotransposon as relatively long direct repeats; they are element-derived and contain information used in expression and reverse transcription. They are not the short host-derived TSD and are not the reverse-complement TIR of a typical DNA transposon. All three structures can be represented as blocks at an insertion boundary, but their origin, orientation and function distinguish them.

5. Bacterial insertion sequences and composite elements

Insertion sequences (IS elements) are among the simplest common bacterial transposable elements. A typical IS consists principally of the sequences necessary for mobility, often including a transposase coding region bounded by terminal recognition sequences. An IS does not generally carry an unrelated passenger gene whose product gives a separate physiological trait. It can nevertheless strongly affect its host when it inserts into a functional locus. An IS disrupting a coding region changes the DNA template for RNA synthesis; its transcriptional effects can also alter expression of adjacent genes.

Some IS elements provide promoters or alter the spacing between existing regulatory sequences. Consider a bacterial operon containing three genes transcribed from a single promoter. An IS insertion in the first coding region may disrupt the first protein and may also reduce expression of downstream genes if transcription terminates prematurely or transcription and translation coupling is disturbed. This downstream effect is called polarity. It is not an unavoidable outcome of every insertion: orientation, the inserted sequence and operon architecture matter. A new promoter within an IS may instead stimulate expression of a neighboring gene. Thus structural position and orientation, not simply the size of the insertion, determine the phenotype.

A composite transposon contains two IS elements that flank intervening DNA. The inner material may contain a gene unrelated to the movement machinery. If the transposition system uses the outer boundaries of the two flanking IS elements as its ends, it mobilizes the entire composite unit. This provides a mechanism by which DNA that did not originally encode mobility becomes part of a mobile unit. Classical examples are Tn5 and Tn10, which carry resistance-associated cargo; the resistance biochemical mechanisms are not needed to understand their structure here.

The two flanking IS copies need not each be independently functional. One may provide the transposase, while both contribute recognizable outer ends. In a specific family, the IS orientation may be direct or inverted; the important test for movement as a unit is whether the necessary external ends can be paired and processed. If only one IS moves independently, the cargo may be left behind. A DNA map alone showing two IS elements around a gene is therefore compatible with composite architecture but does not guarantee that all three sequences move together in a particular experiment.

A unit or noncomposite bacterial transposon differs because it is bounded by terminal recognition sequences of the whole element rather than by two full flanking IS elements. Tn3 is a classical example. It includes transposition functions and an additional cargo gene. Its transposase (encoded by tnpA) supports formation of a DNA intermediate, and its resolvase (encoded by tnpR) separates a fused intermediate by recombination at specific res sites. The genes required to create and resolve this intermediate belong to the element's movement machinery; an unrelated passenger gene does not become a transposase merely because it is carried by the same element.

It is useful to separate three levels of movement. A plasmid is a DNA replicon that can maintain itself under appropriate conditions. An IS or transposon is a mobile segment that can become inserted into a plasmid or chromosome. A conjugative system may allow a plasmid to pass between cells. If a transposon is carried on that plasmid, the cargo may consequently spread to another cell, but the DNA rearrangement within the replicons and the intercellular transfer are mechanistically distinct. Not every plasmid is transmissible, and not every transposon is an independently replicating DNA molecule.

6. Conservative DNA transposition: cut, transfer and repair

A conventional cut-and-paste DNA transposon solves two molecular problems: it must free itself from its original genomic context and form new covalent junctions at a target. Consider a donor chromosome containing one transposon with two terminal recognition sequences. The transposase binds the terminal sequences, brings the ends together and assembles a protein–DNA complex that coordinates reactions at both ends. Pairing helps ensure that the boundaries of the complete element, rather than arbitrary chromosome segments, are used for movement.

The enzyme must generate reactive element ends. In a common mechanistic model, cleavage exposes hydroxyl groups at the 3′ termini of the element DNA. Cleavage on the other strands releases the element from the donor DNA; families differ in the exact order of cleavage and whether hairpin intermediates form. The essential conserved teaching idea is that the excised element becomes the DNA substrate for strand transfer. The original donor chromosome now contains a break or a disrupted junction and requires repair if the cell is to retain an intact genome.

The transposase then brings the element ends into contact with the target DNA. The exposed 3′ hydroxyl groups attack phosphodiester bonds on opposite target strands at offset positions. In this strand-transfer reaction, new covalent bonds join the element to the target. This is not insertion of a whole piece of DNA through an existing open hole; the molecular operation breaks and forms bonds in the DNA backbone. The reaction usually requires suitable metal ions and an intact catalytic site in the transposase. Host enzymes subsequently complete the junctions and, in the conventional staggered model, generate the TSD.

The donor must also be repaired. The cell may join its broken ends directly, possibly losing or gaining a small number of bases, or use an intact homologous sequence as a repair template if one is available. In the Ac/Ds pigment model, donor repair is what determines whether excision restores a working allele. An excised element can fail to integrate, and an insertion can occur at a site that damages cell fitness; excision alone is therefore not equivalent to a completed successful transposition event.

For an idealized successful cut-and-paste event, one intact element is removed from the donor and one is inserted at the recipient. Its total copy number in the DNA pool under consideration is conserved. The term conservative refers to this copy bookkeeping, not to guaranteed preservation of the donor sequence or its function. DNA repair synthesis can occur even in conservative movement; absence of element duplication does not mean absence of every DNA synthesis reaction.

An important exception to simple copy counting occurs when a donor break is repaired from a sister chromatid still containing an element. The excised copy can insert elsewhere and the original position can regain a copy by repair. Consequently an element family using a fundamentally excisive reaction can increase in copy number over time. Excision, chromosome replication, repair and selection must be distinguished when interpreting measured copy abundance. In a question stipulating no such secondary processes, conservative movement retains the original number. In a real cell, the observed final count may have more than one explanation.

Another complication is target preference. Transposase recognition of the element termini is often highly specific, while insertion-site selection depends on sequence preferences, DNA structure, accessory proteins and accessibility. An element can have a wide target range without being equally likely to insert at all positions. A repeated insertion hotspot may reflect biochemical preference; it may also reflect the survival or recovery of particular insertion-bearing cells. These possibilities can be distinguished only by appropriate sampling and controls.

7. Replicative DNA transposition: why the donor remains

The classical Tn3-family model illustrates how a DNA transposon can multiply without an RNA intermediate. Begin with a donor replicon carrying a transposon and a separate target replicon without that element. A replicon is a DNA unit capable of replication from its own initiation system. In the textbook example both molecules are circular, making their fusion and separation easy to follow. The donor element is not simply excised as a free double-stranded segment before insertion.

The transposase recognizes the element ends and catalyzes cleavage and strand transfer. The reactive element ends join staggered sites in the target DNA while other connections to the donor are retained. The resulting branched DNA structure links donor and recipient. Its architecture allows DNA synthesis to copy the element sequence as the intermediate is completed. One element becomes two copies, and the donor and target replicons become fused in a larger circular molecule called a cointegrate.

The cointegrate contains one copy of the transposon at each junction of the original replicons. Both copies have the same orientation along the fused DNA molecule. This arrangement is important because directly repeated copies contain the specific res recognition sites in orientations appropriate for the resolution reaction. The fused molecule is not the final normal product in a Tn3-type transposition event.

Tn3 resolvase acts at the two res sites. Site-specific recombination exchanges DNA connections between these sites in a controlled fashion. The cointegrate separates into two circular molecules: the original donor replicon, still carrying one element, and the recipient replicon, now carrying one element. DNA synthesis has increased the number of copies by one, whereas the resolution reaction changes their molecular distribution without making another copy. The recipient insertion can display a characteristic TSD generated by the staggered integration chemistry and completion of the junctions.

This sequence explains the different phenotypes of transposase and resolvase mutations. If the transposase is catalytically defective, the early strand-transfer intermediate does not form efficiently, so cointegrate formation and completed movement are both reduced. If only resolvase activity is lost, the strand-transfer and DNA synthesis reactions may still generate cointegrates, which accumulate when the usual separation step is blocked. Alternative resolution through homologous recombination may occur in some contexts, so a resolvase mutant need not show absolutely zero separate recipient molecules. The test is the distribution of intermediates and products under defined conditions.

Replicative transposition should not be confused with ordinary replication of a plasmid. If a plasmid containing one element is copied as a whole, there are now two plasmids, each with the element at the same relative locus. If transposition moves an element to a different replicon, the recipient acquires a new insertion junction absent before. Measurements of total copy number alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. Mapping the flanking DNA before and after the event establishes whether new integration occurred.

A classical comparison of DNA molecules containing different markers can reveal a fused donor–recipient intermediate and separated products. Published molecular analyses of Tn3 and related elements established that specific recombination occurs within the res region; this supports the resolution model rather than a model in which two independent complete insertions appear by chance. In the absence of suitable intermediate or molecular evidence, a pair of final insertions alone does not uniquely identify replicative transposition. The next class produces a comparable copy-number outcome by a different route, using RNA as an intermediate.

8. LTR retrotransposons: copy DNA through an RNA intermediate

Retrotransposons use a different solution to the mobility problem. Instead of first freeing the original DNA sequence, the element's chromosomal copy serves as a template for transcription. An RNA transcript contains the sequence information needed to reconstruct the element at a new site. The host chromosome need not lose its original element merely because a transcript is produced. This is why retrotransposition is fundamentally copy-and-paste.

The LTR retrotransposon is well illustrated by yeast Ty elements and Drosophila copia-like elements. A typical autonomous LTR element has a long terminal repeat at each end of a full-length DNA copy. Between the LTRs lie coding regions for proteins, often represented by gag-like and pol-like functions. The gag-like products contribute to assembly of an intracellular particle; pol-like products supply functions such as reverse transcriptase, RNase H, integrase and, in many families, protease. RNase H cleaves RNA in RNA–DNA hybrids, a function needed during the synthesis of a double-stranded DNA product. Integrase catalyzes insertion of a DNA copy into a target. The exact coding organization varies among families.

Transcription usually starts in the 5′ LTR and terminates in the 3′ LTR. The RNA is used for protein production and as the template for DNA synthesis. In many LTR elements the RNA and proteins assemble into virus-like particles inside the cell. Reverse transcriptase needs a primer with an available 3′ hydroxyl group rather than initiating DNA from nothing. Many such elements use a cellular tRNA primer at a primer-binding site for the first DNA strand and a resistant RNA segment, the polypurine tract, in initiating the other strand. The details of primer usage can vary; the essential result is a double-stranded DNA product derived from the RNA.

During reverse transcription, template transfers and complementary copying reconstruct full LTR sequences at the two ends of the new DNA. The two LTRs at the instant of insertion are therefore generally very similar, often identical in the standard model. The 5′ and 3′ LTRs in the original donor need not have remained identical throughout its evolutionary history. Mutations after insertion can cause the new LTRs to diverge independently. This provides a conceptual basis for using LTR divergence as an approximate indicator of insertion age, with assumptions that will be examined later.

The integrase processes the ends of the newly synthesized double-stranded DNA and catalyzes their joining to target DNA. The host repairs the incomplete junctions. Integration commonly leaves a short target-site duplication outside the two LTRs. In this mechanism the mobile integration substrate is DNA, but the DNA was produced through reverse transcription; therefore the element remains Class I. Merely observing DNA integration cannot distinguish this reaction from the direct movement of a DNA transposon.

LTR retrotransposons share important molecular features with retroviruses. Both use RNA-templated DNA synthesis and integration, and both may produce particle-like assemblies. Most ordinary LTR retrotransposons lack the machinery needed for a complete extracellular infectious cycle, however. A retrovirus may encode an envelope protein and spread between cells; the presence of an LTR and reverse transcriptase alone does not establish infectivity. Endogenous retroviral sequences in vertebrate genomes illustrate how inherited insertions can persist after infectivity is lost. The complete retroviral life cycle is outside the current chapter.

Once inserted, two similar LTRs on the same element may undergo homologous recombination with one another. Because they are directly oriented, such recombination can delete the internal region and one repeat, leaving a solo LTR at the locus. Thus even a family that generates additional DNA by retrotransposition may also contribute to DNA loss. A solo LTR can retain regulatory sequences and influence nearby expression while being incapable of producing a full element by itself. Recognizing these partial products prevents equating every sequence annotated as an LTR-derived repeat with an active retrotransposon.

9. Non-LTR retrotransposons and LINE-1

Non-LTR retrotransposons use RNA-templated DNA synthesis but do not have the two long terminal direct repeats characteristic of the previous group. In mammals, LINE-1 (L1) is the classic autonomous example. LINE means long interspersed nuclear element; interspersed describes copies scattered among other genomic sequences rather than confined to one tandem array. A typical intact human L1 is approximately six kilobases long and contains a 5′ untranslated region with an internal promoter, two principal open reading frames, a 3′ untranslated region and a poly(A) tail. An open reading frame (ORF) is a continuous sequence that can encode a polypeptide.

The first ORF encodes an RNA-binding protein, ORF1p, with nucleic-acid chaperone activity that helps organize the element RNA. The second produces ORF2p, which has endonuclease and reverse-transcriptase activities. An endonuclease cuts within a nucleic-acid strand, generating an end that can be used in the next reaction. The RNA and its proteins form a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex. L1 proteins show a preference for the RNA that encoded them, often called cis preference. It is a preference, not an absolute restriction, and explains why L1 can also mobilize certain other cellular RNAs.

After the complex accesses chromosomal DNA, ORF2p makes a nick at a suitable target, often in a T-rich region. The nick exposes a 3′ hydroxyl group on the target DNA. The poly(A) tail of the L1 RNA can base pair with complementary T-rich DNA, helping position the RNA and the exposed primer end. Reverse transcriptase extends the target DNA 3′ end, reading the L1 RNA as template and synthesizing a complementary DNA strand. This process is called target-primed reverse transcription (TPRT) because the DNA target itself supplies the primer for reverse transcription.

Unlike the standard LTR retrotransposon mechanism, L1 does not have to make a fully double-stranded free DNA copy in an intracellular particle before integration. DNA synthesis begins at the chromosome where insertion is being established. Second-strand processing and synthesis ultimately produce an integrated DNA copy; the precise molecular completion steps can differ between events. Typical new L1 insertions often have a poly(A)-rich tail and short TSDs. Many are truncated at the 5′ end because reverse transcription starts near the RNA 3′ end and may fail to reach the 5′ end. The 3′-derived sequence can therefore remain even when upstream coding and promoter sequences are absent.

A newly inserted truncated L1 may no longer encode both proteins needed for its own movement, and accumulated mutations can make even apparently full-length copies inactive. The genome can contain many hundreds of thousands of L1-derived sequences but only a small fraction of potentially active source elements. A question giving total LINE-derived DNA therefore does not automatically provide the number of active autonomous elements. Non-LTR elements encompass families with different organizations and target preferences; mammalian L1 is the model used here, not a universal architecture for all LINEs.

The distinction between L1 and a bacterial replicative transposon is especially important. Both commonly leave the donor element and create a new copy. L1 makes DNA by reverse transcription at the target; Tn3 uses DNA-based strand transfer, synthesis and cointegrate resolution. Inhibition of reverse transcriptase is expected to impair L1 movement if the relevant activity is blocked, but it does not specifically block the canonical Tn3 mechanism. Conversely, a Tn3-specific resolvase has no equivalent obligatory role in typical L1 TPRT.

10. SINEs, nonautonomy and processed pseudogenes

Short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) are another common group of non-LTR retrotransposons. Many are derived from small cellular RNAs and possess internal promoter information that can be used by RNA polymerase III. They are typically much shorter than LINEs and do not encode the complete element-specific machinery for reverse transcription and integration. In primates the Alu family, derived from 7SL RNA, is a well-known example. A typical Alu is about 300 nucleotides long in its canonical form, with two related sequence units and an A-rich 3′ region.

An Alu DNA copy is transcribed into RNA. Although this RNA cannot produce its own reverse transcriptase, it can recruit the appropriate proteins produced by an active L1 element in the same cell. The L1 ORF2p supplies the catalytic activities for target cleavage and reverse transcription in a TPRT-like process; other host or element factors affect efficiency. The Alu RNA becomes the template for the new Alu DNA insertion. Alu is therefore a retrotransposon even though its sequence encodes no reverse transcriptase. The molecule supplying the template and the locus supplying the protein need not be identical.

This relationship resembles Ac/Ds at the logical level of genetic complementation but differs in chemical mechanism. Ac transposase recognizes compatible Ds DNA ends and catalyzes DNA movement. L1 machinery supports the RNA-mediated movement of Alu. An examination problem may ask whether addition of an active Ac element rescues an Alu unable to transpose. It cannot generally do so because the trans-acting proteins and the necessary cis-recognition information belong to different molecular systems. Autonomy requires the right machinery, not just any mobile-element enzyme.

Processed pseudogenes illustrate another outcome of the same broad RNA-to-DNA machinery. A mature cellular messenger RNA often contains joined exons and a poly(A) tail but lacks the introns present in the genomic gene. If retrotransposition machinery copies such an RNA into genomic DNA, the new locus may resemble the spliced transcript rather than the original gene. It may lack the original promoter and may be nonfunctional; such loci are called processed pseudogenes when they do not retain the usual gene function. Some retrocopies acquire regulatory sequences and become functional retrogenes, so the word pseudogene should not be applied to every retrocopy.

Compare this with a gene duplicated directly at the DNA level. A copied genomic region can retain introns and nearby regulatory DNA, depending on the boundaries of duplication. A processed retrocopy usually shows loss of introns corresponding to the mature RNA and may retain a poly(A)-derived tract and target-site duplications. These are clues rather than absolute tests: an original gene can be intronless, poly(A)-rich sequences occur elsewhere, and ancient copies lose diagnostic features. Combining multiple features and comparison to the parent locus makes the inference stronger.

Nonautonomous elements may become very abundant because they use proteins produced by a smaller number of active autonomous elements. Their shorter templates may also influence propagation efficiency in particular families. However, abundance alone cannot demonstrate a higher rate of present-day transposition. Old copy numbers are the outcome of past insertions, mutation, deletion, fixation, selection and loss of activity. The distinction between sequence history and present mobility is essential for making sense of the repetitive DNA introduced earlier in the subunit.

11. Insertional mutations and changes in gene organization

A new insertion alters the relationship between the recipient DNA and neighboring sequences. Its consequences depend first on its location. In a protein-coding exon, an insertion can interrupt the coding sequence. If the net added bases are not divisible by three and the inserted bases remain in the mature coding transcript, the downstream reading frame is changed. Even an insertion whose length is divisible by three may introduce stop codons or disrupt essential protein structure. Whether the insertion lies in a coding exon, an untranslated exon, an intron or a regulatory region must be established before predicting a frameshift.

An intronic insertion need not alter the encoded protein if the intron is still removed correctly. But it can interfere with splice-site recognition, introduce a new splice site or a premature cleavage and polyadenylation signal, or change transcription. Some element-derived sequences can be included in new exons. This process is known as exonization. The altered RNA may produce a different protein, be unstable, or never be translated. The chapter on interrupted genes provided the structural distinction between genomic DNA, primary transcripts and mature RNA; here that distinction explains why an insertion present in genomic DNA does not necessarily remain in a protein-coding RNA.

A regulatory-region insertion can affect expression even if no coding sequence changes. An element may contain promoter or enhancer-like sequences, transcription termination information, or binding sites for proteins that alter chromatin. A promoter can initiate transcription; an enhancer is a sequence that can increase transcription from an appropriate promoter in a context-dependent fashion. Their effects depend on their sequence, orientation where relevant, distance, accessibility and compatibility with the neighboring gene. An insertion upstream of a gene is not automatically inhibitory and is not automatically activating.

Insertion can also create a new relationship with the epigenetic state of a region. Many host genomes suppress transposable families through compact chromatin and DNA or histone modifications. Repression near an insertion may extend to neighboring DNA in some settings and influence gene accessibility. This is a direct connection with euchromatin and heterochromatin: transcriptional inactivity may reflect the chromatin environment, not merely absence of a functional element promoter. The details of DNA methylation, small-RNA pathways and their regulation are developed in the gene-expression unit. Here the key structural consequence is that mobile sequences can change the organization and accessibility of a chromosome.

A documented human example is insertion of L1 into the factor VIII gene (F8), which established that a new retrotransposition event could disrupt a human gene and cause haemophilia A. The reported insertions retained L1 3′ sequence and poly(A) tracts and were flanked by duplicated host sequence, connecting the clinical observation to the structural predictions of TPRT. Factor VIII participates in blood coagulation; disruption of its expression reduces the capacity to form a normal clot. This is an example of insertional mutation, not a consequence of the reverse transcriptase protein directly damaging the coagulation pathway. The effect of a particular insertion depends on the affected gene, tissue, genetic background and inheritance. A somatic insertion is acquired within a body cell lineage and can produce mosaicism without being passed to offspring. An insertion in a germline lineage may be transmitted if compatible with reproduction. New insertions are therefore not all inherited, and a mutation seen in a tumor is not necessarily present in all cells of the individual.

It is equally important to recognize neutral and sometimes beneficial effects. An insertion in a tolerated intergenic region may have little measurable effect under the tested conditions. Over evolution, host genomes can reuse transposon-derived sequences as regulatory components or, in some cases, genes. This is called co-option or exaptation when a feature acquires a host function different from its original role. The occurrence of useful derivatives does not imply that transposition evolved for the benefit of the host. Many insertions are removed by selection or become inactive; others persist because their effects are neutral or context dependent.

12. Dispersed repeats as substrates for chromosome rearrangement

Transposition itself is not the only way that mobile sequences affect genome organization. Once related copies have become distributed at different locations, they offer homologous sequences that cellular recombination machinery may recognize. Normally homologous recombination aligns corresponding DNA regions. Nonallelic homologous recombination occurs when similar sequences at noncorresponding loci are used instead. A transposable family can thus influence chromosome structure even when all the participating copies are no longer capable of transposition.

Consider two directly oriented repeats on the same DNA molecule with unique sequence between them. Alignment of these copies followed by an intramolecular recombination event can join the outer flanks and release the intervening segment as a circular DNA product in a simplified model. One repeat remains at the junction of the retained molecule. If the segment included a gene, that gene can be deleted. This is why the two LTRs of an element can recombine to form a solo LTR. The recombination process does not require the transposon's original transposase if ordinary recombination machinery acts on the homologous sequences.

If the two repeats are instead inverted relative to one another, the geometry of intramolecular recombination is different. Rather than removing the intervening segment, recombination can reverse its orientation. The resulting inversion may preserve overall DNA amount but change the direction of genes, alter their regulatory context or affect chromosome behavior. The simple rule—direct repeats favor deletion in this intramolecular model; inverted repeats favor inversion—must be applied only after checking the topology and the identity of the molecules involved.

Misalignment between related repeats on different homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids can generate unequal exchange. One recombinant may lose the interval between the misaligned repeats while the other gains an additional copy. This provides a route to both deletion and duplication. In an exam problem, a diagram showing two direct repeats is not enough to decide between deletion and reciprocal duplication: it also matters whether the exchange is within one molecule or between two molecules. Interchromosomal recombination involving related copies can contribute to other rearrangements, including translocations, but the detailed inheritance of such changes belongs in genetics.

The same logic explains why mobile-element copy abundance changes through opposing processes. Retrotransposition adds sequence; excision and recombination can remove it; ordinary mutation degrades sequences; selection alters their representation in populations. Genome size is the result of all these processes, not a direct measure of present-day transposition. Different species can have very different amounts and classes of TE-derived DNA without having proportionally different numbers of protein-coding genes.

A rearrangement associated with two TE copies is not necessarily caused by a new transposition event. Compare the breakpoints. New element integration may produce fresh element–target junctions and TSDs. Recombination between pre-existing copies may instead produce a hybrid repeat at a rearrangement junction and eliminate or duplicate the intervening interval. Neither signature alone is infallible, but reconstructing the ancestral and resulting arrangements provides a testable explanation. This is the structural bridge from transposons to chromosome behavior, DNA repair and inheritance studied in subsequent units.

13. Experimental reasoning: distinguish movement from expression

A central goal of analytical questions is to infer the mechanism from observations without overinterpreting them. Imagine a donor and a recipient whose flanking DNA sequences are known. A DNA test for the internal element alone is not sufficient because it cannot establish the location of the element. A test spanning the left and right insertion junctions provides evidence that the element has entered the recipient DNA. A separate donor-junction test determines whether the original locus still contains the element or instead shows a repair footprint.

For a conservative element in an idealized system, the expectation is loss of the intact donor insertion and appearance of new recipient junctions. For an RNA-mediated element, the original DNA donor generally remains and new recipient junctions appear. Replicative DNA transposition also leaves the donor intact, so one must look for additional evidence: a DNA cointegrate or DNA-based movement in one case, and a requirement for reverse transcription or a diagnostic RNA-processing signature in the other. A donor-present result alone cannot choose between these copy-producing routes.

A particularly instructive reasoning design uses a reporter sequence interrupted by an artificial intron. The reporter is positioned and oriented so that the intron is removed from the transposon RNA, but a functional reporter is possible only after an appropriately processed reverse-transcribed copy has been established in DNA. A genomic insertion containing the exon-joined reporter provides evidence that the sequence passed through a spliced RNA intermediate. By contrast, simple DNA cut-and-paste of an unchanged donor would retain the intron. This is a teaching-level description of the logic used in retrotransposition reporters, not a claim that every assay uses identical arrangements.

The necessary controls include an otherwise comparable donor with inactive reverse transcriptase, an intact donor with a suitable reporter, and a recipient lacking a new insertion. One should also control for expression and viability: a mutant that prevents RNA production, destabilizes the element or kills the cells could reduce the reporter signal without specifically blocking reverse transcription. Mapping the new insertion junctions and checking the exon junction in DNA distinguishes productive integration from expression of the donor RNA or contamination with RNA. A positive reporter alone can be misleading if its activation is possible without integration.

A corresponding comparison for an autonomous DNA element and a nonautonomous deletion derivative tests whether protein can act in trans. The nonautonomous element alone is expected to show little compatible movement; addition of an intact compatible enzyme source may rescue movement. A control carrying a catalytic-site mutation tests whether the rescue depends on enzyme activity. A derivative missing the terminal recognition sequences generally cannot be rescued by adding more transposase, because the missing cis information must be present on the substrate. These comparisons teach the mechanistic difference between a missing protein and a missing DNA or RNA signal.

Finally, the frequency recovered in an experiment is not necessarily the biochemical rate of transposition. An insertion that kills a cell will not be counted in colonies derived from survivors. A selection marker may detect only insertions in a specified location or orientation. The population may expand after a single early event, producing many related insertion-bearing cells rather than many independent events. In data interpretation, distinguish frequency per recovered cell, unique insertion events, total element copies and the time interval of observation. A well-supported explanation states what was measured and what remains uncertain.

To interpret a time course, remember that the amount of an intermediate is determined by both its rate of formation and its rate of removal. The hypothetical graph below starts with no cointegrates in either sample. Both genotypes reach the same measured value at ten minutes, consistent with similar initial production under these conditions. The wild-type intermediate subsequently declines, while the mutant continues to accumulate it. These observations support impaired removal of the intermediate, but equal intermediate counts at one time point cannot establish that the formation rates are identical. A second assay counting separated recipient molecules is needed to establish whether resolution rather than another downstream fate is altered.

14. Quantitative reasoning and genome bookkeeping

Quantitative questions about transposition are usually problems in sequence accounting, copy number and probability, rather than advanced kinetic models. Start with the definition of the DNA pool being measured. In a single idealized pool, let N be the initial number of intact element copies and m the number of completed movement events. If every event is conservative and there is no element loss, repair-mediated duplication or DNA replication affecting the count, then the final number is N: each event removes one intact copy and adds one. If every event is copy-producing and none is lost, the final number is N + m, because each event contributes one additional copy while retaining its source.

For example, a genome contains 12 intact copies of a family. Four completed conservative movements merely change four donor and recipient arrangements under the stipulated conditions; the count remains 12. Four copy-producing movements increase the count to 16. If two of the resulting 16 copies are subsequently deleted, 14 remain. The phrase four events does not mean four rounds of doubling of every copy. If every existing copy makes exactly one additional copy during each round and every descendant copy is equally active, then N after g rounds is N₀ × 2ᵍ: each round multiplies the preceding population by two. Starting with 3 copies, three such rounds yield 3 × 2³ = 24 copies. Real element families seldom satisfy these ideal assumptions, particularly because many new copies are defective or silenced.

At an individual recipient site, let L be the length of the element in base pairs and d the length of the duplicated target sequence. The original target region already contains d base pairs. After insertion the locus contains the element plus two copies of d, so the net added length at that locus is L + d, not L + 2d. If the insertion is 1,400 bp long and generates a 5-bp TSD, the recipient gains 1,405 bp. The final span from the start of the first repeat to the end of the second is 1,410 bp. These two lengths answer different questions.

Now consider a 2,000-bp DNA transposon moving from a donor on the same chromosome to a target where an 8-bp duplication is generated. Suppose the original element alone is removed, the donor is restored with no extra loss or gain of flanking bases, and no secondary DNA synthesis changes the element count. The recipient gains 2,008 bp while the donor loses 2,000 bp. The chromosome's net length gain is 8 bp despite the conserved element count. In real excision, donor footprints or deletion of an old duplicated target copy alter that accounting. A problem must specify what happens at the donor before a precise whole-chromosome length can be calculated.

Genome composition is another frequent source of confusion. Suppose a simplified 3.0 × 10⁹ bp haploid genome contains 4.5 × 10⁸ bp assigned to a dispersed repeat family, with no overlapping annotations. Its fraction is the family length divided by the genome length, which equals 0.15; multiplication by 100 gives 15%. This percentage is a fraction of bases, not of genes or active elements. If the average annotated fragment is 1,500 bp, the rough count of fragments is 4.5 × 10⁸ / 1,500 = 300,000. Fragment count is not necessarily intact copy count, because one historical element may be represented by multiple fragments.

A more subtle use of sequence data is the LTR divergence clock. Suppose a new LTR retrotransposon begins with two identical terminal repeats of equal aligned length. Assume each accumulates neutral substitutions independently at the same constant rate μ substitutions per site per year. After t years, the expected divergence for each relative to their original sequence is μt. The two independently evolving sequences differ from each other by approximately 2μt for small divergence, neglecting repeated changes at the same position. If D is their observed difference fraction, t ≈ D/(2μ). For LTRs differing at 12 of 600 aligned sites, D = 12/600 = 0.02. With an assumed μ of 1 × 10⁻⁸ per site per year, the estimated age is 0.02/(2 × 10⁻⁸) = 1 × 10⁶ years. This is an illustrative calculation, not an empirically measured age. Gene conversion, uncertain rates, selection, alignment errors and multiple substitutions can invalidate this simple estimate.

A final elementary model concerns random insertion. Suppose each of k independent insertions is equally likely to occur at any of G possible positions, and a region of interest has g of those positions. The chance that a single insertion hits it is p = g/G. The chance that one insertion misses is 1 − p. The probability that all k miss is (1 − p)ᵏ by independence, so the chance of at least one hit is 1 − (1 − p)ᵏ. With p = 0.1 and k = 3, this equals 1 − 0.9³ = 0.271, or 27.1%. It is not 30%, because adding three probabilities double-counts outcomes in which more than one insertion hits. Target preferences, chromatin accessibility and survival can violate the equal-probability and independence assumptions. State the model before using it to interpret real insertions.

15. Activity, silencing and limits of the simplified models

A successful insertion depends on a chain of prerequisites. An autonomous element must be transcriptionally accessible when its movement requires newly synthesized proteins; its coding sequence must be functional; the appropriate termini or RNA signals must remain intact; the enzymes must act on available DNA and host factors must complete the product. A nonautonomous element additionally needs a compatible enzyme source. Failure at any one step can reduce the frequency of completed insertion even if the others remain normal.

Hosts can restrict movement at several points. The compact chromatin state discussed in the preceding chapter can reduce transcription of elements. DNA methylation, where used by the organism, and repressive histone modifications can help maintain inactivity. Some eukaryotes use small-RNA-guided pathways that recognize TE-derived transcripts and reduce their abundance or promote silencing. These defenses vary across organisms and tissues; they are not interchangeable universal mechanisms. The purpose of introducing them here is to explain why inherited TE-derived DNA is often present without frequent ongoing transposition.

For example, if transcription is suppressed, an RNA intermediate may become scarce even though an intact reverse transcriptase coding sequence remains in the genome. If only catalytic activity is impaired, RNA may remain abundant but completed insertions decrease. If target integration is defective, a reverse-transcribed product may be present without a new chromosomal junction. These distinguishable outcomes provide the logic for experimental predictions. One should not infer mechanism solely from a final reduced insertion count.

Changes in chromatin or development can alter the fraction of cells in which an element is active, but not every environmental stress or developmental transition increases mobility. The response depends on element family and organism. Conversely, reactivation of one autonomous family need not mobilize unrelated nonautonomous elements. Their recognition signals may be incompatible. A copied RNA or DNA can persist without entering a germline and therefore need not contribute to long-term species-level sequence evolution.

The class scheme also has limits. Not every DNA transposon has conventional TIRs or undergoes excisive or Tn3-like replicative movement; certain elements use other DNA-based routes. Not every retrotransposon has LTRs or an internal protein-coding region. Not all families produce the same length of TSD, and ancient insertions may lose their diagnostic sequence signatures. These qualifications do not make the central distinction unreliable: DNA-based transposition and RNA-mediated reverse transcription remain fundamentally different routes to genomic change.

By the end of this subunit, it should be possible to distinguish a gene's normal locus, a gene duplication, a repeated DNA array, a chromatin-state difference and a mobile-element insertion. They are related but not synonymous. Chromosomes are maintained, copied and segregated by cells, but their contents can change over generations. The following subunit examines how cells control chromosome duplication and distribution in the cell cycle. The detailed chemistry of replication, repair and gene regulation belongs to later chapters; transposition has provided a structural reason why those processes matter.

16. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

These are original practice questions. Select the best option before revealing the explanation.

1. Which observation is sufficient to classify an element as Class I?

  1. A new copy is formed
  2. RNA is an obligatory template for the new DNA
  3. The donor contains DNA
  4. The element occurs many times
Answer and reasoning

B. The RNA template is diagnostic. Copy formation occurs in replicative DNA transposition; donor DNA and high abundance occur in either class.

2. Which sequence is a target-site duplication?

  1. Element-encoded transposase ORF
  2. Two reverse-complement ends within the element
  3. A short host-derived direct repeat flanking the new insertion
  4. A long internal repeated region
Answer and reasoning

C. Staggered insertion and repair can duplicate the host sequence. A is coding information; B describes TIRs; D does not identify the origin or boundary.

3. Ac supplies which activity for compatible Ds movement?

  1. Reverse transcriptase
  2. DNA transposase
  3. RNA polymerase III
  4. Tn3 resolvase
Answer and reasoning

B. Ac encodes transposase recognizing Ac/Ds termini; the other proteins belong to unrelated reactions or ordinary transcription.

4. A classical composite transposon consists of:

  1. Two IS elements surrounding passenger DNA
  2. Two LTRs surrounding gag and pol
  3. Two sister chromatids
  4. Only a duplicated target site
Answer and reasoning

A. The flanking IS boundaries define the composite unit. B is LTR retrotransposon architecture; C and D are not composite elements.

5. Which Tn3 product precedes resolution?

  1. A free RNA copy
  2. A cointegrate with two element copies
  3. A solo LTR
  4. A processed pseudogene
Answer and reasoning

B. Tn3 DNA-based synthesis generates a fused replicon with two copies; the other options describe RNA or LTR-related products.

6. A typical autonomous human LINE-1 contains:

  1. A pair of complete flanking IS elements
  2. Only a short RNA polymerase III transcript
  3. ORFs encoding RNA-binding and endonuclease/reverse-transcriptase functions
  4. Two obligatory LTRs
Answer and reasoning

C. ORF1p and ORF2p support L1 mobility. A describes composite structure, B is more like a SINE and D applies to LTR elements.

7. Which statement about SINEs is correct?

  1. They all encode their own reverse transcriptase
  2. They are tandem satellite arrays
  3. They are DNA-only excisive transposons
  4. Many use compatible LINE proteins to mobilize an RNA template
Answer and reasoning

D. SINEs are usually nonautonomous retrotransposons; their dispersion, lack of complete protein machinery and RNA intermediate rule out A, B and C.

8. Assertion: a solo LTR may remain after a full LTR retrotransposon is lost. Reason: intramolecular recombination between directly oriented LTRs can delete the intervening DNA.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. The orientation permits deletion with one LTR remaining, so the reason directly explains the outcome; all other choices misstate the relationship.

9. A typical processed pseudogene is most directly derived from:

  1. A mature cellular RNA copied into DNA
  2. A chromosome spindle
  3. An unprocessed genomic duplication necessarily retaining all introns
  4. A centromeric satellite
Answer and reasoning

A. The RNA intermediate explains exon junctions and possible poly(A)-derived DNA. B and D do not copy gene transcripts; C describes a different mechanism.

10. Assertion: a transposon-derived sequence need not be currently mobile. Reason: the sequence can retain recognizable family similarity after mutations destroy its essential movement functions.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. Loss of critical functions while retaining recognizable sequence explains inactive remnants. B, C and D contradict that logical link.

17. Part C — Mechanism, data and quantitative reasoning

All numerical datasets and simplified experiments in these problems are illustrative, not reported experimental measurements.

1. An empty target reads A–TGCA–B; a new allele reads A–TGCA–[element]–TGCA–B. The element itself is 820 bp. What is the net recipient length gain?

  1. 820 bp
  2. 824 bp
  3. 828 bp
  4. 816 bp
Answer and reasoning

B. Only one extra TGCA is added: 820 + 4 = 824 bp. A ignores duplication; C counts both repeats as new; D subtracts a repeat.

2. A Ds derivative lacks its transposase coding region but retains the required termini. Which comparison best tests nonautonomy?

  1. Compare unrelated maize species
  2. Provide active Ac versus inactive Ac in otherwise comparable Ds genotypes
  3. Compare genomes of unequal ploidy only
  4. Add an unrelated reverse transcriptase
Answer and reasoning

B. This manipulates the compatible protein source while holding substrate identity fixed. A and C confound genetic background; D supplies the wrong mechanism.

3. A maize kernel has many small pigmented sectors rather than one large sector. In the simplified Ds model, which inference is most defensible?

  1. The events necessarily occurred before the first cell division
  2. The element must be a SINE
  3. The pattern is consistent with restorations in relatively late precursors, but timing is not exact
  4. All cells acquired the identical excision at the same time
Answer and reasoning

C. Smaller lineages are consistent with later restoration, subject to growth and expression differences. A predicts a potentially large sector; B is unrelated; D cannot explain localized independent sectors.

4. A Tn3 resolvase mutant retains transposase activity. Which change is expected relative to wild type?

  1. No element termini can be recognized
  2. Cointegrates accumulate relative to separated products
  3. Reverse transcription becomes obligatory
  4. All donor copies disappear
Answer and reasoning

B. The mutation blocks the normal resolution step downstream of cointegrate formation. A is an early transposase defect; C confuses classes; D is not the replicative outcome.

5. After movement, donor DNA remains intact, and the new recipient insertion contains an exon-joined reporter whose intron is still present in the donor DNA. What does the evidence favor?

  1. An RNA intermediate
  2. Simple unchanged DNA cut-and-paste
  3. Chromosome condensation alone
  4. A pure tandem amplification of the donor
Answer and reasoning

A. Splicing followed by DNA formation at a different locus explains the new exon junction. B and D would ordinarily retain the donor intron; C does not change sequence.

6. Two directly oriented homologous TE remnants flank a gene on a single DNA molecule. Recombination between them yields one repeat at the junction. Which outcome is most consistent?

  1. Inversion of the interval
  2. Deletion of the intervening gene
  3. Reverse transcription of the gene necessarily
  4. Duplication of both original repeats in the retained DNA
Answer and reasoning

B. Direct intramolecular repeat recombination removes the interval and one repeat in the simplified model. A corresponds to inverted repeats; C is unnecessary; D contradicts the junction.

7. An L1 source produces abundant RNA, but ORF2p reverse transcriptase is catalytically inactive. A compatible active alternative source is absent. What is predicted?

  1. New L1 and L1-dependent Alu insertions are reduced
  2. Only L1 is reduced; Alu encodes its own polymerase
  3. Tn3 specifically requires the same L1 protein
  4. L1 RNA cannot be transcribed
Answer and reasoning

A. The shared reverse-transcription activity is lost despite RNA expression. B incorrectly assigns autonomous machinery to Alu; C confuses unrelated systems; D contradicts the observation.

8. A new insertion is 120 bp inside an intron. The mature RNA retains normal exon junctions and its coding sequence is unchanged. Which is supported?

  1. A frameshift necessarily occurs
  2. The encoded sequence is unchanged under the tested conditions
  3. The element cannot be mobile
  4. The intron must have become an exon
Answer and reasoning

B. The measured mature coding sequence is unchanged. A assumes insertion is retained in coding RNA; C is unrelated; D contradicts normal splicing.

9. A genome begins with 15 copies. Six completed replicative DNA movements occur, then three intact copies are deleted. No other events occur. Final count?

  1. 15
  2. 18
  3. 21
  4. 30
Answer and reasoning

B. 15 + 6 − 3 = 18. A ignores net change; C ignores deletions; D incorrectly doubles the entire starting pool.

10. A newly integrated LTR element initially has identical LTRs. Their divergence is 0.04 substitutions/site and the assumed rate per LTR is 2 × 10⁻⁸ per site/year. Under the low-divergence neutral model, estimate age.

  1. 0.5 million years
  2. 1 million years
  3. 2 million years
  4. 4 million years
Answer and reasoning

B. t = 0.04/(2 × 2 × 10⁻⁸) = 10⁶ years. A halves twice; C omits the two-lineage factor; D doubles again. Real rates and gene conversion limit the estimate.

11. Three independent insertions each have probability 0.2 of hitting a defined region. Probability of at least one hit?

  1. 0.6
  2. 0.512
  3. 0.488
  4. 0.008
Answer and reasoning

C. 1 − 0.8³ = 0.488. A double-counts multiple hits; B is the chance all miss; D is the chance all hit.

12. One population shows ten times as many TE-derived fragments as a related population but the recovered insertion frequency is lower. Which interpretation is supported?

  1. Historical abundance and current activity are different measurements
  2. All old copies must be autonomous
  3. The low frequency proves no transposition ever occurred
  4. The larger genome necessarily contains ten times as many genes
Answer and reasoning

A. Old insertions, degradation and selection can increase historical fragments while activity declines. B, C and D infer properties not supported by the two measurements.

13. A mutant DNA transposon is not rescued by abundant compatible transposase. Sequencing shows both terminal recognition sequences are absent. Which explanation fits?

  1. The cis substrate signal is missing
  2. The protein source must be a SINE
  3. All DNA transposons require an RNA template
  4. The host lacks all DNA polymerases
Answer and reasoning

A. An enzyme cannot generally substitute for missing substrate recognition sites. B and C confuse classes; D is unsupported and incompatible with normal DNA replication.

14. A recipient insertion has a 3′ poly(A)-derived tract, an upstream truncation and a short TSD; the donor is unchanged. Which mechanism best fits this combination?

  1. LTR retrotransposition with two intact LTRs necessarily
  2. Non-LTR L1-like TPRT
  3. Direct satellite recombination alone
  4. Tn3 resolution alone
Answer and reasoning

B. TPRT starts near the RNA 3′ end and can yield a truncated 5′ copy. A predicts a different structure; C and D do not account for the RNA-derived pattern.

15. In the illustrative cointegrate graph in Section 13, what is the strongest interpretation of the mutant's increase from 20 to 45 unresolved intermediates after 10 minutes?

  1. The mutant must have exactly 2.25 times the transposase catalytic rate.
  2. Accumulation is consistent with impaired resolution; a product assay is needed to discriminate formation and removal.
  3. Reverse transcription is obligatory.
  4. The wild type never formed a cointegrate.
Answer and reasoning

B. Intermediate abundance is a balance of production and consumption. A incorrectly infers a rate from a ratio of pool sizes; C is not required for Tn3; D contradicts the wild-type value at ten minutes. The graph suggests impaired intermediate clearance but cannot by itself prove a particular molecular defect.
