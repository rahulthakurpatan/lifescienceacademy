1. From filtration to selective recovery

The glomerulus supplies the renal tubule with a large volume of fluid that resembles the freely filterable portion of plasma. That fluid is not yet urine. If every millilitre of glomerular filtrate left the body, a mammal would rapidly lose circulating volume, nutrients and essential ions. The nephron solves this problem by providing an extensive epithelial surface between tubular fluid and the renal interstitium. Across this surface, selected materials return to the circulation, while other materials enter the tubule from the circulation. The resulting urine is therefore a regulated output, rather than a simple miniature sample of plasma.

An adult human with a glomerular filtration rate of 125 mL/min filters approximately 180 L in a day. The figure is an illustrative reference, not a fixed normal value for every individual. A daily urine output of 1.5 L under those conditions implies net water recovery of 178.5 L. More than 99% of filtered water is recovered. A change of only one percentage point in fractional recovery, while filtration remains unchanged, represents 1.8 L/day. This numerical fact explains why relatively small alterations in epithelial water handling can have a large influence on extracellular fluid balance.

Three distinct processes must remain separate in the student's mental model. Filtration transfers water and filterable solutes from glomerular capillary plasma into Bowman's space. Reabsorption moves a substance from tubular lumen toward the interstitium and blood. Secretion moves it in the opposite epithelial direction. Excretion describes the amount leaving the body in final urine per unit time. A molecule can undergo all three renal processes in different regions. The term reabsorption does not imply that a substance was previously absorbed from the gut, nor does secretion necessarily mean glandular production of a molecule.

The physiological reason for coupling high filtration to selective transport is that the glomerulus cannot distinguish every useful small molecule from every harmful small molecule. Glucose, amino acids, bicarbonate and many ions accompany water into the filtrate. Efficient downstream recovery permits filtration to continue removing metabolic waste while conserving valuable resources. The kidney can also eliminate compounds that are insufficiently filtered, for example because a fraction remains protein bound in plasma, by transporting their freely available molecules into tubular fluid. Dissociation from binding proteins can replenish the free pool as secretion proceeds.

It is useful to imagine two connected transport networks: a tubular stream that proceeds from the corpuscle to the collecting system, and a vascular stream that supplies the epithelial cells and receives recovered material. The efferent arteriole, unlike most arterioles leaving a capillary bed, leads into another capillary network. In cortical regions, peritubular capillaries closely surround convoluted tubules. In the medulla, the vasa recta exchange substances with the surrounding interstitium. The anatomy enables the tubule to return material without first mixing it back into the incoming glomerular stream.

A nephron is not a uniform pipe. The proximal tubule performs high-capacity recovery; the descending and ascending loop segments differ greatly in water and salt permeability; the distal convolution and connecting tubule refine ion composition; the collecting duct adjusts the output according to whole-body needs. These tasks depend on different transporter proteins and epithelial architecture. A substance recovered from the proximal tubule cannot subsequently reach the collecting duct unless it is secreted again, enters from another source or remains in the lumen. Consequently, the output from each segment constrains the workload of every segment downstream.

A transporter defect must be interpreted in this serial context. If proximal sodium recovery falls, more sodium may arrive at the thick ascending limb and distal nephron. Those segments may compensate by increasing recovery, but their capacity and regulatory states impose limits. The final urinary change can therefore be smaller than the primary local defect. Conversely, if downstream transport is already saturated, a small upstream disturbance may be amplified. This is why measurements in final urine do not by themselves uniquely identify the segment where a disturbance originated.

The chapter uses the mammalian nephron as a mechanistic reference. Other vertebrates vary in nephron design, filtration rates, electrolyte handling and the importance of tubular secretion. A bird, for instance, possesses both looped and loopless nephrons, whereas typical teleost kidneys lack the mammalian loop-based concentrating architecture. Some marine fish can depend strongly on secretion for eliminating particular solutes. The basic accounting of epithelial flux remains applicable even when anatomical details differ. Mammalian segmental transporter names should not automatically be assigned to every animal.

The previous chapter established the physical and regulatory determinants of filtration. Here the central question shifts from how much fluid enters to what happens to each molecule after entry. The following chapter will concentrate on medullary gradients and water balance. It is important to retain this division of conceptual labour: salt transport is explained here as a local epithelial mechanism, while the full spatial arrangement and hormonal control of urine concentration follow in the next node. Together these explanations reveal how glomerular and tubular physiology jointly maintain homeostasis.

2. Renal mass balance and quantitative language

A quantitative account starts with a conservation equation. Define F as the amount of a substance filtered per minute, R as the amount reabsorbed per minute, S as the amount secreted per minute and E as the amount excreted per minute. If the tubular system does not synthesize or destroy the substance independently of the defined secretion and reabsorption processes, then E = F − R + S. This equation expresses mass conservation, not a particular molecular transport mechanism. It applies whether the movements involve pumps, carriers, channels, passive diffusion or multiple paths operating simultaneously.

For a freely filterable substance, the filtered load equals its plasma concentration multiplied by GFR. When P is expressed in mg/dL and GFR in mL/min, first convert the concentration by dividing by 100. A plasma glucose concentration of 90 mg/dL is 0.90 mg/mL. At GFR 125 mL/min, the filtered glucose load is 112.5 mg/min. Multiplying by 1440 min/day gives 162,000 mg/day, or 162 g/day. This substantial filtered mass does not imply that the person eats or produces 162 g of additional glucose merely because it passes through the glomerulus. Much of the same circulating glucose can be filtered and returned repeatedly.

For solutes with partial protein binding, total plasma concentration may not represent the concentration available for filtration. A more general expression is F = GFR × P × θ, where θ is a specified filtration ratio relative to the plasma concentration being used. For a small freely filterable marker, θ is approximately one. For a protein-bound substance it may be much lower. This distinction matters when comparing filtered and excreted loads: a calculation using total concentration as if it were freely filterable can falsely suggest reabsorption or conceal secretion.

The excreted load is urinary concentration U multiplied by urine flow V. If urine contains 40 mmol/L of potassium and urine flows at 1.2 mL/min, excretion equals 40 × 0.0012 = 0.048 mmol/min. The corresponding daily amount would be 69.12 mmol if those values were maintained throughout a day. The latter condition is an assumption; a spot sample may not represent variable daily excretion. Concentration is an amount divided by volume, whereas excretion is an amount divided by time. A more concentrated urine can carry a smaller amount of potassium per minute than a dilute urine if its flow is sufficiently low.

Suppose a freely filtered solute enters the tubule at 80 μmol/min and leaves in urine at 12 μmol/min. Net reabsorption equals 68 μmol/min. The calculation does not show that secretion is absent. There could be 78 μmol/min of gross reabsorption and 10 μmol/min of secretion. Both yield exactly the observed excretion. Only segmental sampling, tracer methods or selective perturbations can separate the underlying opposing fluxes. The adjective net is therefore essential whenever a balance measurement alone supports the conclusion.

If filtered load is 20 μmol/min and excretion 35 μmol/min, net secretion is 15 μmol/min under the stated assumptions. A purely reabsorptive process cannot account for more material leaving than enters through filtration. In this case the additional molecules must reach the lumen by secretion or by local formation included in a more detailed balance. A substance such as ammonia requires care because renal metabolism itself contributes to its handling. This chapter focuses mainly on solutes for which the simple balance is adequate; acid–base regulation later treats ammonium generation explicitly.

Fractional excretion expresses excretion as a fraction of the filtered amount: FE = 100 × E/F. If 10 mmol of sodium is excreted from 1000 mmol filtered over the same interval, FE is 1%. This does not mean that urine contains 1% sodium by concentration. Nor does it mean that the body loses 1% of its total sodium stores during the interval. The denominator is the amount filtered. Fractional reabsorption is often estimated as 100 − FE when net secretion is negligible; if secretion occurs, this is a net quantity rather than gross reabsorptive flux.

Clearance gives another view of the same balance. Cx = UxV/Px. A freely filtered marker with no reabsorption or secretion has a clearance equal to GFR. If a freely filtered substance is net reabsorbed, its clearance is below GFR; if it is net secreted, clearance exceeds GFR. These comparisons assume the relevant concentration, stable sampling and an appropriate marker of filtration. A low clearance does not necessarily mean that a substance is strongly protein bound; it may instead reflect tubular recovery. A high clearance does not imply a physical flow of that much urine. It is a virtual plasma volume cleared per unit time.

In examinations, creatinine clearance is often used as a practical estimate of GFR. Substituting it into the FE expression gives FE approximately equal to 100 × Ux × Pcr/(Px × Ucr). Urine flow cancels because the measurements refer to the same sample. Creatinine secretion, non-steady conditions, assay limitations and changing production can compromise interpretation. For a designed question in which an ideal filtration marker is explicitly provided, use that marker rather than assuming creatinine is exact. Do not switch concentration units for the numerator and denominator without converting them consistently.

Segmental delivery can be described as the product of tubular fluid concentration and tubular flow at a specified location. Unlike final urinary excretion, delivery describes how much remains in the lumen at that particular point. If a segment receives 100 units/min of a solute and delivers 60 to the next segment, its net recovery is 40 units/min. If the next segment recovers half of what it receives, its absolute recovery is 30 units/min, not 50. Successive fractions cannot be added unless each has the same denominator.

Consider a hypothetical nephron that filters 100 units of sodium. The proximal tubule recovers 65 units, the loop recovers 25 units and the distal system recovers 9 units. The final output is 1 unit. Expressing the loop's recovery as a proportion of the 35 units it receives gives about 71.4%, whereas as a proportion of the filtered load it is 25%. This distinction explains why a relatively small distal recovery expressed as a percentage of the filtered load can nevertheless represent a large fraction of distal delivery.

Water can be tracked with the same conservation approach. If the proximal tubule recovers 65% of the filtered water, 35% of the initial flow is delivered onward. A solute concentration rises if its fractional removal is less than the fraction of water removed. A solute concentration falls if its fractional removal exceeds water removal. Thus concentration changes along the nephron cannot be interpreted without considering water flux. A rising tubular urea concentration in the proximal tubule is compatible with some urea reabsorption because water removal can be proportionally greater.

A useful way to tackle any renal numerical question is to write the units and label the anatomical boundary before substituting numbers. Ask whether the quantity represents plasma, filtrate at Bowman's space, fluid sampled at an intermediate point or urine. Ask whether the stated concentration is total or freely filterable. Decide whether the problem assumes steady state and absence of metabolism. Only then calculate a load or a clearance. This sequence prevents many errors caused by treating every renal concentration as though it measured the same compartment.

3. Polarised epithelial transport and its energy supply

Tubular epithelial cells are polarised: their membrane facing the lumen has a different molecular composition from the membrane facing the interstitium. The apical membrane encounters the tubular stream, whereas the basolateral membrane encounters interstitial fluid and is closely associated with capillary supply. Tight junctions at the boundaries of adjoining cells both separate these membrane domains and regulate the passage of water and selected ions between cells. Directional movement across the entire epithelium becomes possible because transporters are placed asymmetrically.

A transcellular pathway crosses the apical membrane, cytoplasm and basolateral membrane. A paracellular pathway passes between adjacent cells through junctional structures. These are routes through the epithelial barrier, not categories of energy source. A transcellular movement can be passive, and a paracellular movement can be indirectly driven by energy-consuming transcellular transport elsewhere. In the proximal tubule, relatively permeable junctions enable substantial salt and water recovery. In distal segments, tighter epithelia permit steep gradients and more precise adjustment of the final output.

The sodium–potassium ATPase is central to this organisation. Located primarily at the basolateral membrane of tubular epithelial cells, it exports three sodium ions and imports two potassium ions per ATP hydrolysed. The resulting low intracellular sodium concentration provides a favourable inward sodium gradient at the apical surface. The transport cycle is electrogenic because one net positive charge moves outward per cycle. The membrane potential, however, also depends on ion channels and other transport pathways; it cannot be predicted from pump stoichiometry alone.

The distinction between primary and secondary active transport follows from the immediate energy source. The ATPase directly uses ATP hydrolysis to drive unfavourable ion movement. A sodium–glucose cotransporter uses the favourable movement of sodium to bring glucose into a cell, even when glucose itself would not enter spontaneously. It does not directly hydrolyse ATP. Nevertheless, its operation depends on ATP indirectly because the sodium gradient must be maintained. If mitochondrial energy supply fails, sodium gradients deteriorate and multiple secondary transport pathways may lose their driving force.

An ion moves according to an electrochemical gradient that combines chemical and electrical components. For transfer of a solute from compartment one to compartment two, the change in electrochemical potential is RT ln(C2/C1) + zF(ψ2 − ψ1), where R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature, z the charge and F the Faraday constant. For an uncharged molecule the electrical term is zero. The signs depend on the stated direction. A favourable gradient for a positively charged ion can arise from a concentration difference, a negative electrical potential in the destination, or both.

For a symporter moving one sodium and one uncharged solute inward, the sum of the electrochemical free-energy changes of both transported species must be favourable for spontaneous forward cycling. A two-sodium symporter has two sodium contributions. This gives it a greater potential concentrating power per transported solute molecule under comparable gradients. It does not automatically have a larger maximum throughput or identical affinity. Capacity depends on expression, turnover, kinetics and membrane area. The distinction between driving force and capacity will become important when comparing SGLT2 with SGLT1.

An antiporter exchanges transported species in opposing directions. The proximal sodium–hydrogen exchanger uses the inward sodium gradient to move hydrogen ions toward the lumen. Exchange can be electroneutral even though large numbers of charged particles cross the membrane. Electroneutral does not mean passive and does not mean that no energy is involved. It refers to the net movement of charge per transport cycle. A cotransporter that moves one sodium, one potassium and two chloride ions together is also electroneutral by stoichiometry, although its activity can generate secondary transepithelial voltage through associated channels.

Passive channels permit movement down electrochemical gradients. Aquaporins increase water permeability; potassium channels allow movement determined by potassium's electrochemical gradient. A channel does not impose a universal direction of flux. The same channel can support movement in different directions if the driving conditions reverse. Opening a water channel in a membrane without an osmotic or hydrostatic gradient increases permeability without causing sustained net water transfer. A carrier such as a facilitative glucose transporter also does not supply energy to move glucose uphill, despite being selective and saturable.

Solute transport frequently creates local osmotic differences that promote water movement. In a highly water-permeable epithelium, only modest osmotic differences may persist because water follows the recovered solute. In the proximal tubule, recovery is approximately isosmotic under ordinary conditions. This means that tubular fluid osmolality remains near that of the surrounding fluid, not that water and every individual solute are reabsorbed in identical proportions. Glucose can disappear from the lumen while chloride becomes relatively concentrated even when overall osmolality changes little.

Junctional composition determines which ions can move readily through the paracellular route. Claudins are important components of tight junctions and differ among segments. In a permeable proximal epithelium, luminal chloride enrichment and transepithelial potential can favour chloride recovery and subsequent movement of cations. In the thick ascending limb, a lumen-positive potential helps drive paracellular cation reabsorption. The exact magnitude and even direction of a junctional flux depend on concentration, voltage, permeability and local conditions. Calling a junction leaky is insufficient to predict its behaviour without specifying the relevant solute.

Movement across the epithelial layer and movement into capillaries are related but distinct. After a solute crosses into the interstitium, vascular exchange helps return it to the circulating plasma. Peritubular capillary hydrostatic pressure and oncotic pressure influence net water recovery. Because the glomerular capillary has filtered fluid with relatively little protein, efferent plasma may have a higher protein concentration. This can support downstream capillary uptake, although actual pressures depend on the vascular state. Epithelial transport and vascular removal together limit interstitial accumulation.

Tubular transport requires a continuing oxygen and nutrient supply. Energy demand is particularly significant in segments undertaking large amounts of active salt transport, including the proximal tubule and thick ascending limb. An epithelial cell with abundant mitochondria is anatomically prepared for sustained transport, but mitochondrial number alone does not identify the exact molecules moved. When oxygen availability falls, ATP-dependent transport may weaken, secondary gradients may deteriorate and downstream solute delivery can change. The renal medulla is physiologically interesting because oxygen delivery and oxygen demand have to be balanced with preservation of concentration gradients.

Experimental separation of energy dependence is possible using isolated membrane vesicles or epithelial preparations. If a sodium gradient is artificially established, glucose uptake into a membrane vesicle may proceed without supplying ATP directly to the cotransporter. Removing the sodium gradient eliminates this advantage. Conversely, adding ATP to a preparation without the required membrane transporter cannot restore glucose transport. Such observations distinguish the immediate coupling mechanism from the cellular process that originally established the gradient.

In a whole nephron, inhibiting basolateral sodium–potassium ATPase would influence many transport processes at once. Inhibiting a single apical sodium-coupled glucose transporter has a narrower primary target but can still affect overall sodium recovery and distal delivery. Experimental specificity is therefore graded rather than absolute at the level of the whole organ. To infer a direct molecular effect, measurements should ideally include a local transport readout and a comparison in which other driving forces are controlled.

4. Proximal tubule architecture and nutrient recovery

The proximal tubule begins at the urinary pole of the renal corpuscle and follows a convoluted cortical course before continuing into a straighter segment. It is commonly divided into S1, S2 and S3 portions. These designations refer to anatomical and functional regions whose boundaries may differ among species and experimental descriptions. Proximal epithelial cells have a dense apical brush border, extensive basolateral membrane folds and abundant mitochondria. The brush border increases luminal surface area, while basolateral membrane elaboration supports extensive exchange with interstitial fluid.

In typical mammalian physiology, the proximal tubule recovers roughly two-thirds of filtered sodium and water, a large fraction of bicarbonate, and nearly all freely filtered glucose and amino acids under ordinary physiological conditions. These are approximate fractions, not universal constants or rigidly sequential steps. Different solutes are recovered at different rates along the tubule. The early region encounters a fluid rich in nutrients and bicarbonate, whereas later regions receive fluid whose composition has already been transformed by upstream transport.

Glucose enters proximal cells chiefly through apical sodium–glucose cotransporters. SGLT2, encoded by SLC5A2, operates predominantly in the earlier proximal tubule and has high overall transport capacity. Its usual coupling stoichiometry is one sodium per glucose. SGLT1, encoded by SLC5A1, contributes strongly in later proximal segments and couples two sodium ions to one glucose molecule. It can recover residual glucose at lower luminal concentrations. The distribution and contribution of the transporters depend on species, glucose load and physiological state.

After apical uptake, glucose generally leaves the epithelial cell through basolateral facilitative glucose transport pathways, particularly GLUT2 in much of the proximal tubule, with GLUT1 contributing in some regions and contexts. The glucose exits down its concentration gradient into the interstitium. It can then return to the blood. This is a two-membrane pathway: apical entry and basolateral exit are separate mechanistic steps. It is incorrect to label GLUT2 as the apical sodium-driven glucose transporter or to say that SGLT2 directly hydrolyses ATP to pump glucose.

The serial arrangement of glucose recovery gives the proximal tubule both capacity and reserve. Much of the load is recovered early; the later transporter can retrieve glucose that escapes the first region. In normal experimental animals, reported fractions attributed to SGLT2 often range around 90% or higher, with the remainder recovered largely by SGLT1. Such percentages vary with experimental methods and conditions. The key examination concept is the ordering of high-capacity early recovery and efficient late scavenging, rather than memorisation of a single exact fraction.

Suppose an early proximal segment receives 110 mg/min of glucose and normally recovers 100 mg/min. A downstream segment has capacity for 20 mg/min. It will recover the remaining 10 mg/min, and urinary glucose is negligible if no other sources or pathways intervene. After a partial reduction in early recovery to 85 mg/min, the downstream segment receives 25 mg/min and can recover 20, leaving 5 mg/min for excretion. The increase in urinary loss is smaller than the primary decline of 15 mg/min because downstream recovery compensates for part of it. The numbers are constructed for reasoning and are not estimates of actual regional human transport maxima.

Amino acids are recovered through several families of transport proteins rather than a single universal amino-acid pump. Different transport systems distinguish neutral, acidic, basic and imino amino acids and may use sodium, proton gradients or exchange with other amino acids. Basolateral pathways complete their transfer toward the circulation. Specific genetic alterations can selectively impair the recovery of a class of amino acids, demonstrating the molecular specificity of tubular handling. A generalised failure of proximal transport, by contrast, may produce simultaneous losses of glucose, phosphate, bicarbonate and amino acids.

Small filtered peptides and proteins can be recovered through receptor-mediated endocytosis involving the megalin–cubilin system at the brush border. Internalised cargo may be delivered to intracellular compartments for processing and degradation, with constituent amino acids subsequently returned to the circulation. This pathway is distinct from sodium-coupled uptake of free amino acids. It has finite capacity and depends on cellular trafficking. An elevated urinary low-molecular-weight protein concentration can therefore reflect impaired tubular uptake, while large amounts of a poorly filtered plasma protein may indicate altered glomerular permeability or multiple contributing mechanisms.

The proximal tubule also reabsorbs phosphate, bicarbonate, lactate and other organic solutes. Apical sodium–phosphate cotransporters contribute to phosphate recovery; their abundance is regulated by hormonal signals discussed in the electrolyte node. Lactate recovery is important because lactate is a metabolically useful intermediate rather than merely a waste molecule. Whether a solute is recovered depends on physiological value, filtered availability and transport proteins, not simply on whether it is organic or inorganic.

A striking feature of the proximal tubule is that its epithelium can both transport glucose and participate in glucose metabolism. Renal gluconeogenesis can contribute glucose to the circulation, particularly in certain metabolic states. This does not invalidate the simple filtered glucose balance used in introductory calculations, provided that one explicitly treats locally produced glucose released basolaterally as a separate metabolic contribution rather than as glucose secreted into the lumen. A measurement of whole-organ glucose exchange across renal arterial and venous blood is not identical to a measurement of tubular glucose recovery.

Consider how micropuncture observations can support the spatial arrangement of glucose transport. Samples from the early proximal lumen show a progressive decline in glucose delivery. Later samples may show very low delivery under normal conditions. If an early transporter is selectively impaired, more glucose reaches the later segment. A compensatory decline in glucose delivery across the late segment indicates retained recovery capacity. To distinguish transporter activity from a change in water flow, calculate glucose delivery or normalise glucose concentration to a non-reabsorbed filtration marker.

The proximal tubule therefore combines high-capacity salt and nutrient transport with specialised pathways for filtered proteins and metabolic substrates. Its recovery protects the internal environment while reducing downstream workload. It should not be described as the only segment important for urine formation: its major role is bulk processing, and the final output is determined by the entire nephron. A student who understands how the brush border, basolateral ATPase, cotransporters and capillary exchange work together can derive the consequences of many transporter disturbances without memorising isolated lists.

5. Proximal salt, bicarbonate and water transport

The recovery of sodium and water involves multiple pathways that change in relative importance along the proximal tubule. At the early proximal apical membrane, sodium enters through cotransporters for glucose, amino acids, phosphate and other organic solutes, and through the sodium–hydrogen exchanger NHE3. Basolateral sodium–potassium ATPase maintains low intracellular sodium and provides a common energetic foundation for these apparently different pathways. Because the filtrate is rich in sodium salts, a large amount of sodium recovery can be distributed among several molecular routes rather than requiring one transporter to carry the entire load.

Bicarbonate recovery illustrates how epithelial transport may conserve a solute without transporting its original ionic form across the apical membrane. NHE3 brings sodium into the cell and transfers hydrogen ions toward the tubular lumen. Secreted hydrogen ions combine with filtered bicarbonate to form carbonic acid. Brush-border carbonic anhydrase accelerates the conversion of carbonic acid to carbon dioxide and water. Carbon dioxide crosses the apical membrane, where intracellular carbonic anhydrase accelerates the reverse hydration reaction. The products include a hydrogen ion and bicarbonate.

Intracellular bicarbonate can leave across the basolateral membrane through sodium–bicarbonate cotransport, especially NBCe1 in the proximal tubule. The hydrogen ion can participate again in the apical exchange cycle. The net result is recovery of filtered bicarbonate and associated sodium, despite the fact that much of the original luminal bicarbonate entered the epithelial cell indirectly in the form of carbon dioxide. Carbonic anhydrase accelerates reversible reactions; it is not an ATP-driven pump and does not independently determine the net direction of the complete epithelial process.

The distinction between filtered bicarbonate reclamation and net acid excretion is fundamental. In the recovery cycle, the hydrogen ion secreted into the lumen is effectively recycled and reacts with bicarbonate that was already present in the filtrate. The amount of bicarbonate returned to the circulation balances filtered bicarbonate removal. By contrast, excretion of acid bound to phosphate or as ammonium can be associated with addition of new bicarbonate to blood. The quantitative implications of these processes belong to the acid–base node, but the mechanistic distinction is needed here to avoid counting every secreted hydrogen ion as a net acid loss.

Inhibition of carbonic anhydrase slows key reactions supporting bicarbonate recovery. More bicarbonate can remain in tubular fluid, and its accompanying sodium and water contribute to downstream delivery and urinary solute output. Other nephron segments can partially compensate. The effect therefore cannot be predicted from the proximal transporter alone over every time scale. At the level of a controlled acute experiment, the primary expectation is reduced proximal bicarbonate recovery and increased downstream bicarbonate delivery, rather than a direct blockade of all sodium transport.

Early proximal recovery of bicarbonate, organic solutes and water changes the composition of the residual luminal fluid. Chloride concentration tends to become relatively enriched as other anions are preferentially removed. In later proximal regions, this enrichment can favour chloride movement from lumen toward interstitium through transcellular and paracellular pathways. Sodium can follow favourable electrical and chemical gradients across permeable junctions. This sequence helps explain why a single segment can exhibit different dominant modes of transport at different positions.

Chloride movement is not governed by concentration alone, because chloride carries a negative charge. Early and late segments may show different transepithelial voltages as a result of electrogenic nutrient transport and diffusion potentials. A lumen-negative voltage can oppose entry of an anion into that lumen from the interstitium, whereas a lumen-positive voltage has different effects on cations and anions. For a prediction, specify both direction and sign. The paracellular route is not a hole through which every ion moves equally; its selectivity depends on junctional properties.

Water follows solute recovery through pathways of high hydraulic permeability. Aquaporin-1 is expressed in proximal epithelial membranes and facilitates water movement through the cells. Water also passes via paracellular routes. Solute transport establishes small local osmotic gradients, and the high water permeability permits substantial net fluid recovery with comparatively modest sustained differences in total osmolality. This process is called near-isosmotic reabsorption. It is not a requirement that measured osmolality be identical at every microscopic point within an epithelium.

A teaching example makes the distinction between total osmolality and individual concentration clear. Assume that an early tubule receives 100 arbitrary volume units and 100 arbitrary chloride units, so its initial chloride concentration is one unit per volume. After it recovers 40 volume units and 25 chloride units, 60 volume units and 75 chloride units remain. Chloride concentration is now 1.25 times its initial value despite a net recovery of chloride. If glucose delivery instead falls from 100 to 10 units over the same region, its concentration falls to one-sixth of the initial value. The two solutes behave differently under the identical water removal.

The pressure environment around the proximal tubule also matters. Water movement from interstitium into peritubular capillaries is favoured by a relatively low capillary hydrostatic pressure and an oncotic force generated by plasma proteins. An increase in glomerular filtration fraction, with other factors held constant, can raise efferent plasma protein concentration and promote downstream uptake. Changes in renal vascular resistance and interstitial pressure can modify this relationship. The phrase glomerulotubular balance describes the tendency of proximal recovery to change in parallel with filtered load, maintaining a relatively stable fractional recovery across a physiological range; it is not a claim of perfect proportionality.

A simple comparison clarifies the consequences. At GFR 100 mL/min and fractional proximal water recovery 65%, distal delivery from the proximal tubule is 35 mL/min. If GFR rises to 120 mL/min and fractional recovery remains 65%, absolute proximal recovery rises from 65 to 78 mL/min and delivery increases to 42 mL/min. If proximal recovery stayed fixed at 65 mL/min instead, downstream delivery would be 55 mL/min. The difference demonstrates why coupling between filtration and recovery buffers the workload of distal segments without eliminating it.

Urea is filtered readily and undergoes complex reabsorption and recycling. In the proximal tubule, some urea can leave tubular fluid passively as water recovery increases its luminal concentration. This is a clear example of an apparent concentration increase coexisting with net solute recovery. Detailed medullary urea recycling belongs in the next chapter. At this stage, the lesson is that filtered waste is not necessarily transported in only one direction; renal handling reflects permeability and the gradients created by other transported substances.

The proximal tubule can respond to changes in sodium delivery, flow and hormonal environment. Its transport rates reflect the combined availability of substrate, electrochemical gradients, membrane transporters and capillary uptake. A single set of fractional recovery values cannot describe every physiological state. In a student problem, use the stated values; in interpreting an experiment, ask what conditions produced them. This separates a helpful physiological reference from a rigid assertion about all nephrons and all times.

6. Organic solute secretion and bidirectional handling

Tubular secretion supplements filtration by transferring material into the lumen from the interstitial or cellular compartment. The proximal tubule is a major site of secretion of many organic anions and organic cations. Some are endogenous metabolites; others are foreign compounds that the body has absorbed. Secretion is particularly significant for substances whose filtration is limited by binding to plasma proteins. A protein-bound molecule generally cannot move through the glomerular barrier as readily as its unbound form, but the free molecules can be transported by proximal epithelial cells, allowing further dissociation from the bound pool.

Organic anion transport illustrates the organisation of a multistep secondary and tertiary active pathway. OAT1 and OAT3 on the basolateral side of proximal tubule cells can exchange extracellular organic anions with intracellular dicarboxylates, notably alpha-ketoglutarate. Intracellular dicarboxylate availability is supported by sodium-dependent transport, which itself relies on the sodium gradient maintained by sodium–potassium ATPase. The sequence links ATP hydrolysis to an organic solute exchange through intermediate gradients. It is important not to describe the basolateral OAT as a direct ATPase.

Once an organic anion enters the cell, apical exit may occur through several transport proteins, including members of the multidrug resistance-associated protein family. Some apical efflux pathways directly use ATP; others utilise exchange or electrochemical gradients. The exact combination depends on the molecule and species. A complete secretory mechanism must account for both entry from the blood-facing side and exit into the lumen. Increasing one step without maintaining the other may lead to intracellular accumulation instead of proportionately increasing transepithelial secretion.

Organic cation handling involves other transport families. OCT2 is an important basolateral organic cation transporter in human proximal tubule cells. A favourable electrical gradient can assist uptake of positively charged substrates. Apical MATE transporters can exchange organic cations for protons, supporting movement into the lumen when the relevant gradients favour it. Other apical transporters also participate for particular substrates. The names organic cation and organic anion describe molecular charge classes; they do not imply that every member shares a single transporter with all other molecules of its class.

Para-aminohippurate, commonly abbreviated PAH, is a useful experimental example of a filtered and secreted organic anion. At sufficiently low plasma concentrations, the kidney extracts a large fraction of available PAH during one passage through its vasculature. Clearance therefore approximates effective renal plasma flow, as described in the filtration chapter. Extraction is not perfectly complete, and PAH clearance is not an exact measurement of total renal plasma flow without correction. At high plasma concentrations, secretory carriers approach saturation, and the fraction of PAH delivered to the kidney that is extracted falls.

Consider a simplified situation with GFR 100 mL/min, free filtration of a marker at 0.1 mg/mL and secretion of an additional 40 mg/min. The filtered load equals 10 mg/min and excretion is 50 mg/min if reabsorption is absent. Clearance is 500 mL/min. If plasma concentration rises fivefold while the secretory component has already reached its 40 mg/min maximum, filtered load rises to 50 mg/min, excretion rises to 90 mg/min and clearance falls to 180 mL/min. The example shows why excretion rate may increase while clearance decreases.

Competition between organic substrates provides another experimental approach. Suppose two anions use overlapping basolateral transport pathways. Increasing the concentration of one may reduce the transport of the other, resulting in lower clearance of the competing molecule. The observation supports an interaction at a shared transport system but is not sufficient alone to identify a specific protein, because altered binding, metabolism or filtration might contribute. Evidence from expression systems, selective inhibitors and controlled concentration measurements strengthens the molecular attribution.

Creatinine is often treated as a filtration marker because it is filtered and has relatively modest additional secretion. That secretion explains why creatinine clearance may overestimate actual GFR. The difference depends on physiology and measurement conditions. In a hypothetical paired clearance experiment, an ideal filtration marker has clearance 100 mL/min and creatinine has clearance 115 mL/min. The discrepancy is consistent with net creatinine secretion but would also require attention to marker properties and assay errors. One should not infer that creatinine is perfectly inert in the nephron.

Uric acid handling provides a different pattern. In humans, urate is filtered and undergoes both reabsorption and secretion in the proximal tubule via specialised transporters. The resulting net urinary fraction is only part of the filtered load. A decrease in urate excretion need not mean that filtration fell, and an increase need not mean that secretion alone rose. A change in apical reabsorption could explain either observation. Comparative physiology also matters because urate metabolism and its importance as a nitrogenous end product differ among species.

Tubular secretion is often conflated with the production of urine, as if the kidney creates all excreted waste by secretion. In reality, a freely filtered substance can leave in urine without ever being secreted. Another substance may enter through filtration, undergo partial reabsorption and be secreted again. A third may be generated within the kidney, as occurs in ammonium handling. The appropriate mass balance depends on the origin and pathways of the molecule. A useful answer names the substance, the membrane or segment, the direction of flux and the energetic mechanism rather than relying on the word secretion alone.

Transporter saturation can occur in secretory pathways as well as reabsorptive pathways. The shape of a secretion curve differs in its relationship to excretion: once secretory capacity is limited, the additional excretion from secretion approaches a plateau, but filtration can continue increasing with plasma concentration. Excretion therefore generally continues to rise, with its high-concentration slope approaching the filtration contribution if other processes are negligible. This is the counterpart of the reabsorptive glucose titration curve, which will be examined later.

Secretory transport can influence the kidney's own exposure to substances. Basolateral uptake that outpaces apical removal can increase intracellular concentrations, whereas effective apical efflux reduces accumulation. This mechanistic relationship is useful for understanding renal cell biology and experimental toxicology. It does not justify predicting individual drug doses or clinical outcomes from transporter names alone. At examination level, focus on how transport polarity and driving forces determine net epithelial movement.

7. Loop of Henle: water removal and selective salt recovery

The loop of Henle receives tubular fluid that has already undergone substantial proximal recovery. Its characteristic descending and ascending arrangement is important for the spatial organisation of renal concentrating function. The loop is not one physiologically uniform segment. Thin descending limbs, thin ascending limbs where present, and thick ascending limbs have different epithelial properties. Cortical and juxtamedullary nephrons also differ in loop length and in the extent to which their segments reach deep into the medulla.

Many thin descending limb regions have relatively high water permeability associated with aquaporin-1 expression. Water can leave the lumen if the surrounding medullary interstitium has higher effective osmolality. This reduces tubular volume and raises the concentration of solutes that remain. However, descending limbs are heterogeneous; certain deeper regions have low water permeability and varying solute permeabilities. A simple introductory diagram showing water leaving a descending limb captures an important principle but should not be treated as a uniform description of every thin-limb subsegment.

Thin ascending limbs in long-loop nephrons have low water permeability and can allow passive movement of sodium chloride under favourable gradients. Their contribution depends on local solute concentrations and the medullary environment. They do not use the same high-capacity apical NKCC2-dependent active salt transport that characterises the thick ascending limb. The distinction between thin and thick ascending portions is necessary when considering oxygen demand, medullary concentrating mechanisms and the effects of specific transporter defects.

The thick ascending limb has a relatively water-impermeable epithelium and recovers substantial sodium chloride. A key apical transporter is the sodium–potassium–two chloride cotransporter NKCC2, encoded by SLC12A1. The transport cycle brings one sodium ion, one potassium ion and two chloride ions into the epithelial cell. The net charge transferred by this particular cycle is zero. Basolateral sodium–potassium ATPase exports sodium into the interstitium, and chloride leaves through basolateral pathways including ClC-K channels with the accessory protein barttin.

Potassium availability is important for continued NKCC2 operation. Some potassium taken up by the apical cotransporter returns to the lumen through the ROMK channel. This recycling supplies luminal potassium for repeated cotransport and contributes to a lumen-positive electrical potential relative to the interstitium. Potassium recycling in the thick ascending limb is mechanistically different from net potassium secretion in principal cells of the distal nephron. The same channel family can support different physiological functions because the associated transport systems and electrochemical conditions differ.

The lumen-positive transepithelial voltage favours paracellular movement of positively charged ions toward the interstitium. Calcium and magnesium recovery in the thick ascending limb depends substantially on this driving force and on the permeability of specific junctional pathways, including those involving claudin-16 and claudin-19. The pathway is indirect: NKCC2 does not transport calcium or magnesium in its four-ion cycle. An intervention that weakens potassium recycling or NKCC2 can nevertheless alter divalent cation recovery by changing voltage and the integrated epithelial transport state.

Water impermeability means that salt removal from the thick ascending limb is not accompanied by proportional water movement. As sodium chloride leaves, the tubular fluid tends to become more dilute. The interstitium receives solute, which contributes to the osmotic gradient used elsewhere for water recovery. This is why the ascending limb is commonly called a diluting segment. Dilution of luminal fluid and accumulation of solute in the interstitium are two consequences of the same local transport process. They do not require the transporter to move water in the opposite direction.

Consider a model thick ascending segment that receives fluid at 10 mL/min carrying 3 milliosmoles per minute of osmotically active particles. The input osmolality, approximating litres of fluid as kilograms of water, is 300 mOsm/L. If 1.5 milliosmoles per minute are removed while water flow remains 10 mL/min, the output contains 150 mOsm/L. In an equally salt-reabsorbing but water-permeable segment that loses 5 mL/min of water, output concentration would remain about 300 mOsm/L. The contrast demonstrates why the ratio of solute permeability to water permeability matters more than the amount of salt removal alone.

The thick ascending limb recovers roughly a fifth to a quarter of filtered sodium in many standard physiological descriptions. The actual value depends on filtered load, species and hormonal or neural state. Since the proximal tubule has already recovered a large fraction, the thick ascending limb may retrieve most of the sodium delivered to it despite recovering a smaller fraction of the original filtered amount. Accurate interpretation again depends on naming the denominator.

Loop diuretics are useful mechanistic probes because they inhibit NKCC2. A primary effect is reduced sodium chloride recovery in the thick ascending limb, causing increased distal sodium delivery and reduced generation of the medullary osmotic gradient. Reduced lumen-positive potential can decrease paracellular calcium and magnesium recovery. Downstream transport and endocrine responses modify final urine composition. The effect on urine flow involves both retained luminal solutes and impaired water-concentrating conditions; it is not simply direct opening of an apical water channel in the ascending limb.

At the end of the cortical thick ascending limb, the macula densa forms a specialised epithelial region in contact with the juxtaglomerular apparatus. It senses luminal salt delivery through transport-dependent mechanisms involving NKCC2. Changes in sodium chloride delivery contribute to feedback that adjusts glomerular haemodynamics and renin release. This connection links tubular recovery to filtration: modifying upstream transport can change the signal delivered to the macula densa even if plasma composition remains initially unchanged.

A useful experimental comparison is to perfuse two isolated thick ascending segments with identical luminal sodium chloride concentration but different potassium availability. Reducing luminal potassium sufficiently can impair NKCC2 cycling, but the extent of the effect also depends on potassium recycling and the preparation. In a separate experiment, increasing water permeability artificially would undermine the ability of salt recovery to dilute the lumen. These perturbations test different causal requirements: adequate substrates for the cotransporter and a low water permeability for dilution.

Transport in the thick ascending limb is energetically demanding because active sodium extrusion must support sustained net salt movement. The outer medulla has relatively limited oxygen delivery compared with the workload of some of its epithelial segments. An experimental reduction in ATP production may therefore affect salt recovery and consequently distal delivery. This illustrates the physiological link between renal blood supply, epithelial energy metabolism and urine formation without requiring a detailed clinical model of renal injury.

The full concentrating system requires more than a thick ascending limb. Countercurrent arrangement, medullary blood flow, urea recycling and regulated collecting duct water permeability are all essential components in mammals. The next node examines how those elements operate together. Here the essential conclusion is local: an epithelium that transports salt while restricting water movement can produce dilute tubular fluid and a more concentrated surrounding interstitium. This principle becomes the foundation for understanding the renal medulla.

8. Distal convoluted tubule and transition to the collecting system

The distal convoluted tubule receives fluid that has already lost most of its original sodium and water. It begins after the macula densa region and continues toward the connecting tubule. Its cells lack the extensive brush border characteristic of proximal cells but possess substantial basolateral membrane elaboration and mitochondria. These features are consistent with active ion handling. The segment is commonly divided functionally into early and late portions, with transporter expression gradually changing rather than necessarily switching at a single sharp anatomical boundary.

In the early distal convoluted tubule, the sodium–chloride cotransporter NCC, encoded by SLC12A3, moves one sodium ion and one chloride ion into the cell across the apical membrane. The cycle is electroneutral. Basolateral sodium–potassium ATPase supports the sodium gradient, and chloride leaves via basolateral pathways. NCC differs from NKCC2 both in stoichiometry and segmental location. NKCC2 requires luminal potassium as one of its substrates and predominates in the thick ascending limb; NCC does not directly transport potassium.

The early distal convolution has relatively low water permeability. Continued sodium chloride recovery without equivalent water removal further dilutes tubular fluid. This is why the distal convoluted tubule and thick ascending limb are both described as diluting regions, despite employing different principal apical cotransporters. The term diluting does not mean adding water to the lumen; dilution can occur because osmotically active particles are removed faster than water.

Thiazide-sensitive transport provides a way to distinguish distal NCC from loop NKCC2. In an isolated preparation, inhibition of NCC reduces coupled sodium chloride recovery in the distal convolution. In an intact nephron, increased salt delivery beyond the inhibited region can alter the workload of the connecting tubule and collecting system. The magnitude of final sodium loss depends on compensation. A comparison of loop and distal transporter inhibition should therefore separate the primary local effects from downstream changes in electrolyte and water balance.

Calcium is handled by specialised transcellular pathways in the distal nephron. Apical TRPV5 channels contribute to calcium entry in relevant distal and connecting segments. Intracellular calcium-binding proteins help buffer and facilitate intracellular movement, while basolateral sodium–calcium exchange and calcium ATPase contribute to extrusion into the interstitium. This active transcellular organisation differs from the predominantly paracellular calcium movement in the thick ascending limb. Hormonal regulation, particularly by parathyroid hormone, becomes important in the electrolyte node.

Magnesium recovery in the distal convoluted tubule depends in part on apical TRPM6-associated pathways. Since much of filtered magnesium has already been recovered upstream, the distal segment has a strategically important role in determining final magnesium excretion. A relatively small absolute change in distal magnesium transport can therefore have a significant effect on the fraction excreted. This illustrates a general principle of final control: a segment responsible for only a modest portion of total recovery can dominate the variation in final excretion.

The late distal convolution and connecting tubule introduce more prominent electrogenic sodium transport through epithelial sodium channels, along with potassium secretory pathways. Their boundaries and transporter expression vary across species. It is best to regard the transition as a change in physiological organisation from predominantly coupled sodium chloride recovery toward a system in which sodium entry can create an electrical gradient affecting separate ions. The collecting duct continues this latter form of regulation.

A physiological question may ask which transporter directly accounts for sodium chloride uptake in an early distal segment. NCC is the most appropriate answer under ordinary mammalian assumptions. If the question instead asks which membrane protein creates a lumen-negative potential by electrogenic sodium entry in a principal cell, ENaC is more appropriate. Both support sodium recovery, but the mechanism and electrical consequences differ. In an experiment measuring transepithelial potential, those differences can provide useful clues.

Suppose 5% of a filtered sodium load reaches a distal control region. If this region normally recovers 80% of its input, final sodium excretion is 1% of the filtered load. A reduction of local recovery from 80% to 60% doubles final excretion from 1% to 2%, even though the absolute change is only one percentage point of filtered sodium. The calculation shows why distal transport is important despite its relatively small contribution to the bulk sodium recovery performed by the kidney.

The distal convolution is exposed to changes in luminal composition arising from altered filtration, proximal recovery and loop salt transport. Its response depends on transporter abundance and activity, as well as regulatory input. The WNK–SPAK/OSR1 signalling network is one important regulator of NCC phosphorylation and activity. At NET/SET depth, the central principle is that a kinase cascade can change transporter function and therefore sodium delivery to downstream segments. Detailed mutation-specific pathways are unnecessary for understanding the main physiological consequences.

Distal transport must be integrated with the overall renal sodium and potassium balance. Changes in NCC activity can alter sodium delivery to ENaC-expressing cells, where electrogenic sodium uptake supports potassium secretion. Potassium homeostasis may therefore be influenced indirectly by a sodium chloride transporter that does not transport potassium itself. The important causal chain is upstream sodium recovery, downstream sodium availability, membrane voltage and potassium secretory pathways. This chain also explains why a change in urinary potassium cannot automatically be assigned to a defect in a potassium channel.

The distal convolution and connecting tubule prepare the filtrate for its final processing. Their transport changes affect both the quantity of ions delivered downstream and the degree of dilution. They also provide a transition from high-capacity constitutive transport toward more tightly regulated adjustment. With this foundation, the student can understand why final urine composition is sensitive to hormones, luminal flow and the relative activity of different cell types in the collecting system.

9. Connecting tubule and collecting duct: final adjustment

The connecting tubule and collecting duct system receive a small but physiologically decisive portion of the filtered sodium and water. Unlike the early proximal tubule, where bulk recovery proceeds under relatively constitutive conditions, the late nephron responds strongly to endocrine state, luminal composition and flow. Its importance is best understood by examining the small difference between two large quantities: the amount filtered and the amount recovered. Changing that difference by a small absolute amount can change final excretion severalfold.

Principal cells in the connecting tubule and collecting duct support sodium reabsorption, potassium secretion and regulated water recovery. Sodium enters through apical epithelial sodium channels, commonly abbreviated ENaC, and is extruded by the basolateral sodium–potassium ATPase. ENaC permits movement of sodium down its electrochemical gradient. It is not a cotransporter for chloride and it does not directly hydrolyse ATP. The pump sustains the favourable intracellular sodium concentration and indirectly supports continued channel-mediated entry.

Electrogenic sodium entry removes positive charge from the tubular lumen relative to the interstitium. The resulting lumen-negative transepithelial voltage can favour secretion of positively charged potassium ions into the lumen. Basolateral sodium–potassium ATPase also supplies intracellular potassium. Apical ROMK channels and, under some conditions, large-conductance potassium channels known as BK channels allow potassium movement along its electrochemical gradient. The relationship between sodium absorption and potassium secretion is not a one-for-one obligatory exchange through ENaC itself. They are separate processes coupled through electrical and chemical conditions.

Aldosterone influences principal cell transport by acting through the mineralocorticoid receptor and regulating proteins involved in sodium entry and extrusion. Increased ENaC activity and increased sodium–potassium ATPase function can promote sodium recovery and potassium secretion when sufficient distal sodium and flow are available. The effects depend on time, hormonal context and transport capacity. The full regulatory pathway and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system are treated in node 7.F.06; the present emphasis is the cellular effector mechanism.

Luminal flow influences potassium secretion. Increasing flow can help maintain a favourable gradient by carrying secreted potassium away, and flow-sensitive mechanisms may increase BK-channel activity. However, high flow alone does not guarantee high potassium excretion. If sodium delivery is very low, ENaC activity is suppressed, or the relevant potassium pathways are unavailable, the response may differ. A well-designed examination question specifies the relevant conditions or asks for the expected primary effect with other variables held constant.

Water permeability in principal cells is regulated independently of sodium entry. Vasopressin acts through basolateral V2 receptors and signalling pathways that promote the insertion of aquaporin-2 channels into the apical membrane. Water can then cross the cell and leave through basolateral aquaporins, including AQP3 and AQP4 depending on location. Net water movement depends on the osmotic driving force between lumen and interstitium. AQP2 is not a pump; it increases permeability rather than creating the medullary gradient. A detailed account of this regulation appears in the water balance chapter.

Intercalated cells contribute especially to acid–base transport. Alpha intercalated cells possess apical proton secretory machinery including H+-ATPase and, in some circumstances, H+/K+-ATPase. Basolateral bicarbonate exit through AE1 helps return bicarbonate to extracellular fluid. Beta intercalated cells can secrete bicarbonate through apical pendrin, a chloride–bicarbonate exchanger. Additional intercalated-cell phenotypes exist. The labels alpha and beta summarise different polarity patterns, not a complete description of every cell in the collecting system.

The H+/K+-ATPase illustrates how potassium recovery can occur in the late nephron when physiological circumstances favour conservation. The collecting system is often introduced as a site of potassium secretion, but it is more accurate to describe it as a regulated site of potassium handling. Principal cells and intercalated cells can support opposing fluxes. Their net contribution depends on dietary potassium, hormonal signals and other conditions. For mass balance, final urine reports the sum of these movements, not the activity of one cell type.

Chloride follows sodium transport through more than one route. Paracellular chloride movement can be influenced by the transepithelial electrical gradient, while intercalated-cell pathways such as pendrin contribute to chloride recovery in particular contexts. Sodium chloride balance therefore depends on coordinated activity across neighbouring epithelial cell types, not merely on an identical cotransporter in every region. A change in one cell's transport can alter the gradients acting on another cell.

The late nephron also reveals why the ionic composition of final urine cannot be predicted solely from plasma composition. Under low sodium intake, renal sodium excretion may be markedly reduced while potassium excretion remains necessary to balance intake. After a water load, urine may be dilute despite ongoing sodium recovery. After an acid load, increased renal acid elimination can alter urinary ammonium and titratable acid without requiring plasma sodium to change in the same direction. The kidney adjusts multiple outputs through interacting but partly separable control mechanisms.

Consider a simplified distal segment receiving 4 mmol/min of sodium and delivering 0.15 mmol/min beyond it. A reduction in recovery by 0.15 mmol/min doubles final delivery to 0.30 mmol/min if nothing downstream compensates. The absolute change is less than 4% of segmental input, yet the output doubles. This type of numerical reasoning is especially relevant to the distal nephron, where the final excreted fraction is small relative to upstream transport.

A comparison of ENaC inhibition and ROMK inhibition clarifies primary versus secondary effects. Inhibiting ENaC directly reduces apical sodium entry and tends to reduce the lumen-negative voltage that favours potassium secretion. Inhibiting ROMK directly reduces a potassium conductance, but the final response depends on other potassium channels and upstream events. In the thick ascending limb, ROMK inhibition also interferes with potassium recycling and consequently salt recovery. A transporter name alone therefore cannot identify the entire phenotype unless the location is specified.

The collecting system ends in papillary ducts that discharge into the calyces. At this point, epithelial processing has determined the fluid that proceeds through the urinary tract. Changes during transit and storage are not the principal mechanism of mammalian urine formation; the great majority of regulated composition changes occur before the fluid enters the renal pelvis. 10. Glucose transport maximum, renal threshold and splay

A transporter can be highly effective without having unlimited capacity. Each transporter molecule undergoes a finite number of cycles per unit time, and the epithelial surface contains a finite number of such molecules. As substrate delivery rises, total transport can increase until the available pathways operate near their collective capacity. The transport maximum, often written Tm, is the approximate highest sustainable transport rate for a substance under specified conditions. It is expressed as an amount per unit time rather than a concentration.

Glucose provides the classical example of saturable renal reabsorption because it is freely filtered and normally recovered almost completely from the proximal tubule. With GFR fixed, filtered glucose load rises linearly with plasma glucose concentration. For plasma concentration P in mg/dL and GFR in mL/min, F = GFR × P/100 in mg/min. At P = 100 mg/dL and GFR = 125 mL/min, the filtered load is 125 mg/min. At P = 300 mg/dL, the load is 375 mg/min. The straight filtered-load line on a titration plot represents filtration rather than saturation of a carrier.

In an idealised homogeneous system with a sharp maximal reabsorption rate of 375 mg/min, glucose would be recovered completely up to a filtered load of 375 mg/min. At GFR 125 mL/min, the predicted plasma concentration at that intersection is 300 mg/dL. Above the intersection, reabsorbed load remains at 375 mg/min, and urinary glucose output increases as the filtered load exceeds that capacity. This is a useful mathematical model, but it is not an accurate description of the onset of glycosuria in every human.

The renal threshold is the plasma concentration at which urinary glucose becomes detectable under the measurement conditions. In many textbook human examples, glucose may start appearing in urine at plasma concentrations around 180–200 mg/dL, lower than the idealised Tm/GFR intersection. Neither this range nor the illustrative maximum of 375 mg/min is universal. Transport capacity, filtration, nephron heterogeneity, assay sensitivity and other factors affect the observed relationship. Threshold and Tm therefore describe different properties.

The curved transition between nearly complete glucose recovery and the maximal reabsorptive plateau is called splay. Several mechanisms contribute, including variation in individual nephron filtration rates and transport capacities, gradual saturation of transporter kinetics and regional differences in recovery. Some nephrons may begin to deliver glucose beyond the proximal tubule before the total renal reabsorptive machinery reaches its collective maximum. The whole-kidney curve is the summed behaviour of many heterogeneous units.

Imagine two equal filtration units, each filtering 50 mL/min. One has a glucose recovery capacity of 100 mg/min, while the other has a capacity of 150 mg/min. At plasma glucose 200 mg/dL each filters 100 mg/min. Both can recover their loads. At 240 mg/dL each filters 120 mg/min. The first unit excretes 20 mg/min, but the second still recovers its full load. Total filtered load is 240 mg/min, lower than the combined capacity of 250 mg/min, yet urinary glucose appears because unused capacity in one nephron cannot directly recover glucose from another nephron's lumen.

At 300 mg/dL, each unit filters 150 mg/min. The first recovers 100 and the second recovers 150, so combined reabsorption reaches 250 mg/min and excretion is 50 mg/min. The onset of glucose excretion occurred before the combined Tm was reached. This model demonstrates how nephron heterogeneity creates splay even if the individual recovery curves have perfectly sharp transitions. Real carrier kinetics add further smoothness, and nephrons are not restricted to two capacity classes.

The excretion curve is calculated as the vertical difference between filtered load and reabsorbed load when secretion is absent. At low plasma glucose, those curves nearly overlap, so excretion is near zero. During splay, the reabsorbed curve begins to diverge from the filtration line and excretion rises gradually. At high plasma glucose, reabsorption approaches a plateau and excretion becomes approximately linear with the same slope as filtered load. The slope of the high-concentration excretion line is proportional to GFR when plasma concentration is the horizontal variable.

The same information can be expressed in terms of clearance. If glucose excretion is negligible, glucose clearance is near zero despite ongoing filtration. At very high glucose concentrations in the ideal model, clearance equals (GFR × P − Tm)/P = GFR − Tm/P when P is expressed in compatible units. As concentration increases, the Tm/P term becomes relatively small and glucose clearance approaches GFR from below. Clearance can never exceed GFR in this particular model because no secretion is included.

A distinction between apparent threshold and intrinsic transporter affinity prevents a common conceptual error. Renal threshold is an observed whole-organ concentration at which urine becomes detectably glucose-positive. Affinity describes properties of molecular binding or transport kinetics under specified conditions. A transporter with relatively favourable affinity may still operate at a low maximum rate if few molecules are expressed. Conversely, abundant high-capacity transporters can recover large loads. A threshold measurement alone cannot establish the kinetic constants of SGLT1 or SGLT2.

Changes in GFR alter the filtered glucose load at any given plasma concentration. If maximal reabsorptive capacity remains 300 mg/min, an ideal system with GFR 100 mL/min has a theoretical intersection at 300 mg/dL, whereas one with GFR 150 mL/min has an intersection at 200 mg/dL. In a real kidney, changes in filtration may be accompanied by changes in tubular growth and transport, so the fixed-Tm assumption must be stated. This example is an exercise in controlled variables rather than a diagnostic rule for individual patients.

Selective impairment of SGLT2 can decrease the overall capacity for proximal glucose recovery and permit glucose excretion at lower plasma concentrations. Downstream SGLT1-mediated recovery limits how much glucose is lost, and the magnitude of the effect depends on filtration and residual transport. If a question reports glycosuria in an individual with normal plasma glucose, reduced tubular transport capacity is one possible explanation. If both plasma glucose and urinary glucose are elevated, filtered delivery may exceed recovery capacity. The same urinary observation can therefore arise from different causal mechanisms.

A graded-load experiment can distinguish an increase in filtered substrate from a decrease in Tm. Measure plasma concentration, filtration and urinary excretion at several controlled glucose loads, then calculate the reabsorbed load. If the maximal plateau is unchanged but the filtration line becomes steeper, increased GFR is a candidate explanation. If the plateau falls while GFR is unchanged, reduced recovery capacity is supported. A change in splay without a large change in plateau may indicate altered heterogeneity or kinetics. Measurements at just one glucose concentration cannot reliably distinguish all these alternatives.

Saturation is not a unique feature of glucose. Amino acid, phosphate and organic ion transport pathways also have finite capacity. However, hormonal regulation, competition among substrates, local metabolism and changes in water flow can complicate their titration relationships. Glucose is pedagogically valuable because the simple model of freely filtered substrate with saturable reabsorption exposes the basic accounting. Do not assume that every other renal solute produces an identical graph or that each pathway has one immutable transport maximum.

Finally, a glucose titration graph should be read with attention to axes. The horizontal axis usually represents plasma glucose concentration or filtered glucose load; the vertical axis represents transport or excretion per unit time. A concentration threshold cannot be read from a graph whose horizontal axis is a load unless GFR is known. Conversely, a Tm in mg/min cannot be read directly from a concentration axis. These distinctions frequently separate a correct Part C answer from an apparently plausible but dimensionally inconsistent option.

11. Micropuncture, clearance and experimental reasoning

Much of the modern understanding of tubular physiology developed from experiments that measured fluid within individual nephron segments. Early micropuncture work associated with investigators including A. N. Richards and collaborators used accessible animal preparations to investigate glomerular filtrate and tubular modification. Later mammalian micropuncture methods made it possible to collect small volumes of fluid from superficial nephrons and measure pressure, composition and flow. The essential methodological advance was to observe the fluid before it became final urine rather than infer every segmental process from whole-kidney output.

A micropuncture experiment places a very fine pipette into a selected tubular region. The researcher may collect fluid, inject a marker or perfuse a segment under controlled conditions. Identifying the sampled location is crucial because an early proximal sample and a late proximal sample represent different amounts of preceding transport. Deep medullary regions are often less accessible than superficial cortical segments. Thus the experimental sampling design influences which nephron populations can be observed and how broadly the results can be generalised.

Tubular fluid to plasma concentration ratios, abbreviated TF/P, are useful but require interpretation. For a freely filterable marker that is neither reabsorbed nor secreted, its mass in the lumen remains constant along the tubular path. If water is removed, the marker becomes more concentrated. Inulin is the classical example under the appropriate experimental assumptions. TF/P inulin of 2 means that the concentration is twice that in plasma; if initial filtrate concentration equalled plasma and marker amount is conserved, half of the original tubular water remains.

The fraction of the filtered water remaining at a sampling site is therefore approximately 1/(TF/P inulin). Fractional water recovery is one minus that ratio. If TF/P inulin equals 3, one-third of the original water remains and two-thirds has been recovered. The calculation depends on conservation of marker mass. If the marker is absorbed, secreted or measured inaccurately, the inferred water fraction can be biased. A student's explanation should include the physiological property that makes the marker useful, rather than memorising the reciprocal without reasoning.

For another freely filterable solute X, its fractional delivery relative to the original filtered load can be calculated as (TF/P X)/(TF/P inulin), assuming the stated marker and filtration conditions. This follows by comparing the concentration ratio for X with the concentration ratio for a conserved marker that reports water removal. A solute with TF/P X = 1.2 and TF/P inulin = 3 has fractional delivery 0.4. Despite its concentration exceeding plasma by 20%, 60% of the original filtered solute has been net recovered before the sampling site.

A second example concerns glucose. At a proximal location, TF/P glucose = 0.10 and TF/P inulin = 2.0. Fractional glucose delivery is 0.05, so net recovery is 95% of the filtered glucose at that point. The raw glucose concentration ratio might tempt a reader to report 90% recovery; accounting for water removal gives the correct value. This form of calculation is frequently useful in CSIR-NET analytical questions because it links concentration data to actual epithelial transport.

Now consider a freely filterable substance with TF/P X = 5 and TF/P inulin = 2.5. Fractional delivery is 2.0, indicating that the lumen carries twice the original filtered amount. Under the simple model without luminal production, net secretion must have occurred upstream. If a question instead concerns a substance generated by renal metabolism, the interpretation must allow for additional sources. The mathematical result establishes net addition to the tubular stream but not necessarily the molecular mechanism of that addition.

Serial sampling can reveal the contribution of an intervening segment. Suppose TF/P inulin is 1.5 at point A and 3 at point B. If TF/P sodium is 1.05 at A and 0.9 at B, fractional sodium delivery is 0.70 at A and 0.30 at B. The intervening segment therefore recovers 0.40 of the original filtered sodium, or about 57.1% of the amount delivered at A. Water delivery changes from two-thirds to one-third of the filtered volume. The segment's fractional recovery of its input is not identical to its recovery expressed against the original load.

Single-nephron filtration rate can be estimated from tubular collection if the flow and conserved marker concentration are measured. If tubular flow at a site is 12 nL/min and TF/P inulin is 2.5, single-nephron GFR is approximately 30 nL/min. The formula follows from marker conservation. Whole-kidney GFR is the sum of filtration across functioning nephrons and cannot be obtained from one superficial nephron measurement without assumptions about nephron number and heterogeneity. A single nephron provides local mechanistic insight rather than an automatic whole-organ census.

Isolated perfused tubules offer a complementary strategy. A segment can be removed and exposed to controlled luminal and bathing solutions. Researchers can vary individual ion concentrations, osmotic gradients, hormones or inhibitors while measuring fluid and solute flux. This method allows causal questions to be addressed more directly than observational whole-kidney clearance, but it also removes the normal circulation, systemic feedback and neighbouring segment interactions. A finding in an isolated segment establishes a local capability under the experimental conditions, not necessarily its exact contribution in vivo.

Imagine an isolated thick ascending limb perfused with 300 mOsm/L solution. If salt transport decreases the output osmolality while volume flow remains approximately unchanged, the observation is consistent with salt recovery and low water permeability. Adding an NKCC2 inhibitor to the lumen and observing a reduction in dilution supports the importance of NKCC2-dependent transport. Measuring transepithelial voltage and calcium flux before and after intervention can help connect primary salt transport with secondary paracellular divalent cation recovery. A vehicle control and a check of tissue viability are essential to distinguish specific inhibition from nonspecific deterioration.

An epithelial cell model may be used to test membrane localisation and substrate coupling. For example, expression of a sodium–glucose cotransporter can increase glucose uptake in the presence of an imposed sodium gradient. Replacing extracellular sodium with a suitable substitute can reduce coupled uptake. A control cell lacking the transporter allows background uptake to be estimated. Surface labelling can distinguish lower activity caused by decreased transporter abundance from a change in transport kinetics. No single result necessarily establishes all steps of transepithelial recovery, especially when the model lacks normal cell polarity.

Genetic approaches can provide strong complementary evidence. If deletion of an early proximal glucose transporter increases delivery of glucose to later segments, the result supports its contribution to early recovery. Persistence of some recovery indicates alternative pathways or compensation. A double perturbation targeting both early and late glucose transport can reveal their combined contribution. Developmental adaptations and species differences must be considered; a chronic knockout phenotype is not always identical to an acute selective intervention in an adult animal.

Clearance measurements remain important because they provide whole-organ integration. A marker may have clearance below, equal to or above GFR depending on net tubular handling and filtration properties. Combining clearance with segmental sampling can distinguish a local change from compensatory downstream transport. For example, increased glucose delivery after an upstream intervention together with little urinary glucose suggests substantial downstream recovery. If both local delivery and urine excretion rise, downstream capacity may be exceeded or impaired.

An experiment can also manipulate renal vascular pressure or filtration while measuring proximal delivery. A proportional increase in absolute proximal recovery as filtration rises supports glomerulotubular balance under the tested conditions. To establish whether capillary uptake, luminal substrate delivery or hormonal changes explain the response requires additional controls. A correlation between GFR and recovery is not by itself a complete molecular mechanism. The distinction between observation, inference and causal proof is a recurring theme of experimental physiology.

When interpreting an unfamiliar experimental graph, first identify the dependent variable: concentration, flux, clearance, fractional delivery or voltage. Identify which compartment is measured, the direction of transfer and which factors are held constant. Then ask which mechanism is directly affected by the intervention and what secondary changes may follow. This structured approach is more reliable than matching one keyword to a memorised transporter. It also prepares students for questions in which several mechanisms are compatible with the data and the most justified conclusion is narrower than the strongest possible interpretation.

12. Final urine composition and integrated segmental reasoning

Final urine contains water and a variable mixture of inorganic ions and organic solutes. Urea, creatinine and urate are important nitrogen-containing components in humans; sodium, potassium, chloride, ammonium, phosphate and other ions contribute to the dissolved solute load. The precise concentrations depend on intake, metabolism, hydration, filtration and tubular regulation. Urine composition is not represented by one universal set of values. It is the output of a dynamic system that must eliminate wastes while regulating body fluid composition.

Under ordinary conditions, glucose and freely filtered amino acids are largely recovered. Large proteins are strongly restricted by the glomerular barrier, while small filtered proteins can be recovered by proximal endocytosis. A small amount of protein may nevertheless be present in normal urine, including proteins derived from the urinary system itself. The term normal urine should not be interpreted as chemically pure water plus a fixed amount of urea. Different substances can be present at low concentrations depending on the measurement method.

Urea originates mainly from nitrogen metabolism and is freely filtered, with complex segmental reabsorption and recycling. Creatinine originates from creatine-related metabolism and is filtered with a modest secretory contribution. Urate handling involves both reabsorption and secretion. These differences illustrate why waste products do not all follow the same nephron pathway. A biological explanation of final urine must identify the particular substance rather than assume that waste is never reabsorbed.

A hypothetical 24-hour urine sample of 1.5 L contains sodium at 80 mmol/L and potassium at 45 mmol/L. Total sodium excretion is 120 mmol/day and potassium excretion is 67.5 mmol/day. If another sample has sodium at 160 mmol/L but a volume of 0.6 L, sodium excretion is 96 mmol/day, less than in the first sample despite twice the concentration. A comparison of concentration alone would reverse the correct conclusion about sodium loss. This distinction applies equally to urea, glucose and other soluble components.

Urine osmolality reports the number of dissolved osmotically active particles relative to water, while urinary excretion rates report the output of particular solutes. These measurements answer different questions. A concentrated urine can indicate effective water conservation, but it does not establish that a specific solute is being excreted at a higher rate. Conversely, a large volume of urine can occur because water recovery is reduced or because unrecovered luminal solute retains water and promotes osmotic diuresis. The next node develops the quantitative distinction using osmolar clearance and free-water clearance.

An integrated segmental example begins with 100 units of filtered sodium and 100 units of filtered water. The proximal tubule recovers 65 units of each. The descending limb recovers 10 water units in the hypothetical model without corresponding sodium recovery. The ascending limb removes 25 sodium units with negligible water. Distal salt recovery removes another 8 sodium units; the collecting system recovers 1 unit of sodium and 24 units of water. Final output is 1 sodium unit and 1 water unit. These illustrative numbers are internally balanced but do not prescribe fixed physiological percentages for all conditions.

The example exposes why salt and water handling can diverge after the proximal tubule. If collecting duct water recovery falls from 24 to 10 units while other model values remain fixed, final water output rises from 1 to 15 units without a change in final sodium output. Sodium concentration falls to one-fifteenth of its previous value. The assumption of unchanged salt handling isolates water permeability conceptually. In vivo, changes in flow and hormone levels can also modify ion transport, so a purely isolated change is an experimental simplification.

Consider a perturbation of proximal sodium and glucose transport. More glucose remains in the lumen and can contribute to osmotic retention of water; more sodium may reach the loop and distal nephron. The macula densa can respond to altered salt delivery, potentially modifying filtration. The final urine may contain glucose if later proximal recovery is insufficient. This sequence connects a molecular alteration to local solute delivery, water recovery, filtration feedback and whole-kidney excretion. Every arrow in that causal chain has assumptions, and the magnitude of the response depends on compensation.

A selective disturbance of the thick ascending limb has a different primary effect: it reduces salt recovery from a relatively water-impermeable segment. Distal sodium delivery rises, and generation of the medullary osmotic gradient can weaken. Calcium and magnesium recovery may also change because of altered transepithelial voltage. Additional water loss can result from both the retained solute and diminished concentrating capacity. The comparison with proximal glucose transport illustrates why two perturbations that both increase urine volume need not act through the same primary mechanism.

A disturbance in the collecting duct water pathway can increase urine volume even when earlier salt transport remains functional. In that situation the nephron can still deliver dilute fluid to the late segments, but reduced final water permeability limits recovery in the presence of an osmotic gradient. By contrast, an ascending limb defect can reduce the gradient itself. This separation of gradient generation and water permeability is fundamental for the next node. The current chapter supplies the local transport basis for each process.

A change in final potassium excretion is also ambiguous without supporting measurements. It may reflect altered dietary input, redistribution between cells and extracellular fluid, changes in filtration, modified proximal or loop recovery, or changes in distal secretion. Increased sodium delivery can favour potassium secretion when electrogenic sodium uptake and potassium conductance are available. An endocrine signal may alter those transporters. Consequently, urinary potassium concentration cannot be used alone to identify a specific channel or regulatory hormone.

Renal acid handling introduces a further layer of complexity. The proximal tubule recovers filtered bicarbonate and participates in ammonium production; distal intercalated cells adjust hydrogen ion and bicarbonate transport. Final urinary pH is not equivalent to total net acid excretion because substantial acid can be carried in buffered form. A highly acidic urine with a small buffer content can carry less total acid than a less acidic urine containing more ammonium and titratable buffers. The acid–base node develops this distinction quantitatively.

An important feature of homeostasis is that the kidney's output changes in response to the body's input and internal demands. A temporarily increased excretion rate after a salt load may restore balance rather than indicate loss of transport function. A decrease in water output during dehydration can protect extracellular fluid volume. When interpreting experiments, distinguish the adaptive response of a healthy feedback system from a primary transport defect. The same final urine pattern may result from different combinations of regulated responses.

The nephron's serial organisation also makes localisation an inverse problem. Several changes upstream can produce similar final urinary excretion; a downstream change can mask an upstream defect. Measuring GFR, plasma concentrations, segmental delivery, transporter localisation and hormonal conditions provides a stronger basis for inference. This is why renal physiology is an excellent topic for Part C examination questions: a correct answer often requires integrating molecular cell biology with mass balance and experimental design rather than recalling a single anatomical fact.

The core framework can now be reconstructed from first principles. Glomerular filtration creates a large initial load. Polarised epithelial transport uses ATP-dependent gradients, carriers, channels and paracellular pathways to recover needed material and add selected substances. Proximal segments perform bulk recovery; the loop separates salt and water handling; the distal convolution and collecting system refine final output. Capacity limitations explain solute escape, and experimental measurements reveal where and how it occurs. The next node builds on the salt–water separation to explain medullary countercurrent mechanisms and vasopressin-dependent control of water balance.

13. Worked analytical cases and quantitative synthesis

Case one considers a freely filtered substance with a plasma concentration of 2 mmol/L, GFR of 120 mL/min and urine flow of 2 mL/min. The urine concentration is 30 mmol/L. Filtered load is 2 × 0.120 = 0.240 mmol/min; excretion is 30 × 0.002 = 0.060 mmol/min. Net recovery is 0.180 mmol/min and fractional excretion is 25%. Clearance is 30 × 2/2 = 30 mL/min, one-quarter of GFR. The agreement between fractional excretion and the clearance ratio is expected for a freely filtered substance under the assumptions.

If this same solute has a free fraction of only one-half and the given plasma concentration is the total, the filtered load becomes 0.120 mmol/min rather than 0.240. Excretion remains 0.060 mmol/min if the urinary measurements are unchanged. Net recovery is now 0.060 mmol/min and fractional excretion relative to the filtered load is 50%. The measured clearance using total plasma concentration is still 30 mL/min. Comparing total-plasma clearance with GFR without considering binding would therefore underestimate the fractional excretion relative to actual filtration. Always specify the plasma concentration convention.

Case two compares two solutes at a GFR of 100 mL/min. Solute A has a clearance of 50 mL/min, while solute B has a clearance of 150 mL/min. Both are freely filterable and are not metabolised in the tubule. Solute A undergoes net reabsorption of half its filtered load. Solute B undergoes net addition equal to half its filtered load. This does not tell us which molecular transporters operate or whether A also has a secretory component and B also has a reabsorptive component. The clearance comparison identifies the net direction, not the complete transport pathway.

Case three concerns glucose at plasma concentration 320 mg/dL and GFR 100 mL/min. An idealised model has a maximal glucose recovery rate of 250 mg/min, with complete saturation at this load and no secretion. Filtered load equals 320 mg/min, and urinary glucose excretion is 70 mg/min. At urine flow 2 mL/min, urinary glucose concentration is 35 mg/mL, or 3500 mg/dL. These numbers are deliberately constructed for examination practice. They are not clinical thresholds or predictions of urine composition for a particular person.

If the same model is tested after an increase in GFR to 120 mL/min at unchanged plasma glucose, filtration rises to 384 mg/min. With Tm fixed at 250 mg/min, excretion rises to 134 mg/min. The difference of 64 mg/min exactly equals the increase in filtered load because the reabsorptive machinery was already saturated. At lower plasma concentrations before saturation, an increase in filtration may instead be fully compensated by additional recovery. The response to the same GFR change therefore depends on where the system lies on its titration curve.

Case four concerns water removal and the concentration of a conserved marker. The tubular concentration of a freely filtered inert marker rises from 1 arbitrary unit in initial filtrate to 2.5 units at the end of a segment. The fraction of initial water remaining is 0.4. A second freely filtered solute has a concentration of 1.5 times its plasma value at the same point. Its fractional delivery is 1.5/2.5 = 0.6. Thus 40% of the solute has been net recovered while 60% of water has been recovered. Its concentration increased because water removal exceeded solute removal proportionally.

Case five tests transporter location. An isolated epithelial segment shows sodium chloride uptake that requires luminal potassium, and inhibition of apical potassium recycling decreases uptake and lumen-positive voltage. The combination supports a thick ascending limb mechanism involving NKCC2 and ROMK. A distal convoluted tubule using NCC would not directly require potassium as a cotransported substrate. However, potassium availability can influence many cellular processes indirectly, so the most convincing identification would also include transporter localisation, pharmacological sensitivity and control of epithelial viability.

Case six tests compensatory transport. A nephron filters 100 units of sodium and normally reabsorbs 65 proximally, 25 in the loop and 9 downstream, excreting 1. An intervention reduces proximal recovery to 60. The loop increases recovery to 28 and distal recovery rises to 10. Final excretion is 2. Although the primary defect reduces proximal recovery by 5 units, final excretion increases by only one because other segments recover four additional units. The correct interpretation is partial compensation, not absence of a proximal effect.

If the downstream segments could not increase recovery, final excretion would instead be 6. Comparing these cases demonstrates how the reserve capacities of downstream segments determine the magnitude of whole-kidney responses. One may identify the primary defect by measuring delivery immediately after the affected segment, even when final urine changes little. This is the experimental reason to combine segmental data with final clearance.

Case seven examines electrochemical coupling. Suppose an apical channel permits sodium to enter a principal cell, while intracellular sodium remains low because of basolateral pump activity. If the luminal side becomes more negative relative to the interstitium as sodium is absorbed, potassium secretion through apical channels may become more favourable. Inhibition of ENaC decreases the electrical effect of sodium entry and may reduce potassium secretion. The analysis does not require ENaC to exchange sodium for potassium; it recognises that independent pathways interact through a shared electrical field.

Case eight compares two animals with equal daily sodium excretion of 100 mmol but urine volumes of 1 L and 4 L. Sodium concentration is 100 mmol/L in the first and 25 mmol/L in the second. The fourfold concentration difference is entirely explained by water output in the simplified example. An investigator who measures only urinary sodium concentration could wrongly conclude that the first animal loses four times as much sodium. To understand the underlying transport, the investigator also needs filtration and segmental information.

Case nine considers a glucose load study with paired measurements. At plasma glucose 100, 200, 300 and 400 mg/dL and GFR 100 mL/min, filtered loads are 100, 200, 300 and 400 mg/min. Measured glucose excretion rates are 0, 5, 40 and 130 mg/min. The inferred reabsorbed loads are 100, 195, 260 and 270 mg/min. The data show a gradual divergence from complete recovery and an approach toward a plateau, consistent with splay and saturation. The largest observed recovery is 270 mg/min; whether this equals the true asymptotic maximum cannot be established without additional measurements.

Case ten concerns interpretation of a bicarbonate pathway. A proximal epithelial preparation is exposed to an inhibitor of carbonic anhydrase. Luminal bicarbonate delivery increases while glucose transport remains largely unchanged. The selective result is consistent with impaired bicarbonate reclamation rather than a complete collapse of sodium gradients. If the same preparation had lost ATP production, both sodium-coupled glucose uptake and bicarbonate recovery might decline. A control measuring cell integrity and sodium–potassium ATPase function would help distinguish a pathway-specific perturbation from general cellular failure.

The common skill in these cases is identifying what is measured and what remains unknown. A mass balance can establish the magnitude of net transfer, a tracer can locate the region where delivery changes, an inhibitor can support a causal mechanism, and a localisation experiment can identify the appropriate membrane. Strong physiological explanations combine these forms of evidence without overstating any one of them. This habit of reasoning is the bridge between descriptive renal anatomy and analytical examination readiness.

39. A nephron filters 100 Na units, recovers 60 proximally, 28 in the loop and 10 distally. Final excretion is:

  1. 12 units
  2. 28 units
  3. 40 units
  4. 2 units
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. 2 units

Subtract all recovery expressed relative to the same original load.

40. An upstream intervention reduces proximal glucose recovery but urinary glucose does not increase. Best interpretation is:

  1. The upstream measurement must be false
  2. GFR is necessarily zero
  3. Downstream recovery may compensate
  4. Glucose is not filtered
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Downstream recovery may compensate

Later proximal glucose recovery can retrieve additional delivered glucose until its reserve is exhausted.

41. Urine A contains 100 mmol/L Na at 1 L/day; B contains 60 mmol/L at 2 L/day. Which excretes more?

  1. A, by 40 mmol/day
  2. Both excrete equally
  3. B, by 20 mmol/day
  4. A, by 20 mmol/day
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. B, by 20 mmol/day

A excretes 100; B excretes 120 mmol/day.

42. A solute concentration increases from 1 to 1.5 units while volume falls from 100 to 40 units. The fraction of initial solute remaining is:

  1. 1.50
  2. 0.60
  3. 0.40
  4. 0.25
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 0.60

Initial amount 100; final amount 1.5 × 40 = 60.

43. A freely filterable solute is 50% unbound at total P=4 mmol/L and GFR=100 mL/min. Filtered load is:

  1. 0.20 mmol/min
  2. 0.40 mmol/min
  3. 2 mmol/min
  4. 20 mmol/min
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 0.20 mmol/min

Use free P=2 mmol/L times 0.10 L/min.

44. Which result distinguishes a local NCC defect from a primary NKCC2 defect most directly?

  1. Higher final urine volume
  2. Lower plasma K+ alone
  3. Higher urinary Na+ alone
  4. Impaired thiazide-sensitive NaCl uptake in an isolated DCT
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Impaired thiazide-sensitive NaCl uptake in an isolated DCT

Segment-specific perfusion and transporter sensitivity provide more direct localisation than whole-kidney outcomes.

45. A glucose titration experiment at GFR=100 mL/min and P=400 mg/dL yields E=130 mg/min. Reabsorbed load is:

  1. 130 mg/min
  2. 530 mg/min
  3. 400 mg/min
  4. 270 mg/min
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. 270 mg/min

F=400 mg/min; R=F−E=270 mg/min in the absence of secretion.

46. Which outcome supports a selective carbonic anhydrase effect rather than generalized ATP depletion?

  1. Loss of all Na-coupled transport
  2. Loss of cell viability
  3. Reduced bicarbonate recovery with preserved glucose uptake
  4. Absence of any filtered solute
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Reduced bicarbonate recovery with preserved glucose uptake

The relatively selective transport change supports impairment of bicarbonate reclamation.

47. A solute has TF/P X=6 and TF/P inulin=3. Assuming no metabolism or binding, the best inference is:

  1. 66% reabsorption
  2. Net addition to tubular fluid equal to one filtered load
  3. No net transport
  4. 50% reabsorption
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Net addition to tubular fluid equal to one filtered load

Fractional delivery is 2; luminal amount is twice the filtered input.

48. A reduction in apical ROMK activity in thick ascending limb primarily threatens:

  1. Potassium recycling that supports NKCC2
  2. SGLT2-dependent glucose uptake
  3. AQP2 trafficking
  4. Glomerular slit diaphragm structure
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Potassium recycling that supports NKCC2

Luminal potassium recycling supports cotransport and the lumen-positive potential.

  4. Assertion false, reason true

Splay allows glucose escape before whole-kidney aggregate capacity is reached.

  2. Both false
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true

Reclaiming previously filtered bicarbonate differs from generating new bicarbonate during net acid excretion.

51. Match SGLT2, NKCC2, NCC and ENaC to their main locations in order.

Answer: C. Early proximal, thick ascending, distal convoluted, principal cell

These distinct apical transporters form a high-yield map of nephron epithelial organisation.

52. Which statement correctly connects renal filtration and tubular recovery?

  2. High filtration requires efficient selective recovery of useful solutes
  3. Reabsorption occurs only in the glomerulus
  4. Secretion and excretion are synonyms

Answer: B. High filtration requires efficient selective recovery of useful solutes

Urine output integrates filtration with selective reabsorption and secretion.

53. Which pair is correctly matched?

  4. NCC — thick ascending limb NKCC2

54. A specimen has high urine glucose but plasma glucose is normal. Which general mechanism is compatible?
