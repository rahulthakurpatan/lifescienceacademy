1. Why a tumor is a tissue, not merely a collection of cancer cells

When a normal epithelium is examined in a tissue section, the epithelial cells are only one component of a larger arrangement. Beneath the epithelial sheet are structural proteins, connective tissue cells, blood vessels and immune cells. These components contribute to the delivery of substances, removal of damaged cells and maintenance of spatial organization. The cells of the epithelium do not need to perform every one of these functions independently. A tumor develops within an arrangement of this kind rather than in an empty space. Consequently, a change in one cellular lineage can alter the demands placed on its neighbors, and the response of those neighbors can influence the subsequent behavior of the altered lineage. This is the central problem of tumor microenvironment biology.

The tumor microenvironment comprises the local cellular and noncellular surroundings of a neoplastic population, including interactions among the cells, extracellular structures, soluble signals and physical conditions. Cancer cells are participants in that environment, not merely passive recipients of outside influences. A solid tumor can contain malignant epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, perivascular cells, immune cells and extracellular matrix. Their relative abundance and arrangement differ among tissues and among regions of the same tumor. A tumor microenvironment is therefore not defined by a fixed percentage of each cell type. It is defined by a set of interacting components that establish the local conditions in which cancer cells live.

The term stroma commonly refers to the supporting non-neoplastic cellular and extracellular components of a tissue. Parenchyma denotes the principal functional cells of the organ; in the context of a carcinoma, the malignant epithelial component can be distinguished from the associated stroma. This distinction is useful, but the exact terminology depends on the tissue and type of tumor. For example, in a malignancy of mesenchymal origin, one cannot identify every elongated fibroblast-like cell as normal stroma merely from its shape. The cellular lineage and functional state must be established. Similarly, the term nonmalignant does not mean unaffected: a stromal cell can retain its own genome without being a cancer cell while displaying profoundly altered behavior in the tumor.

The difference between a cell-autonomous and a non-cell-autonomous effect is essential for connecting the present chapter with the preceding chapters on oncogenes and tumor suppressors. If a mutation changes the activity of a protein within the cell that carries it, the immediate consequence can be described as cell-autonomous. If the altered cell releases a signal that changes the behavior of a neighboring cell, that subsequent consequence is non-cell-autonomous with respect to the original mutation. The distinction concerns the relationship between the site of an alteration and the site at which its effect is measured. It is not a claim that a cell-autonomous alteration has no environmental consequences. In tumors, the two levels are often linked by reciprocal interactions.

Consider a conceptual example in which two cancer-cell populations possess comparable alterations in a growth-promoting gene. One population is supplied with oxygen and nutrients by well-functioning vessels, whereas the other occupies a poorly perfused region. Even if their growth-promoting proteins have the same activity, the rates at which their cells successfully produce viable descendants may differ. The poorly supplied region can impose restrictions that are not corrected simply by having an active oncogene. Conversely, a supportive environment cannot by itself establish that every cell within it is malignant. The genetic and environmental conditions contribute different information about the eventual population behavior. Their relationship explains why the same mutation need not produce identical tissue-level outcomes in different settings.

It is also necessary to distinguish local conditions from systemic conditions. Circulating substances, nutritional status and organism-wide immune responses can influence tissues, but the concentration and activity of a substance in one microscopic region depend on local delivery, consumption and production. Two parts of the same organ can therefore experience distinct conditions while sharing the same circulation. Within a tumor, cells near a perfused vessel may encounter a different availability of oxygen from cells far from that vessel. A measurement made in blood does not automatically reveal the concentration at every location. The present chapter focuses on the local interactions; broader systemic physiology belongs to the appropriate later units.

A population of cancer cells can alter its surroundings through secretion, consumption, mechanical interactions and changes in the organization of tissue. Neighboring nonmalignant cells can respond to those changes. Their responses can in turn modify the substances and structures available to the cancer population. The term reciprocal interaction expresses this two-way relationship. It is stronger than the statement that several cell types happen to be present together. To establish reciprocity, the response of one component must be connected to an influence from another and the subsequent change must affect the first component or its descendants. The feedback may be reinforcing, restraining or mixed, depending on the functions of the participating cells.

The analogy of a tumor to an abnormal organ is useful because it directs attention toward organized relationships among distinct components. It should not be taken to mean that a tumor has the same physiological purpose or stable organization as a normal organ. Tumors frequently contain irregular structures, variable patterns of cell death and abnormal vascular organization. Their local environments can change as the malignant population grows. The analogy instead emphasizes that a tissue-level phenotype cannot be described completely by listing the mutations present in its malignant cells. It is necessary to understand how those mutations are expressed within a physical and cellular context.

2. Evidence for the importance of the surrounding tissue

Early studies of cancer genetics established the importance of changes within malignant cells, but several kinds of observations indicated that these changes could not alone explain all tissue behavior. Normal and abnormal cells can share parts of the same signaling machinery while responding differently to their surroundings. Tumors can have striking differences in the amount of surrounding connective tissue even when their malignant cells have a related origin. Some tumors contain substantial inflammatory infiltrates, whereas others contain fewer such cells in the regions sampled. These observations suggested that a description of the malignant lineage must be supplemented by an account of the tissue in which it develops. The question was not whether mutations matter, but what determines their phenotypic consequences.

In his 1971 discussion of tumor angiogenesis, Judah Folkman developed the idea that expansion of many solid tumors depends on obtaining a vascular supply. The idea linked a tissue-scale requirement to a cellular response in blood-vessel-forming cells. It also suggested a different perspective on a possible intervention: an essential support process might be relevant even when the malignant-cell genotype remains unchanged. The historical paper should not be interpreted as proof that every tumor recruits new vessels in exactly the same manner. Existing vessels can also supply some tumors, and later work revealed substantial diversity in vascular behavior. Its importance was establishing vascular support as a major biological question in cancer.

The 1992 study by Shweiki and colleagues linked low oxygen availability to increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor, VEGF. This supplied evidence connecting an environmental condition with a signal capable of affecting blood-vessel-associated cells. A subsequent 1995 study examined multicellular spheroids and found that expression was increased in oxygen-poor regions and could change when the oxygen limitation was relieved. The comparison made a spatially variable condition biologically relevant rather than assuming uniform expression throughout a population. It did not establish that oxygen is the only regulator of VEGF or that increased expression inevitably produces effective perfusion. The finding is best understood as evidence for one environmentally responsive link within a larger regulatory system.

A different line of work emphasized the relationship between epithelial cells and surrounding matrix and stroma. Bissell and Radisky's 2001 review synthesized observations showing that tissue organization and communication influence malignant phenotypes. A useful conceptual contrast is between a cell studied under a simplified isolated condition and a related cell embedded within a tissue-like arrangement. An alteration observed under one condition may be expressed differently under the other. This comparison does not mean that every genetic alteration is reversed by normal surroundings. Rather, it identifies the context in which a phenotype is observed as an important experimental variable. The distinction is particularly relevant for questions about cell polarity, adhesion and differentiation.

Immune research established another reason to consider the behavior of normal cells in cancer. The immune system can recognize and eliminate some altered cells, but the populations that remain after immune-mediated restriction need not be a random sample of the original population. Schreiber, Old and Smyth described an integrative framework of cancer immunoediting in 2011 that encompasses tumor restraint, selection and escape. This framework explains why immune activity and continued tumor development are not necessarily contradictory observations. A successful immune response may remove susceptible cells while other descendants remain. It also recognizes the contribution of tissue conditions that modify immune function. It is a conceptual framework, not a requirement that every lesion pass through three easily observable stages.

The diversity of fibroblast functions provides a particularly important caution against a simple classification of all stromal cells as tumor-promoting. In a 2014 study of a pancreatic cancer mouse model, Özdemir and colleagues examined the consequences of depletion of a particular myofibroblast population. The affected animals showed less favorable disease behavior and survival, accompanied by changes in the tumor environment. This observation was inconsistent with the prediction that removing every supportive-looking stromal component must improve the disease. The evidence concerns a defined population and biological model; it does not establish that all fibroblasts are protective in all cancers. It demonstrates why functional conclusions must be tied to the identity and context of the cells examined.

The development of the vascular normalization concept provides a second important exception. Jain and colleagues emphasized that the quality of vascular function matters in addition to the number of vessels. A tumor can contain irregular, poorly functioning vessels, and changes in vascular organization may alter the delivery of oxygen or other substances even when vessel abundance decreases. This idea challenged the assumption that a simple reduction in vessel density necessarily corresponds to a proportional reduction in delivery. The important historical progression is from recognizing the need for a vascular supply to understanding the actual performance of that supply. These findings together illustrate how improved biological models arise by testing predictions rather than merely increasing the number of terms used to describe a tumor.

3. Local communication and reciprocal signaling

Cell communication was introduced in Unit 4.A and Unit 4.B as a way for cells to coordinate activities. In a tumor, these same modes of communication operate between cells whose behavior has changed and cells that retain other tissue functions. Paracrine signaling refers to an influence of a locally released signal on nearby responsive cells. Autocrine signaling refers to a signal influencing the cell that produced it or related cells of the same signaling population. Direct contact can convey information through molecules at adjacent cell surfaces. A matrix-associated cue can be recognized through receptors at a cell–matrix interface. These mechanisms differ in their physical routes, and several can contribute simultaneously in a particular region.

The presence of a soluble signal does not establish that every cell in its vicinity responds. A receiving cell must possess the relevant machinery, including an appropriate receptor and downstream regulatory components. Two cell populations exposed to the same extracellular concentration may therefore produce different responses. For example, a vascular-associated cell and an epithelial cell may express different receptors or differ in the ability to transmit the signal. The biological effect also depends on the state of the receiving cell. A cell already engaged in a particular response may react differently from one in a quiescent state. This is why identifying the source of a factor is only one part of explaining its biological action.

A local signal can be represented conceptually as a sequence from source to extracellular availability to reception to response. The source produces or releases the signal, but extracellular availability depends on diffusion, retention, uptake and degradation. The receptor converts an external condition into a molecular influence within the receiving cell. Downstream regulatory components determine the consequence. A defect or change at any of these stages can alter the response even if other stages remain similar. For example, an increase in the expression of a receptor does not prove that more ligand is available. Likewise, increased extracellular ligand concentration does not establish activation of every downstream pathway in the receiving cells.

The direction of communication is important in distinguishing a one-way influence from a feedback relationship. Suppose an altered epithelial population produces a signal that changes the behavior of a nearby fibroblast population. If the fibroblasts subsequently release signals or modify matrix in a way that affects the epithelial cells, a reciprocal relationship becomes possible. The effect on the epithelial population might support survival, restrict expansion or modify differentiation. The mere fact that the fibroblasts respond to the first signal does not identify the direction or magnitude of their subsequent influence. The outcome has to be inferred from evidence of both links rather than from a label such as activated stroma.

The same general reasoning applies to a vascular-associated population. A cell experiencing a local shortage of oxygen can increase expression of an angiogenic signal. Responsive endothelial cells may undergo changes related to vascular growth. If the resulting vessels establish effective perfusion, the original shortage may be reduced. However, a newly formed structure may be poorly organized or inadequately perfused, and the shortage can persist. The feedback is therefore conditional on the actual functional outcome, not simply on the presence of an angiogenic signal or an increased count of vessel structures. This example helps distinguish regulation of the signal from restoration of the environmental variable that originally influenced it.

An extracellular signal can be retained or presented by matrix molecules, so the local physical surroundings can influence its effective distribution. A factor produced by a cell need not remain freely soluble throughout the surrounding fluid. Association with extracellular structures can change its mobility, and remodeling of those structures can change its availability. This means that a measurement of total factor in a tissue does not necessarily reveal the freely accessible fraction at a particular receptor. The distinction is analogous to the difference between total and free concentration in ordinary binding problems. It connects matrix biology with the communication principles developed earlier in the unit.

Communication can also be limited by spatial organization. A ligand released into one tissue compartment may not reach every neighboring compartment at the same concentration. Cell–cell contact depends on adjacency; matrix-associated signals depend on the interface accessible to a cell. The physical arrangement of malignant and nonmalignant components therefore matters alongside their total abundance. A tissue containing a large population of potentially responsive cells may exhibit little interaction if those cells are separated from the relevant source or cannot reach the affected region. Students should learn to interpret tissue diagrams as spatial relationships rather than simply as lists of components.

4. Fibroblasts and the changing stromal compartment

Fibroblasts are connective tissue cells that contribute to maintenance and remodeling of the extracellular surroundings. In normal tissue, they participate in structural support and responses to injury. Their function cannot be described solely by the production of one protein. A fibroblast can contribute matrix constituents, influence extracellular organization and communicate with other cell types through secreted mediators. During repair, its behavior may change in response to damage-associated signals. Such changes are normally regulated in relation to the tissue's need for restoration. A tumor may provide persistent or abnormal signals that alter those responses, creating a local stromal state different from the one found in the corresponding healthy tissue.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts, commonly abbreviated CAFs, are fibroblast-like stromal populations associated with tumors. The term identifies a broad biological category rather than a single uniform differentiation state. Cells within this category can differ in their origin, expression of structural proteins, secretory activities and relationships with neighboring populations. Some display properties associated with contractile myofibroblasts, whereas others show different patterns of signaling and matrix production. Therefore the detection of one frequently used marker does not define every cell that belongs to the functional category. A marker can help identify a population in a specified study, but the function of that population must be established separately.

Tissue-associated fibroblasts need not become malignant for their behavior to change. A normal fibroblast possesses signaling pathways that respond to the local environment. Signals associated with altered epithelial cells, inflammation or tissue injury can influence its state. A changed state may persist for a period even though the fibroblast does not share the characteristic genetic alterations of the malignant lineage. This is a clear example of a non-cell-autonomous influence. It is important not to assume that every difference between a tumor-associated fibroblast and a healthy-tissue fibroblast must be explained by mutation. The source, duration and stability of the observed difference are empirical questions.

One prominent example of a regulatory influence is transforming growth factor beta, TGF-beta. This signaling family has important roles in tissue regulation and can influence fibroblast behavior and matrix-associated processes. Its consequences depend on the receiving cell and its regulatory background. In one context, a signal can participate in a growth-restraining response in epithelial cells; in another context, its effects on stromal cells can contribute to changes in matrix and tissue organization. These are not necessarily contradictory effects, because the receiving populations have different functions. A diagram should identify the cell type responding to the signal rather than classifying the signal as uniformly supportive or inhibitory.

Myofibroblast-like cells can exert forces on surrounding extracellular structures, which is useful in the organization of healing tissues. Persistent alterations in the abundance or activity of such cells can influence the mechanical conditions experienced by other populations. The resulting consequences may depend on the geometry, composition and stiffness of the matrix. A tissue can become more mechanically restrictive in one region and provide distinct surfaces for adhesion in another. These are different physical properties. It is therefore not sufficient to describe a fibroblast-associated change merely as more matrix. One must ask what material is present, how it is organized and which cells interact with it.

Fibroblasts can also influence the availability of soluble mediators. A change in a stromal population can alter the concentration of factors capable of influencing epithelial cells, vascular cells or immune cells. In this way, a local response originating in one compartment can affect several other compartments. The consequences depend on receptor expression and downstream regulatory state in the recipients. It is possible for one mediator to have different effects in two nearby populations. A schematic arrow from a fibroblast to a cancer cell represents a particular proposed communication relationship, not a universal indication that the fibroblast always supports the cancer cell.

A useful comparison is between an ordinary wound response and a tumor-associated stromal response. Both may involve changes in matrix, immune-cell recruitment and vascular activity. In a wound, these changes contribute to restoration and are ordinarily limited as the tissue returns toward a stable condition. In a tumor, the disturbances that provoke the response can persist or change as the abnormal population expands. The resemblance was developed in Dvorak's 1986 analysis of wound healing and tumor stroma. It should not be taken to imply that all healing tissues are cancers or that the two processes are identical. The comparison identifies shared physiological functions that can be expressed in different contexts.

The study of fibroblast heterogeneity is important because functional categories cannot be inferred from the direction of a single measurement. Suppose a tissue contains more cells displaying a particular contractile marker than another tissue. The observation identifies a difference in the measured population, but does not by itself establish whether those cells restrain or promote the expansion of malignant cells. Different subpopulations can have distinct or even opposing roles. A change in the measured abundance of one population may also alter the proportions of other populations. The phenotype of the whole tissue depends on the interactions among these components, which is why a universal assumption about every fibroblast is unreliable.

In an examination question, fibroblasts should be distinguished from endothelial cells and from malignant epithelial cells by their relevant contributions. Fibroblasts are commonly important sources and organizers of connective-tissue matrix; endothelial cells form the cellular lining of blood vessels; epithelial cancer cells comprise the altered lineage in a carcinoma. None of these simplified assignments excludes communication among the groups or production of some overlapping mediators. The key is to recognize which function is being described. When a question asks about the effect of stromal remodeling on a cancer cell, the relevant causal link is the change in local conditions rather than an assumed mutation in the fibroblast.

5. Extracellular matrix as a structural and signaling interface

The extracellular matrix, abbreviated ECM, is the organized collection of extracellular macromolecules and associated structures with which cells interact. In an epithelial tissue, a basement membrane supports and separates the epithelial sheet from underlying connective tissue. The interstitial matrix of the connective tissue provides another set of structural and signaling relationships. Collagens, laminins, fibronectin, proteoglycans and other components contribute distinct properties to these arrangements. The basic chemistry and structural roles of these molecules are developed in Unit 4.B.04. Here the question is how a change in matrix composition or organization alters the conditions experienced by an abnormal cell population. Matrix is neither an inert filling material nor a second population of cells.

A change in matrix abundance has to be distinguished from a change in matrix organization. Two samples can contain similar amounts of collagen but display different alignment or packing of the fibers. Their accessibility to cells and the mechanical resistance encountered at a particular interface may differ. Likewise, an increase in the abundance of a constituent need not establish that the constituent has been distributed uniformly across a tissue. Measurements of total amount, spatial architecture and mechanical properties answer different questions. In a tumor, these distinctions matter because malignant cells and stromal cells occupy positions within a spatially heterogeneous structure rather than encountering an averaged matrix composition.

Matrix deposition and matrix turnover are normally coordinated. Fibroblasts and other cells can contribute extracellular constituents, while cells expressing relevant extracellular proteases can participate in their remodeling. A tissue can change because deposition has increased, because degradation has decreased, because existing constituents have been reorganized, or because several of these processes have changed together. The observation of an increased amount of matrix therefore does not identify a unique mechanism. For instance, equal rates of synthesis in two tissues do not imply equal final abundance if the removal rates differ. This is an important connection between tissue structure and the general balance equations encountered in molecular and population biology.

Matrix metalloproteinases, commonly called MMPs, illustrate a class of enzymes that can participate in extracellular remodeling. The term denotes metal-dependent proteases with particular substrate relationships; it does not mean that every member cleaves every matrix component. Their activities depend on expression, activation, localization and inhibition. A measurement of a transcript encoding one member of this class is therefore different from a demonstration of relevant extracellular cleavage. At the tissue level, the consequences of proteolysis can extend beyond the loss of a physical structure. Remodeling can alter the accessibility of binding sites, the distribution of associated factors and the relationship among nearby cells. The detailed breakdown associated with invasion is considered in 4.E.09.

Integrins connect cellular behavior to features of the extracellular surroundings. These receptors are heterodimeric proteins composed of alpha and beta subunits, with extracellular regions that participate in ligand recognition and intracellular associations that communicate with the cytoskeleton and signaling machinery. Different integrins can recognize different ligands or share overlapping ligand preferences. The cancer-relevant significance of this arrangement is that a cell can respond to matrix-associated information through its existing surface receptors. The cell does not need to acquire a new mutation each time it encounters a different matrix surface. A change in the receptor's environment can modify the signaling state of an otherwise genetically similar cell.

The term mechanotransduction refers to the conversion of a mechanical influence into a cellular response. The matrix and cell-associated structures can resist forces generated by the cell, and the resulting relationships affect the organization and activity of intracellular components. Integrin-associated adhesion structures provide an important interface through which these relationships are communicated. Focal adhesion kinase, FAK, is one example of a signaling component associated with such structures. The existence of a mechanical influence does not mean that every consequence can be calculated simply from matrix stiffness. Ligand identity, receptor state, the geometry of attachment and the properties of the cell all influence the response. The present chapter uses the principle to explain tissue context; see 4.B.05 for integrin signaling.

The mechanical context can help explain why a normal cell behaves differently on two surfaces even when the soluble environment is similar. If one surface provides an appropriate adhesive interaction and the other does not, the organization of the cell and its downstream signaling may differ. In a tissue, this distinction can influence survival and differentiation as well as shape. A malignant cell may show altered dependence on appropriate adhesion, but that does not mean that it becomes insensitive to every mechanical or extracellular influence. It can retain or acquire particular dependencies on its surroundings. Consequently, describing a tumor cell as abnormal in its attachment behavior is not sufficient to predict its response to every matrix context.

Anoikis provides a conceptual link with the preceding chapter on apoptosis. In many anchorage-dependent cells, inappropriate loss of matrix attachment can engage death-associated responses. Such a safeguard helps restrict the persistence of cells outside their appropriate tissue context. Some cancer cells show reduced sensitivity to these responses, allowing survival under conditions that would normally limit persistence. The important distinction is between altered adhesion, altered survival after loss of attachment and actual invasion of a tissue. A cell can display one of these properties without necessarily displaying all the others. The molecular sequence of invasion and dissemination belongs to the subsequent chapters, so the emphasis here is on the survival-related meaning of the cell–matrix interface.

The association of growth-related factors with extracellular structures supplies another reason why remodeling can change cellular behavior. A factor can be present in a region without being equally accessible to every nearby receptor. Binding to a matrix constituent can limit mobility or contribute to local retention. Changes in the structure can alter how much factor is locally accessible, without requiring a proportional change in the amount synthesized by a cell. This is a different mechanism from increasing receptor abundance. Both can affect the response, but they concern different stages in the communication relationship. An appropriate experiment would distinguish total abundance from local availability and from receptor-associated output.

Fibrosis and desmoplasia illustrate the tissue-level consequences of persistent stromal changes. Desmoplasia refers to a prominent fibrous stromal reaction associated with certain tumors. It is not present to the same degree in every tumor and is not itself a definition of malignancy. A dense stromal compartment can alter the physical relationships among cancer cells, vascular structures and immune populations. In some contexts, such changes can impair movement or delivery within tissue. In others, particular structural components may restrict the expansion of an altered population. An observation that a tumor contains abundant collagen therefore does not specify a universally favorable or unfavorable biological effect. Its functional importance must be established in the relevant setting.

The 2014 pancreatic cancer model discussed in the historical section provides a useful example of why structural observations cannot substitute for functional interpretation. A reduction in fibrotic material following alteration of a stromal cell population might initially appear to indicate a less restrictive environment for normal tissue function. Yet the study found that the broader disease outcome was worse in the particular model. This teaches a general lesson about the relationship between an intermediate endpoint and a tissue-level phenotype. A matrix-associated change can influence several different relationships at once, and a favorable change in one measurement does not guarantee a favorable change in all of them. The proper conclusion is conditional on the observed model and outcome.

A student interpreting a tissue image should distinguish a matrix constituent from the cells that contribute to it. An extracellular collagen-rich region is not itself a fibroblast, although fibroblasts may contribute to its formation and organization. An image showing colocalization of a fibroblast-associated marker and matrix does not prove that all of the visible matrix was produced by that particular population. Other cells and earlier states of the tissue may contribute. Conversely, a cell can influence matrix structure without producing every constituent of the matrix. This distinction is particularly important in static sections of a changing tissue. An image supplies spatial information, while causal interpretation requires additional knowledge about the relevant cellular activities.

The relationship between matrix and cell behavior is reciprocal. A cell may alter its surroundings by producing or remodeling extracellular material, and the resulting surroundings may change the signals and forces experienced by that cell or its neighbors. This creates a possible feedback relationship without requiring a direct soluble signal to pass from one cell to another. The feedback may stabilize a particular tissue state or contribute to continuing change. Its direction cannot be inferred from the existence of the interaction alone. For example, a matrix-associated change that restricts movement in one physical arrangement may have a different effect in another. The appropriate question is which cell–matrix relationship has changed and how the measured phenotype depends on it.

The study by Levental and colleagues in 2009 illustrates a specific evidential connection between matrix organization and cell signaling. Their measurements in breast cancer-associated systems linked collagen crosslinking and greater stiffness to changes in focal adhesion organization and growth-associated signaling. The relationship was examined using complementary tissue and cellular observations. This gave greater support to a mechanistic connection than would a comparison of total collagen content alone. Nevertheless, the result concerned the defined systems studied, not a universal law that every stiff tissue is malignant or that increasing the stiffness of any tissue produces a tumor. The relevant distinction is that a structural property can influence signaling in cells with particular existing regulatory states.

6. Blood vessels, angiogenesis and VEGF

All living cells require suitable conditions for metabolism. In a multicellular animal, the transport of oxygen and nutrients through the circulation helps satisfy those requirements at distances greater than those accessible by local diffusion alone. Blood vessels also provide routes for removal of metabolites and for interactions with circulating cells. In a normal organ, the vascular network is organized in relation to the physiological demands of the tissue. As an abnormal population expands, its demands can exceed the supply available through the pre-existing arrangement. This discrepancy provides a biological reason for examining vascular responses in cancer. It also explains why the growth of a cell population cannot be understood solely from the rate at which its cell-cycle machinery permits progression.

Angiogenesis is the formation of new blood vessels from existing vessels. It is an ordinary biological process in appropriate developmental, reproductive and repair contexts. Tumor-associated angiogenesis uses aspects of the same normal cellular machinery, but can occur within an abnormal tissue environment and may produce an irregular network. The endothelial cells that line the vessels participate in the response. Other cells, including perivascular cells, contribute to the organization and stability of the vascular structures. An angiogenic response is not identical to division of the malignant epithelial population. The responding vascular cells and the cancer cells whose surroundings are changed can belong to distinct cellular lineages.

The need for vascular supply is easily understood through a growing cell aggregate. Cells at the outside of an aggregate may have relatively direct access to substances in the surrounding fluid, while cells deeper within it depend on transport across intervening distances. If consumption continues and supply is limited, the local concentration of an essential substance can decline with distance. The precise distribution depends on the material, geometry and rate of use. There is no universal maximum tumor diameter that applies to every biological context. A small aggregate can also remain dependent on pre-existing vessels, and a larger tumor can contain regions with distinct degrees of oxygen availability. The simple aggregate model explains why increasing size can create an increasing transport challenge.

Vascular endothelial growth factor, VEGF, is a representative signal associated with vascular growth and regulation. VEGF-A is particularly important in the classical angiogenesis example. It can be produced by various cells, including cancer cells and stromal populations under relevant conditions. VEGF receptors are expressed on responsive vascular-associated cells; VEGFR2 is a major receptor mediating many endothelial responses to VEGF-A. The receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase, connecting the extracellular signal to intracellular regulatory machinery. The basic chemistry of receptor activation is covered in Unit 4.A. Here the important point is the direction of the communication relationship from a producing population to a responding endothelial population.

A simplified causal sequence begins with an environmental or regulatory condition associated with increased production of an angiogenic factor. The factor becomes available to responsive endothelial cells and contributes to changes in their behavior. New vascular structures may then form or existing vascular organization may change. If these structures establish functional blood flow and exchange, they can change conditions for nearby populations. Each step must be kept separate. A high level of VEGF is not itself a blood vessel, a structure resembling a vessel is not automatically perfused and blood flow does not necessarily establish equal oxygenation throughout the surrounding tissue. The relevant cancer phenotype emerges from the sequence rather than the measurement of any one step alone.

The term angiogenic switch is used to describe a transition toward an environment in which proangiogenic influences outweigh restraints sufficiently to permit vascular growth. The concept is useful because it directs attention toward a balance of signals rather than a single absolute concentration. Normal tissues can contain both influences that support vascular growth and influences that restrict it. Changes in the activity or abundance of these influences can shift the balance. The term should not be interpreted as a universally abrupt molecular switch that every tumor activates at the same time. Different tissues can acquire vascular support through different histories, and the relevant transition may be gradual or spatially restricted.

New vascular structures do not necessarily reproduce the arrangement of the corresponding normal organ. Tumor-associated networks may display irregular branching, variable vessel caliber, incomplete support and heterogeneous permeability. These features can be associated with uneven delivery within the tissue. The presence of many vascular profiles in a histological section therefore does not establish that every region receives adequate blood flow. A vessel can be present yet poorly functional, and a cell may be distant from a well-perfused vessel even when other vessel-associated structures are nearby. This distinction is central to interpreting evidence about angiogenesis because the biological requirement is effective delivery and exchange, not the mere existence of structures that can be counted.

Endothelial cells form the cellular lining of blood vessels and participate in the regulation of interactions between the circulation and surrounding tissue. Perivascular supporting cells, including pericytes in appropriate microvascular contexts, contribute to vessel organization and stability. Their relative association with the endothelial layer can differ between healthy and abnormal vessels. A change in the number of endothelial-associated profiles does not necessarily indicate an equivalent change in their supporting relationships. Functional vascular behavior depends on the integrated structure. This explains why a measurement of vessel density and a measurement of vessel maturity or function can lead to different interpretations of the same tissue.

Permeability is distinct from perfusion. Perfusion concerns blood flow through a tissue or network, whereas permeability concerns the ease with which particular substances cross a barrier. A vessel with substantial permeability can allow material to escape the circulation, yet still provide inadequate distribution to the surrounding region if flow is poor or the tissue conditions restrict further movement. Conversely, well-organized vessels can support exchange without being maximally leaky. The biological importance of a change in permeability depends on the substance being considered and the transport context. Students should avoid treating leakage, blood flow and nutrient delivery as three names for the same variable.

The presence of a high local concentration of an angiogenic signal does not necessarily mean that all nearby endothelial cells respond equally. Their receptor expression, cellular state and physical surroundings can differ. Existing vascular structures can also differ in their ability to participate in a response. These considerations explain why vascular growth may occur in particular regions rather than uniformly throughout a tumor. A bulk measurement of VEGF-associated expression can therefore mask important spatial differences. Likewise, an endothelial-associated marker observed throughout a specimen does not provide a direct map of flow. Spatial information and functional measurements are necessary to connect the signal to the actual support of the malignant population.

Some tumors obtain vascular support by growing in association with existing host vessels, a phenomenon known as vessel co-option. This represents an important limitation of any claim that new vessel formation is universally required for tumor growth. In a tissue already containing a substantial vascular network, an abnormal population can encounter pre-existing vessels as it expands. The resulting relationship can supply the population without requiring that every vessel originated through a tumor-induced angiogenic response. The evidence for co-option varies among tumor types and locations. For the examination, the key distinction is between obtaining a vascular supply and specifically generating new vessels. The two concepts are related but not identical.

The predicted consequence of impaired angiogenesis depends on the reliance of the population on that process. If the growing population cannot obtain adequate supply through existing or newly formed functional vessels, restrictions on metabolism and survival may increase. However, a population with access to pre-existing vessels may respond differently, and changes in vascular function need not be proportional to changes in vessel count. The appropriate answer to an analytical question is therefore conditional. Reduced angiogenesis can limit growth when vascular support becomes limiting, but an observed reduction in vessel density is not enough to calculate the resulting change in cell number. Information about perfusion, alternative supply routes, consumption and survival is relevant.

Vascular normalization provides a useful example of the relationship between structure and function. The concept emphasizes that certain changes in abnormal vascular organization can make the remaining vessels function more effectively, at least under defined circumstances. A reduced number of immature or poorly functioning structures can coexist with improved performance of the remaining network. Jain's 2005 review explains this apparent paradox in the context of antiangiogenic research. The concept is not a universal prediction that reducing vessel density improves delivery, nor is it a treatment instruction. It shows that counting vessels alone cannot establish how efficiently a tissue receives oxygen or other substances.

An anatomical section that shows numerous vessel profiles in one region and fewer in another may appear to suggest that the first region has a better supply. This is a reasonable hypothesis, but the conclusion depends on whether the structures are functional and how the supply relates to demand. A region with high consumption can remain relatively oxygen-poor despite a substantial vascular network. A region with low consumption may have a different concentration profile at the same measured vessel density. The balance between delivery and consumption is what determines the availability of a substance at a cell. This prepares the way for the following section, which treats oxygen-responsive regulation as a response to that balance rather than to vessel number alone.

In ordinary sprouting angiogenesis, endothelial cells participate in different tasks. Cells near the leading end of a sprout can respond to directional information, while neighboring cells contribute to extension and organization of the developing vascular structure. The names tip and stalk refer to these functional positions rather than necessarily permanent cellular lineages. Later, supporting interactions contribute to organization of the resulting vessel. This division of labor explains why an angiogenic signal cannot be interpreted solely as an increase in the proliferation rate of every endothelial cell. A proliferative response is one component of a coordinated tissue process, and the organization and connectivity of that process determine whether a vascular structure can eventually contribute to circulation.

VEGF receptors are regulated in the context of other endothelial communication systems. The normal developmental examples involving VEGF and Notch signaling illustrate that neighboring endothelial cells can adopt different functional states in response to interactions within the developing structure. The general lesson for cancer is that vascular patterning depends on coordinated cellular behavior, not solely on the total number of endothelial cells. Evidence that an endothelial-associated pathway is active is therefore different from evidence that a complete and functional vascular network has formed. Students need not memorize the detailed molecular circuitry of every endothelial state to understand this distinction. The same principles of local signaling and cell specialization apply across the normal and abnormal contexts.

7. Oxygen availability and hypoxia-responsive regulation

Oxygen availability in a tissue is a consequence of delivery, movement through tissue and consumption by cells. Hypoxia refers to oxygen availability that is low relative to the relevant biological context. It is not defined in this chapter as one universal concentration at which every cell behaves identically. Different cell types and metabolic states can show different responses to a given local oxygen level. A cell may remain viable under oxygen limitation while changing its metabolism or expression of particular genes. More severe or prolonged limitations can contribute to loss of cellular function and death. The consequences therefore depend on magnitude, duration and the capacity of the cell to respond.

It is important to distinguish oxygen concentration from oxygen flux. Concentration describes the amount present per unit volume at a location, whereas flux describes movement across a unit area per unit time. A region may receive a substantial delivery of oxygen but maintain a relatively low concentration if consumption is also substantial. Conversely, a low rate of delivery does not by itself define the concentration unless other processes are specified. In a tissue, the spatial distribution is established by the balance among delivery, diffusion and use. This is the foundation for the simplified mathematical models later in the chapter. It prevents the common mistake of inferring local oxygen concentration directly from one measurement of blood flow.

Oxygen-sensitive regulation (see Semenza, 2007) involves hypoxia-inducible factors, HIFs, which are transcriptional regulators. A simplified model of HIF-1 distinguishes its oxygen-regulated alpha subunit from the beta subunit, also known as ARNT in this context. When oxygen is adequately available, oxygen-dependent hydroxylation of specific residues in HIF-1alpha can promote its recognition by the von Hippel–Lindau protein, VHL, as part of a ubiquitin-associated regulatory system. Subsequent protein turnover limits accumulation of the alpha subunit. The key is that oxygen availability influences a molecular modification that changes recognition and stability. The host gene encoding HIF-1alpha need not change its sequence whenever local oxygen availability changes.

Under sufficiently low oxygen conditions, the oxygen-dependent modification of HIF-alpha can be reduced. Greater stability of the alpha subunit can contribute to formation of a functional transcriptional complex with the beta subunit. This can influence expression of responsive genes, including genes associated with adaptation to oxygen limitation and, in relevant contexts, VEGF expression. The sequence provides a link from a physical environmental variable to regulation of transcription. It should not be represented as direct binding of oxygen to VEGF or as direct synthesis of blood vessels by HIF. A transcriptional change occurs within one cell, while a subsequent angiogenic response involves communication with responsive vascular-associated cells.

The biochemical nature of the oxygen-sensing mechanism is useful at the examination level. Prolyl hydroxylase domain proteins participate in reactions that use molecular oxygen and 2-oxoglutarate, with appropriate cofactors. The modification of HIF-alpha is therefore linked to the availability of a substrate for an enzyme-mediated process. Under conditions in which the modification is less effective, the recognition and turnover of the target can change. The recognition machinery and the proteolytic machinery are distinct from the modifying enzyme. This is another example of regulation through a sequence of molecular events rather than through a change in gene sequence. It also connects the current topic with the protein modification and turnover principles developed earlier.

VHL provides a bridge between genetic cancer mechanisms and environmentally responsive regulation. A defect affecting VHL-associated recognition can alter HIF stability even when oxygen is not limiting in the ordinary sense. This illustrates a general principle: a response normally coupled to an environmental input can be altered by a defect within the machinery that interprets the input. A high level of HIF-alpha is therefore not by itself a direct measurement of local oxygen concentration. It may reflect an oxygen-related change, altered regulatory machinery or other influences. An examination question should distinguish a direct oxygen measurement from evidence of activation of an oxygen-responsive pathway.

The example also shows how environmental and cell-autonomous effects can converge on the same readout. In one situation, the ordinary regulatory system is responding to low local oxygen availability. In another, a genetic alteration affects a component that normally regulates the stability of the transcription factor. Both situations may be associated with increased activity of selected downstream responses. Their causal histories are different. Distinguishing them requires information about the environmental condition and the state of the relevant host pathway. A measurement of the downstream factor alone is insufficient to reconstruct the history. This directly connects the chapter with the earlier discussion of tumor suppressor loss and pathway-level interpretation.

The response to oxygen limitation can involve changes in metabolism as well as expression of angiogenic signals. Cells may alter the relative contributions of metabolic pathways, uptake of nutrients and patterns of metabolite production or use. It is important not to equate every instance of increased glycolysis with hypoxia. Many cancer cells show substantial glycolytic activity even when oxygen is available, a distinction associated with the Warburg phenomenon. Conversely, mitochondrial respiration is not necessarily absent from a tumor that contains oxygen-poor regions. Metabolic states can vary among cells and over time. This chapter focuses on the relationship between local resource availability and cellular response rather than teaching the full biochemical pathways again.

The production and removal of metabolites can also influence the local chemical environment. The concentration of a metabolite depends on its production, transport, use and removal. Changes in tissue pH reflect several related processes, including acid production, buffering and exchange with the circulation. It would be too simple to assign every pH difference in a tumor to the concentration of a single metabolite. Local conditions can differ within the same tissue, and the capacity of cells to regulate their intracellular environment also matters. A measurement of extracellular pH is therefore not interchangeable with a direct measurement of intracellular pH, oxygen availability or glucose use.

A major reason to study hypoxia in cancer is its relationship to variation within a tissue. A poorly supplied region can experience a different combination of stresses from a well-supplied region. Some cells may stop dividing, some may adapt and some may die. A tumor section can therefore contain viable peripheral cells, stressed internal cells and regions of necrosis at the same time. This does not imply that every oxygen-poor region has the same composition. The history of supply and demand, the local availability of other nutrients and the regulatory properties of the cells all contribute. Such spatial variation is one reason a single bulk measurement cannot fully describe the tumor's biological state.

A response involving increased VEGF expression can be interpreted as one link between oxygen availability and vascular behavior. If an oxygen-limited cell population produces more of the factor, it may influence responsive endothelial cells in its surroundings. A change in vascular support can in turn alter local oxygen availability. However, if the resulting structures are poorly functional or the demands of the growing population continue to increase, the original limitation may not be resolved. This creates an important distinction between an attempted compensatory response and successful compensation. In an examination question, high angiogenic signal abundance together with persistent hypoxia is not necessarily contradictory.

When interpreting a report of hypoxia-associated markers, the student should identify the variable actually measured. A measurement of local oxygen concentration addresses the physical environment. Measurement of HIF-alpha abundance addresses a molecular state that can be influenced by that environment. Measurement of a downstream transcript addresses a further consequence that can also depend on other regulators. These variables occupy different positions in the proposed causal sequence. Observing two of them together strengthens some interpretations but does not eliminate every alternative. The distinction is useful not only in cancer biology but also in other contexts in which a physical signal is converted into a transcriptional response.

The oxygen-responsive modification system also illustrates that protein stability and transcriptional competence are related but separable. A second oxygen-dependent regulatory influence, associated with factor inhibiting HIF, can affect interactions between HIF-alpha and transcriptional coactivators. The two aspects answer different molecular questions: how much of the regulatory protein is available, and how effectively the available protein can participate in transcription. This explains why a measurement of HIF-alpha abundance cannot specify the output of every HIF-responsive gene. The expression of an individual target also depends on its regulatory context. The simpler VHL-associated turnover model remains useful, provided that its predicted result is not confused with the complete activity of the transcriptional system.

At the level of chemical balance, the oxygen-dependent hydroxylation reaction involves molecular oxygen and 2-oxoglutarate and produces succinate and carbon dioxide as associated products. Iron participates as a required catalytic cofactor in the relevant dioxygenase chemistry. The oxygen dependence of the reaction provides a mechanistic explanation for how availability of oxygen can influence a covalent modification of a protein. It is distinct from the subsequent ubiquitin-dependent recognition and turnover of that protein. This separation is useful when reading a pathway diagram because the chemical modification, substrate recognition and proteolytic removal are not interchangeable events. Each has its own molecular participants and can be affected by different conditions.

8. Immune interactions: restraint and promotion

The immune system has an important relationship with cancer because an abnormal host cell can differ from the surrounding normal population in ways that affect recognition and removal. The relevant responses involve several cell types and mechanisms already studied in Unit 4.C. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes can recognize appropriate antigen-derived peptide–MHC complexes and contribute to elimination of target cells. Natural killer cells respond to a combination of activating and inhibitory information associated with potential targets. These responses should not be collapsed into one identical recognition pathway. The cancer-relevant principle is that the future contribution of a malignant lineage depends partly on whether its cells remain subject to effective restriction by other cells of the organism.

An antigen is a molecular structure capable of being recognized by an appropriate component of the adaptive immune system. The presence of an altered gene in a cancer cell does not guarantee that the corresponding protein generates a peptide displayed in a relevant recognition context. The protein must be expressed and processed, and the appropriate presentation and recognition relationships must be available. Even if recognition occurs, the resulting cellular response depends on other conditions. Therefore the observation of a genetic difference between cancer and normal cells is not equivalent to proof of effective immune-mediated removal. The distinction is especially important when comparing genomic evidence with evidence of the actual behavior of immune cells in a tissue.

A second distinction concerns the presence of immune cells and their functional activity. An immune cell can be observed within a tissue section without demonstrating that it has recognized or eliminated a malignant cell. The measured abundance may reflect recruitment, retention, survival or a combination of these processes. The location of the cells relative to malignant populations can also be relevant. Two tumors with the same total number of lymphocytes per unit area might differ in whether those lymphocytes are predominantly located around the malignant compartment or within it. Spatial and functional evidence provide different information from a simple count. Neither should automatically be substituted for the other.

Cancer immunoediting describes the dual relationship between immune responses and the development of malignant populations. In the commonly taught framework, elimination refers to immune-mediated restriction or removal of abnormal cells. Equilibrium describes a situation in which residual abnormal cells are constrained while the population and its composition can change. Escape refers to a state in which a malignant population persists and expands despite the immune response. These concepts identify possible relationships between tumor growth and immune control. They do not require the three states to be identifiable as distinct consecutive periods in every patient. Nor does escape mean that every immune cell disappears from the tissue.

The concept of equilibrium is useful because an apparently stable population size can conceal active processes. Cells may continue to be produced and removed, and some lineages may become more or less represented over time. A constant count can therefore coexist with changes in composition. If restriction acts differently on different lineages, the surviving population need not retain the original distribution of cellular properties. This is an example of selection within a tissue environment. The important connection with the previous cancer chapters is that differences in reproductive contribution can be shaped by interactions with other cells, not only by the intrinsic division rates of the malignant populations.

Macrophages illustrate why the phrase immune cell should not be treated as a single functional category. Macrophage populations participate in recognition and removal of material, signaling and tissue-associated responses. Their properties can vary with the local environment and developmental history. Tumor-associated macrophages are defined by their association with tumor tissue, not by one inevitable function. Some responses can contribute to restriction of abnormal cells; other responses can be associated with tissue remodeling, altered vascular conditions or regulation of immune activity. The outcome must be linked to a defined population and observed function rather than inferred from the general name of the cell type.

Textbook diagrams often distinguish inflammatory and repair-associated macrophage states using simplified categories. Such distinctions can be useful for illustrating that immune cells can participate in different physiological tasks. However, actual macrophage populations need not fall into two sharply separated and universally stable classes. A cell's state can change with its environment, and a tissue may contain cells displaying a combination of features. Students should therefore use a simplified classification to interpret the stated data rather than assume that every cell expressing one marker has all the properties associated with a textbook category. This is especially important in a heterogeneous tissue in which several signals may coexist.

Regulatory T cells provide another example of how a normally useful immune function can have different consequences in a tumor context. Regulation of immune activity is essential for maintaining tolerance and limiting excessive tissue damage. Within a tumor, however, regulatory influences can modify the ability of other immune populations to restrict malignant cells. The relevant biological question is whether the balance of activities permits effective control of the abnormal population. It is not whether immune regulation is intrinsically harmful in all tissues. A process that serves an important normal function can have a different consequence when the tissue context and the population being regulated have changed.

Immune checkpoints are regulatory relationships that help govern the strength and duration of immune responses. The PD-1 and PD-L1 relationship is a familiar example in cancer biology. PD-1 is an inhibitory receptor expressed by relevant immune populations, and its ligands can be expressed in several cellular contexts, including some malignant and nonmalignant cells. The interaction can influence the activity of responding lymphocytes. The presence of either molecule alone does not establish the full functional state of the tissue. Other regulatory inputs and the location and condition of the cells are also important. Detailed therapeutic targeting belongs to 4.E.12; here the example illustrates why a tumor may contain immune cells without being effectively eliminated.

An important normal role of inflammation is to coordinate responses to injury or infection. The signals associated with these responses can influence survival, proliferation and remodeling in nearby tissues. In a persistent abnormal setting, some of these activities may contribute to a local environment in which an altered lineage continues to expand. The existence of a tumor-associated inflammatory response does not establish that inflammation initiated the cancer, nor does it imply that every inflammatory cell has the same function. The time course matters: an immune response may initially be associated with tissue damage, later contribute to repair, or participate in several processes at once. Causal conclusions must be linked to the particular stage being examined.

The comparison between immune restraint and immune-associated promotion can be framed in terms of measurable endpoints. If the question concerns elimination of malignant cells, evidence about their loss following a relevant immune response is directly relevant. If it concerns altered tissue organization, changes in matrix, vascular conditions or signaling may be more informative. A change in the abundance of an immune population cannot automatically be translated into either outcome. For example, increased accumulation of a cell type could coexist with little change in the rate of malignant-cell removal. The best interpretation identifies the specific relationship that the observations support instead of labeling the entire infiltrate as favorable or unfavorable.

The microenvironment also affects the conditions under which immune cells themselves function. Local resources, tissue architecture and interactions with neighboring cells can influence their behavior. The immune population is therefore both a participant in the environment and a recipient of environmental influences. This is another example of reciprocity. A change in a stromal compartment can be associated with a change in immune composition even though the two populations are not identical. A change in vascular function can influence the relationship between circulating cells and the local tissue. These interactions explain why analysis of the immune response cannot always be separated from analysis of the physical and cellular structure of the tumor.

The historical concept of immunosurveillance emphasized the ability of immune responses to recognize and restrict abnormal cells. Immunoediting extends that concept by considering the possibility that the surviving population has been shaped by those responses. This distinction does not require assuming that immune activity always contributes to cancer. A protective response can reduce the total number of abnormal cells while changing the composition of those that remain. The observation of a later tumor does not mean that the earlier response had no effect. Conversely, the detection of tumor-associated immune cells does not prove that effective surveillance is occurring at the measured time. The conclusion must be grounded in the relevant temporal and functional evidence.

9. Spatial heterogeneity and microenvironmental selection

The concept of heterogeneity can be understood at two distinct levels. Genetic heterogeneity refers to differences in genetic information among cells. Phenotypic heterogeneity refers to differences in measurable properties or behavior, which may arise from genetic differences, differences in regulation or differences in the environment. Cells sharing a substantial portion of their genetic history can occupy different states because their local conditions differ. Conversely, genetically distinct lineages can exhibit similar responses under a given condition. In a tumor, these forms of heterogeneity often coexist. The observation of a different protein expression pattern in one region does not automatically establish that the cells in that region belong to a genetically distinct clone.

Spatial variation is a predictable consequence of a tissue in which delivery and consumption occur at different locations. Oxygen, nutrients and locally produced signals can show gradients. Matrix organization can vary among regions, and immune populations may be distributed unevenly. These properties mean that the environment experienced by one cell can differ substantially from the environment experienced by another cell only a short distance away. The important biological consequence is that an average value calculated across the tissue can obscure the conditions relevant to each population. A region containing relatively few cells may still be important if those cells encounter conditions very different from those of the majority.

Temporal variation is equally important. Blood flow, expression of stress-associated factors and the composition of nearby cell populations can change over time. A cell observed in a particular state at one moment may later adopt a different state if the surroundings change. This does not establish that every state is fully reversible, nor does it establish that a genetic change has occurred whenever expression differs. The appropriate distinction is between an observed response and the mechanism that makes it persist. A short-term environmental response may depend on continuing exposure to a condition, whereas a heritable change can be transmitted to descendants even when the original condition has changed.

Selection refers to differences in the contribution of individuals or lineages to later populations. In the context of a tumor, the relevant contributions include production of descendants and the probability that those descendants remain in the population. If an environmental condition affects two lineages differently, their relative representation can change even when the condition does not create a new genetic alteration. This is distinct from the production of new variation. An observation of changed clone frequency therefore does not automatically prove that the environment increased the mutation rate. It may instead have altered the relative success of variants that were already present. This distinction was introduced in the cancer fundamentals chapter and is essential for tissue-level reasoning.

A simple example helps separate absolute and relative change. Suppose two populations both lose cells in a stressful region, but one population loses a greater proportion than the other. The less affected population can become a larger fraction of the surviving cells even though its own absolute number has declined. The observation of enrichment is therefore not equivalent to evidence that the enriched population has proliferated. This matters when interpreting measurements that report fractions rather than absolute counts. An increase in the representation of a particular phenotype can reflect differences in survival, differences in proliferation or changes in the denominator caused by other populations. Additional observations are needed to identify the mechanism.

The local environment can also change the expression of a phenotype without changing the relative number of pre-existing lineages. If cells respond to a change in oxygen availability by altering expression of a responsive gene, the fraction of cells displaying the marker may increase. This can happen even if no population has become more abundant relative to another. The change reflects regulation within cells rather than a change in lineage composition. In practice, both regulatory changes and shifts in composition may contribute to a measured difference. A single measurement of the fraction of marker-positive cells cannot necessarily distinguish these possibilities.

These distinctions explain why a tumor cannot be represented adequately as a uniform population exposed to one uniform signal. A cell near a well-perfused region may have access to conditions different from those of a cell near a poorly supplied region. Its interaction with matrix and immune cells may also differ. The consequences of an intrinsic regulatory alteration can therefore vary across the tissue. This does not mean that every difference in cellular behavior is caused by the microenvironment. Rather, the environmental explanation becomes a testable alternative to a purely cell-autonomous explanation. The student's task is to identify what evidence would discriminate the two possibilities in the specific question.

The relationship between selection and reciprocal interactions adds another layer of complexity. A changing population can alter the surroundings by changing the total amount of material consumed or released and by modifying the organization of the tissue. The changed surroundings can affect the future behavior of the populations. Thus the environment is not always a fixed external condition. Some of its properties emerge from the activities of the populations themselves. This feedback explains why a tissue-level state may change even when no single component can be identified as the sole cause. It also explains why an observation made at a late stage may be a consequence of earlier population changes as well as a possible influence on subsequent events.

The comparison with the previous chapter on virus-associated cancer is useful. Chronic tissue injury can create repeated demands for regeneration, and altered cellular lineages can respond differently to the resulting conditions. A viral contribution to a particular intracellular regulatory pathway is distinct from the tissue-level influence of repeated injury. These influences may coexist. The microenvironmental explanation does not replace evidence about a viral protein or host genetic alteration; it describes another level of the causal system. This principle also applies to noninfectious causes of cancer, where the interplay between altered cells and their surroundings can modify the subsequent behavior of the tissue.

The next chapter on local invasion examines how abnormal cells acquire or express the ability to cross tissue boundaries. The present chapter explains why those boundaries and the surrounding structures matter before considering the steps of crossing them. A cell's interaction with its environment can influence survival, organization and movement without necessarily establishing invasion. Invasion requires evidence that the relevant population has extended beyond the normal boundary. The distinction prevents the inappropriate inference of an advanced cancer-associated capability from an isolated matrix-associated marker or a change in the local signaling environment.

10. Interpreting evidence from mixed tissues

An important first step in interpreting a microenvironment study is to identify the population in which a molecular change was measured. Whole-tissue measurements can contain contributions from malignant cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and immune populations. If the abundance of a transcript increases in the whole specimen, at least two broad explanations are possible. Individual cells may express more of the transcript, or the specimen may contain a greater proportion of cells that already express it. Both can occur together. The observation is a valid tissue-level result, but the cellular source and mechanism cannot be assigned without further evidence. This is a recurring problem in examination questions about mixed biological samples.

A hypothetical example makes the ambiguity clear. Consider a tissue in which fibroblasts express a matrix-associated transcript at a high level and malignant cells express it at a low level. A sample containing a higher fraction of fibroblasts can show greater average expression even if the expression within each cell type is unchanged. If the analysis were restricted to the malignant cells, the apparent difference might disappear. Conversely, a change within the malignant population could be hidden in the bulk average if the composition of the tissue also changes. The meaningful denominator and the composition of the sample are therefore essential parts of the interpretation.

Microscopy and spatial localization provide evidence that is complementary to bulk measurements. A stain associated with endothelial cells can help identify vascular structures in a tissue section. A stain associated with a fibroblast population can help locate a stromal compartment. A matrix-associated signal can show the distribution of particular extracellular material. However, the meaning of each signal depends on its specificity and the context of the measurement. A marker may not identify every member of a population or may also be expressed in another context. An image showing two signals in nearby regions supports proximity, but proximity alone does not prove direct molecular interaction or a particular direction of causal influence.

An anatomical measurement of vessel density is informative when the question concerns the abundance of vascular structures. It does not directly establish the rate of flow through them. Functional evidence of perfusion addresses a different variable. A measurement of tissue oxygen availability then addresses the result of delivery, transport and consumption. These three observations can be related without being interchangeable. A report that vessel density decreases while perfusion is maintained or improved is therefore not inherently contradictory. It suggests that some property of vascular function or the relationship between supply and demand has changed. The precise explanation requires information beyond the vessel count alone.

The distinction between correlation and causation is particularly important in the interpretation of fibroblast-associated observations. If a particular stromal population is more abundant in advanced tumors than in less advanced tumors, its abundance is associated with the observed stage. The association does not establish whether the population contributed to progression, responded to changes in the tumor, or was influenced by another variable affecting both. A comparison that examines the consequence of a defined change in the population provides different evidence. Even then, the interpretation must account for other changes that may accompany the perturbation. The 2014 mouse study is instructive because it measured both stromal changes and broader outcomes instead of relying solely on association.

The 1995 spheroid study provides a complementary example of an environmentally responsive variable. The association of VEGF expression with inner regions was informative because the spatial context differed from the exterior of the aggregate. The reported change in expression when oxygen limitation was relieved further supported a relationship between the environmental condition and expression. That comparison addresses the regulation of the factor. It does not directly establish the subsequent formation of functional vessels, which would require a different kind of evidence. The conclusion must stop at the level measured unless additional observations support later steps. This is an excellent model for interpreting causal chains in examination problems.

The comparison of vascular density and vascular function developed in the normalization literature also illustrates the need to define the endpoint. A change in the structure of a vascular network may be associated with changes in the delivery of material to the tissue. Measurements of the structural and functional properties are both important because the direction of change need not be the same. The 2004 work by Tong and colleagues reported improvements in properties of surviving vessels and in delivery-related conditions in a defined experimental context. The result cannot be generalized to every tumor or every possible intervention. Its educational significance is the demonstration that vascular quantity and functional transport can diverge.

A conceptual comparison between a malignant population alone and the same population in the presence of a defined nonmalignant compartment can help distinguish a cell-autonomous phenotype from a context-dependent one. If a measured response changes with the surrounding compartment despite comparable genetic information in the malignant cells, that observation supports an environmental contribution. The comparison does not identify which of the many possible interactions is responsible. It may reflect soluble signals, matrix-associated effects, differences in available resources or contact-dependent communication. Additional evidence about those variables is needed before one mechanism can be assigned. The appropriate inference is that the context matters for the observed response, not that genetics has become irrelevant.

A final question concerns whether the measurements describe the same cells at the same time. A tissue can contain a population showing an oxygen-associated response and a different population showing an immune-associated response. Bulk evidence of both does not establish that every malignant cell experiences both conditions. Similarly, two measurements from different times can describe a changing tissue rather than simultaneous features. Good examination reasoning identifies the unit of observation, the timing and the spatial resolution of the supplied evidence. The conclusion should be restricted to the association that the measurements actually establish. This principle connects the topic to the methods of biological investigation taught in the final unit.

11. Quantitative reasoning about transport and population behavior

Quantitative reasoning begins by specifying what is measured and which assumptions are being made. A tissue contains many interacting variables, and a simple equation is useful only when its variables correspond to a defined biological question. For example, vessel density can be represented as a count per unit area in a section, while perfusion can be represented as a volume of blood delivered per unit tissue mass per unit time. These measurements have different dimensions and cannot be equated numerically. A concentration of oxygen has units of amount per volume, while a flux has units of amount per area per time. Recognizing the dimensions prevents a student from treating distinct biological quantities as interchangeable.

A simple diffusion model provides a useful starting point for understanding how a local substance can vary with position. Consider an idealized one-dimensional region with concentration C measured in mol per cubic metre and position x measured in metres. Fick's first law expresses the flux J as −D dC/dx, where D is a diffusion coefficient in square metres per second. The negative sign means that the net diffusive flux is directed toward lower concentration. The concentration gradient has units mol per fourth power of metre, so multiplication by D gives mol per square metre per second. This is the appropriate dimension for flux. The equation assumes a continuum description and a suitable effective diffusion coefficient; it is not a complete description of transport in a real vascular network.

For a simple linear concentration profile, the magnitude of the flux is D times the concentration difference divided by the distance across which that difference occurs. Suppose two idealized regions have the same concentration difference and diffusion coefficient, but the distance is twice as large in the second. The magnitude of the gradient and therefore the predicted flux are half as large. The calculation explains why transport distance matters without assigning a universal distance limit to a biological tissue. Real concentration profiles need not remain linear because consumption, local sources and structural variation can change the gradient. The model is appropriate only when the stated assumptions apply.

Diffusion time provides another simple scaling relationship. In one-dimensional free diffusion, the mean squared displacement grows as 2Dt, where D is the diffusion coefficient and t is time. Setting a representative squared distance equal to this quantity gives a characteristic time scale of L squared divided by 2D. It follows that doubling the distance increases this characteristic time fourfold if D remains fixed. The relationship refers to a statistical displacement scale, not the exact travel time of each individual molecule. It helps explain why longer distances can become important in tissue transport. In a real tumor, binding, consumption, geometry and fluid movement can change the relationship between a physical distance and effective delivery.

Consumption can be included in a simplified material balance. Let C denote the local concentration of a substance, S its net supply rate per unit volume and U its removal or use rate per unit volume. For an idealized well-mixed compartment, dC/dt = S − U. All three terms have units of concentration per time. If S exceeds U, the concentration increases within the model; if U exceeds S, it decreases. If the terms are equal, the concentration remains constant even though supply and use continue. The model is useful because a lower concentration can arise from reduced supply, greater consumption or both. It does not describe spatial gradients, so it must not be used to infer uniformity within an actual tissue.

The same conservation principle can be written for an extracellular matrix component in a defined region. Let M be its amount, P its deposition rate and R its removal rate. The balance is dM/dt = P − R. A stable amount does not imply that the component is not being synthesized or removed; it can reflect equal ongoing rates. If a comparison shows greater M in one region at a particular time, the observation does not establish that P was higher at every earlier time. A different removal rate or starting amount could account for the difference. This model connects the matrix section with the experimental distinction between abundance and turnover.

A quantitative model of mixed tissue measurements illustrates the role of composition. Suppose a specimen contains two populations A and B with fractions f and 1−f. If each cell contributes equally to a simple measurement and the mean signals per cell are a and b, the mean signal in the specimen is f a + (1−f)b. The expression is a weighted mean. If a and b remain fixed but the fraction f changes, the bulk signal can change without any change in expression within either population. The calculation assumes the stated linear measurement and equal contributions per cell. Actual assays can have different normalization properties, so the weighted mean is an idealized illustration rather than a universal formula for every molecular measurement.

The relative representation of two lineages can be calculated independently of their absolute growth. Suppose the initial numbers are A0 and B0, and the respective net retention or expansion multipliers over a defined interval are mA and mB. The final numbers are A0 mA and B0 mB under the assumption that no cells switch between the two categories and there is no additional migration. The fraction of lineage A is A0 mA divided by the sum of A0 mA and B0 mB. The result is dimensionless. A fraction greater than the initial value indicates enrichment, but does not prove that A increased in absolute number. Both multipliers could be less than one, indicating loss of cells from both populations.

When considering a vascular observation, it can be helpful to distinguish the number of structures from an average contribution per structure. In a deliberately simplified arithmetic model, total effective delivery T is the sum of the contributions q from individual vessels. If n vessels each contribute an equal mean amount q per unit time, T = nq. A change in n can be offset by a change in q. For example, reducing n to one half while doubling q leaves the modeled total unchanged. This is not a physiological equation for calculating real tissue perfusion: vessel organization, network connectivity, pressure differences and exchange make the actual relationship more complex. The toy model explains why a count alone is insufficient.

The interpretation of a percentage requires special care when multiple cell populations are present. Suppose the number of lymphocytes in a defined region remains constant while the number of malignant cells increases. The lymphocyte fraction among all cells can decline even though no lymphocytes have been lost. If the question asks about recruitment or removal of immune cells, a change in the fraction alone is insufficient. A similar problem arises when comparing the fraction of vessel-associated profiles in samples of different area. The appropriate denominator must be specified. A density per unit area, a fraction among all cells and an absolute count in a defined region answer different questions.

A quantitative comparison also depends on whether the measured values represent independent observations. Several fields sampled from one tumor can help describe variation within that tumor, but they are not necessarily equivalent to the same number of independently sampled tumors. Cells within one region share aspects of their environment. A large number of measured cells does not automatically provide the same information as a large number of independent biological specimens. The distinction is especially relevant in spatially heterogeneous tissue. At NET/SET level, the important principle is to identify the biological unit that the conclusion concerns and avoid counting repeated measurements as independent evidence for a population-level claim.

12. Integrating molecular and tissue-level explanations

The main organizing idea of this chapter is that a tumor phenotype emerges from interactions among populations and physical conditions. A genetic alteration within a cancer cell can change its own signaling, but its effects can extend beyond that cell if the altered population modifies the surroundings. A nonmalignant population can respond through normal regulatory machinery and thereby change the conditions experienced by the cancer cells. The resulting effect can be reinforcing or restraining. This is why a tissue-level explanation should identify the source, route, receiver and measured consequence of an interaction. The framework is more informative than treating every stromal or immune cell as uniformly supportive of cancer.

The representative examples illustrate distinct levels of biological regulation. The fibroblast example connects changes in local signaling with matrix production and organization. The integrin example connects extracellular structures with intracellular regulatory activity. The angiogenesis example connects a signal produced in one population with a response in endothelial cells and a potential change in tissue supply. The hypoxia example connects a physical condition with oxygen-sensitive protein regulation and transcription. The immune example connects recognition, regulation and the relative persistence of altered cells. These are not separate unrelated facts; they show how ordinary physiological mechanisms can become part of an abnormal tissue-level relationship.

The same framework explains why a simple measurement of one component cannot establish the behavior of the whole tissue. Increased VEGF expression does not prove effective oxygen delivery. Increased matrix abundance does not establish a uniformly supportive or restrictive effect. Increased abundance of an immune marker does not prove successful elimination. A change in the fraction of a cell population does not by itself establish a change in its absolute count or proliferation. Each measurement provides information at a particular level. The strongest interpretation connects the measured variable with the specific hypothesis while acknowledging alternatives that remain possible.

At the level of therapeutic principles, a process that supports the tissue environment can be relevant even when it is not encoded by a mutation in the malignant cells. The history of angiogenesis research illustrates this idea. At the same time, the diversity of fibroblast functions and vascular responses shows why a change in an intermediate component cannot be assumed to have a uniformly beneficial outcome. Interventions that change the local environment must be understood in relation to their actual biological consequences. The detailed treatment approaches, pharmacology and clinical considerations belong to later chapters; the present discussion establishes the conceptual reason for considering both malignant and nonmalignant compartments.

The syllabus places interaction of cancer cells with normal cells alongside oncogenes, tumor suppressors, cell-cycle regulation and metastasis because these subjects address complementary aspects of cancer. The preceding chapters establish how the behavior of an altered lineage can arise from changes in its regulatory machinery. This chapter explains why the consequences of those changes depend on the tissue in which the lineage exists. The following chapter on invasion develops the relationship between malignant cells and tissue boundaries more specifically. A complete account of cancer requires these levels to be connected rather than regarded as competing explanations.

Worked analytical examples

1. Diffusion flux

Hypothetical D = 2 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s; concentration difference 0.10 mol/m³; distance 2 × 10⁻⁴ m. Magnitude J = DΔC/L = 1 × 10⁻⁶ mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Doubling distance gives 5 × 10⁻⁷. This assumes a linear profile and constant D.

2. Diffusion time scaling

If L doubles at fixed D, L²/(2D) increases fourfold. This is a characteristic displacement time, not exact molecular travel time.

3. Mixed-tissue mean

Hypothetical fibroblast signal 8 units, epithelial signal 2 units. At fibroblast fraction 0.25, mean = 0.25×8+0.75×2=3.5. At fraction 0.50, mean=5.0. No change within either group is required.

4. Selection without expansion

A starts at 80, B at 20. Final counts are 40 and 5. A fraction rises from 80% to 40/45=88.9% despite absolute loss of 40 cells.

5. Vascular count versus delivery

A simplified sum of contributions: 100 structures at 0.5 unit each gives 50 units; 60 at 0.9 each gives 54. Structure count decreases by 40% while modeled total increases by 8%. This is not a real vascular-flow calculation.

6. Matrix balance

A hypothetical region deposits 12 units/day and removes 8 units/day. At fixed rates, net gain=4 units/day; after 5 days, 20 units accumulate. The amount at the end also depends on starting material.

7. Cell-count denominators

A region has 100 immune cells and 400 other cells initially; later 100 immune cells and 900 other cells. Fractions are 20% and 10%. The absolute immune count is unchanged.

8. Local supply–use balance

A well-mixed hypothetical compartment has supply 3 and use 5 concentration units/h. With constant rates for 2 h, concentration falls 4 units. The same change could result from other supply/use pairs with the same net difference; this is not a spatial model.

Part C — Original analytical practice

1. A hypothetical tissue contains fibroblasts with mean signal 10 and cancer cells with mean signal 2. Fibroblast fraction rises from 20% to 50%, with unchanged within-group signals. What is the bulk mean change?

  1. 3.6 to 6.0
  2. 2 to 10
  3. 8.4 to 6.0
  4. No change
Answer and explanation

A — 3.6 to 6.0. 0.2×10+0.8×2=3.6; 0.5×10+0.5×2=6.0. Composition changes alone explain the difference under the linear assumptions.

2. A region has 50 vessel profiles contributing a mean of 2 hypothetical delivery units each. Later it has 40 profiles at 3 units each. What follows under the stated additive model?

  1. Total falls by 20% because count falls
  2. Total increases from 100 to 120 units while count falls by 20%
  3. Total stays at 100 units
  4. The tissue necessarily becomes fully oxygenated
Answer and explanation

B — Total increases from 100 to 120 units while count falls by 20%. 50×2=100 and 40×3=120; count falls 10/50=20%. The toy model does not determine actual oxygenation.

3. Two idealized regions have equal concentration differences and D, but distances of L and 3L. What is the ratio of flux magnitudes, short:long?

  1. 1:3
  2. 9:1
  3. 3:1
  4. 1:9
Answer and explanation

C — 3:1. For linear profiles J magnitude is DΔC/L, giving 3:1. Ninefold scaling pertains to a diffusion time comparison, not steady linear flux.

4. Under the one-dimensional free diffusion scaling t=L²/(2D), what happens when distance becomes three times larger at fixed D?

  1. It becomes three times larger
  2. It is unchanged
  3. It decreases to one ninth
  4. The characteristic time becomes nine times larger
Answer and explanation

D — The characteristic time becomes nine times larger. (3L)²/L²=9. This is a characteristic scale, not the exact path time of a molecule.

5. In a hypothetical tissue, 60 A and 40 B cells become 30 A and 10 B after an interval. Which is correct?

  1. A rises from 60% to 75% despite losing 30 cells
  2. A grows by 15 cells
  3. B grows from 40% to 75%
  4. Both lineages expand
Answer and explanation

A — A rises from 60% to 75% despite losing 30 cells. Final total=40; A=30/40=75%. Relative enrichment does not imply absolute proliferation.

6. A defined tissue region contains 200 immune cells among 800 total cells, then 200 among 1,600. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. Half the immune cells die
  2. Fraction falls from 25% to 12.5% without an absolute immune-cell decline
  3. The absolute count doubles
  4. Immune-mediated killing necessarily stops
Answer and explanation

B — Fraction falls from 25% to 12.5% without an absolute immune-cell decline. The numerator stays 200, while the denominator doubles. The observation does not establish immune function.

7. A hypothetical matrix compartment begins with 40 units, deposition is 9 units/day and removal 6 units/day for 4 days. Final amount?

  1. 76 units
  2. 16 units
  3. 52 units
  4. 12 units
Answer and explanation

C — 52 units. Net accumulation is 3×4=12; adding 40 gives 52. The other values omit a term or the initial amount.

8. A hypothetical well-mixed compartment starts at 12 concentration units. Supply is 4 and removal is 6 units/h for 3 h. Which is correct?

  1. Final is 24
  2. Final is 18
  3. The spatial concentration profile is uniquely known
  4. Final concentration is 6 units under the stated constant-rate model
Answer and explanation

D — Final concentration is 6 units under the stated constant-rate model. Net rate is −2 units/h; 12−6=6. A well-mixed model does not determine a real tissue gradient.

  1. Expression of a signal and functional vascular outcome are distinct readouts
  2. Both necessarily have equal oxygen supply
  3. The sample without increased perfusion cannot contain VEGF
  4. VEGF RNA identifies all vessel structures
Answer and explanation

A — Expression of a signal and functional vascular outcome are distinct readouts. Similar expression does not specify downstream response, flow or delivery. Other variables can differ.

  2. The molecular regulatory state can differ despite equal environmental oxygen
  3. VHL is an oxygen molecule
  4. HIF is necessarily absent from both groups
Answer and explanation

B — The molecular regulatory state can differ despite equal environmental oxygen. The state of the recognition/turnover machinery can affect HIF. Oxygen and protein abundance are distinct measurements.

  4. All fibroblasts in both samples have identical activity
Answer and explanation

  2. Matrix abundance is the sole determinant of survival
  3. The result establishes that no fibroblast can promote cancer
  4. The data contradict a universal claim that removing this stromal population always improves the outcome

  4. The tissue cannot be malignant
