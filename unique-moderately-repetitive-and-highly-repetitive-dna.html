1. Why DNA quantity is not the same as genetic information

When a chromosome is described as a DNA molecule carrying genes, it is tempting to imagine a long sequence in which almost every stretch is a separate instruction. However, if the DNA of a typical eukaryotic cell is examined, some stretches occur only once, some occur in a few related copies, and others occur again and again. Two genomes can contain very different amounts of DNA without having proportionately different numbers of protein-coding genes. The central problem of this chapter is to understand why counting DNA bases is not equivalent to counting distinct sequences or genes.

A genome is the complete genetic material of a specified biological system. In the present chapter, unless otherwise stated, copy numbers refer to one haploid nuclear genome, meaning one chromosome set. A diploid cell has two homologous sets, and a cell that has replicated its DNA has additional physical molecules without necessarily having acquired additional genomic loci. A locus is a specified position in a genome. These distinctions matter because a sequence at a single locus on each homolog is not classified as repetitive simply because a diploid cell has two homologous copies. Repetition refers to occurrences within the reference genome, rather than the normal doubling that accompanies the cell cycle or ploidy.

A sequence can be considered at several different scales. A four-base motif such as AGTC will occur repeatedly by chance in any sufficiently large genome. A particular several-hundred-base stretch, with its characteristic order of bases, may nevertheless occur at only one locus. Thus, when we speak of unique DNA we normally mean a biologically or experimentally meaningful stretch of sequence, not a single base or every possible short word within it. Homology is similarity attributable to shared ancestry; detectable sequence similarity can be used as evidence of relatedness but is not by itself proof of a common function. Repeated copies can have accumulated substitutions or interruptions since their origin. The required degree of similarity depends on how a repeat is detected.

Students should already know that complementary DNA strands are antiparallel and pair through A–T and G–C base pairing, that a base pair (bp) describes a position in duplex DNA, and that 1 kilobase (kb) equals 1,000 bp. The chapter on nucleic acid topology and molecular stability supplies the physical basis of duplex melting and reassociation. Here those principles become tools for discovering how information is distributed across chromosomes.

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to classify a repeat by abundance and arrangement independently, distinguish a repeat unit from an array and a repeat family, explain the classic evidence for repeated DNA, interpret a reassociation curve with either vertical-axis convention, calculate copy numbers and fractions, and predict the consequences of changing the repeat number or experimental conditions. These abilities provide the basis for understanding gene families, chromosome landmarks and mobile genetic elements in the following chapters.

2. Single-copy sequences, repeated sequences and genomic complexity

A single-copy sequence has one distinguishable occurrence in a haploid genome at the resolution and similarity threshold being used. A single-copy protein-coding gene is one example, but a unique intron, a unique intergenic segment or a regulatory region may also be single-copy DNA. Conversely, repeated DNA is not necessarily noncoding: the genes producing ribosomal RNA are repeated in many organisms. The two questions — how often a sequence occurs and what it does — must be answered separately.

Suppose a haploid DNA molecule consists of 80,000 bp of entirely distinct sequence and ten identical copies of a 2,000-bp region. Its physical length is 100,000 bp. If we represented each distinct sequence once, the length would be 82,000 bp. This latter quantity is an idealized sequence complexity. Although both physical length and complexity can be stated in bp, they describe different aspects of the genome. Copying an existing sequence increases the physical amount of DNA much more than it increases the amount of distinct information measured by this definition.

Low-copy, moderately repetitive and highly repetitive are relative abundance categories. In classical treatments, moderately repetitive families may contain tens to thousands of homologous copies, while highly repetitive families often contain thousands to millions. These descriptions overlap and are not universal cutoffs. Some textbooks separate low-copy repeats as their own category. A sequence present twice or a few times is not strictly single-copy, but its reassociation behavior may be too close to the single-copy component to resolve. In an examination question, follow the explicitly defined copy-number convention rather than treating a particular numerical boundary as a law of biology.

The distinction between the abundance of a family and the fraction of all DNA contributed by that family is particularly important. A hundred copies of a 10-kb sequence occupy more DNA than ten thousand copies of a 5-bp sequence. The first family has a smaller copy number but contributes twenty times as much physical DNA: 1,000,000 bp versus 50,000 bp. A biochemical measurement that records the proportion of nucleotides will report the first contribution as larger even though the second family is encountered more frequently when counting repeat units.

For a perfect repeat family with copy number n and unit length L, its total length is nL. If the haploid genome size is G, its fraction is nL/G. Multiply by 100 to express a percentage. This accounting assumes the repeat units are nonoverlapping, all have length L, and that the full units are counted. If copies have deletions, their actual lengths must instead be added. If a family occurs within another repeat, counting both independently can double-count bases. Genomic complexity is also an approximation when imperfectly matching copies are grouped together: the more divergence the criterion allows, the smaller the estimated number of distinct families.

Worked example 1. A 120-Mb haploid genome contains 15,000 intact 400-bp copies of family X and 1,000 copies of a 3-kb family Y. X occupies 15,000 × 400 = 6,000,000 bp = 6 Mb, or 5% of the genome. Y occupies 1,000 × 3,000 = 3 Mb, or 2.5%. Together they occupy 7.5% if their intervals do not overlap. The copy-number ratio is 15:1, but the DNA mass ratio is only 2:1. A larger number of repeats cannot be translated directly into a corresponding increase in DNA mass.

Sequence complexity and the C-value. The C-value is the amount of DNA in an unreplicated haploid chromosome set. A genome can gain DNA if an existing region is copied, even when no new kind of sequence is introduced. Imagine genome A contains 90 Mb of unique DNA and a 1-Mb block repeated ten times; genome B contains the same 90 Mb plus the same 1-Mb block repeated 110 times. The genome sizes are 100 and 200 Mb respectively, but their idealized distinct sequence complexity is 91 Mb in both cases. In reality, mutation among copies gradually makes their distinct information more complex than this perfect-copy model. It is therefore important to specify whether complexity means the amount of distinct sequence at a chosen resolution, the diversity of expressed products, or organismal biological complexity. None is a simple replacement for the others.

There is a useful way to distinguish the number of repeat families from the total number of repeat units. If a chromosome has 500 copies of family A and 500 copies of family B, it has 1,000 repeat units but only two families in this simplified description. Under suitable conditions each family can pair with its own complementary members, but a family A fragment cannot ordinarily form a stable full duplex with family B. The total repetitive fraction might be the same as for a chromosome with 1,000 copies of A alone, yet the distribution of matching partners differs. Reassociation measures that distribution; it does not merely count how many nucleotides are classified as repetitive.

3. Tandem, dispersed and inverted organization

Abundance is the first axis of classification. The second is physical arrangement: where are the copies relative to one another? In a tandem array, repeat units lie next to each other in succession, like a row of matching tiles. The sequence written as (CA)8 represents CACACACACACACACA on one strand. Its unit is CA, its copy number in that array is eight, and its array length is sixteen bp. The second strand has the complementary antiparallel sequence and is not counted as a separate repeat array. The same array read from an adjacent starting base can be described with a rotated motif, AC; a change of notation is not necessarily a new biological repeat.

In dispersed or interspersed repetitive DNA, homologous copies are separated by other stretches of DNA. They can occur at distant sites on one chromosome or on different chromosomes. A dispersed family can be extremely abundant even though none of its individual copies lies next to another family member. Conversely, a tandem array of three units at one locus is not necessarily a highly repetitive component of a genome. Many genomes contain both a dispersed set of tandem-repeat loci and a dispersed set of individual long repeats. These descriptions are compatible: the arrangement is tandem at each short array but dispersed at the scale of the chromosome.

A direct repeat has the same sequence orientation as another copy on a chosen reference strand. An inverted repeat has the opposite orientation, meaning the sequence at one site corresponds to the reverse complement of the other. The distinction matters because inverted repeats can, under appropriate strand-separation and folding conditions, produce paired stems within one strand; direct repeats instead offer similar sequences that can misalign when two DNA molecules pair. Neither orientation alone determines copy number. Inverted repeats and foldback behavior will become relevant when interpreting the fastest component of a reassociation experiment.

Repetition can also be hierarchical. Consider an array made of the alternating units A and B: ABABABAB. At the smaller scale it contains four A and four B units. At a larger scale, AB is repeated four times. If A and B are each 100 bp long, the region contains 800 bp whether it is described as eight 100-bp units or four 200-bp units. The chosen repeat level should reflect the question being asked. Higher-order repeat units made from multiple related monomers occur in large chromosome-associated arrays, a useful real biological example of this arithmetic.

Sequence maps provide an important form of evidence. A probe or short sequence that matches many sites indicates a repeated family under the chosen similarity conditions. If the sites are adjacent, a tandem model is supported; if widely separated, a dispersed arrangement is supported. An abundance measurement alone cannot make this distinction. Similarly, an array of imperfect copies may still have an unmistakable tandem organization even if its monomers are not identical. A repeat class is therefore defined by several properties together rather than a single visual feature.

4. Satellite DNA: discovery, arrays and density

Before complete genome sequences were available, investigators could nevertheless separate DNA molecules according to their physical properties. In equilibrium centrifugation through a cesium chloride density gradient, a DNA fragment migrates until the surrounding solution has the same buoyant density as the DNA. For ordinary duplex DNA, its mean base composition is an important determinant of this density; under comparable conditions, G+C-rich DNA has a higher buoyant density than A+T-rich DNA. The bulk DNA of a genome typically gives a major band, representing a distribution of fragments around a characteristic density.

Early studies of mammalian DNA revealed smaller bands separated from the main band. Because they appeared alongside the major DNA band, they became known as satellite bands. A band was not initially evidence of repetition by itself: any sufficiently abundant compositionally unusual DNA could form a separate band. What made satellite DNA biologically significant was the combination of composition measurements, its rapid reassociation behavior and later direct examination of its sequence organization. The early mouse satellite studies and subsequent reassociation work established that some portions of the genome contained very large arrays of related sequence units.

The density separation requires fragments large enough to preserve the relevant compositional difference and a sufficient amount of material to detect a distinct peak. If satellite DNA has a composition similar to bulk DNA, its band can overlap the main peak. In a genome with several families having different compositions, more than one satellite band may appear. Thus modern satellite DNA is primarily a structural and sequence-based term for large tandem arrays, not a requirement that every family produce a separate centrifugation band. Nor should the term be confused with the small chromosomal satellite separated by a secondary constriction in classical cytogenetics: that is a visible chromosome feature, not a definition of the molecular repeat class.

Large satellite arrays frequently contain hundreds of thousands of base pairs or more, although lengths and sequence units differ enormously among species and chromosome regions. The repeat monomer may be short or relatively long, and higher-order units can be formed from several related monomers. These arrays are especially prominent in some centromeric and pericentromeric regions, where chromosomes require large organized chromatin domains. Satellite DNA is often not translated into protein; however, absence of a protein-coding region does not imply absence of structural significance or transcription. Some satellite transcripts are biologically relevant, and the functional importance of an individual array must be established with evidence rather than inferred from its label.

A practical lesson from satellite discovery is that molecular properties report different aspects of DNA. The position of a density peak primarily reflects average composition; its area reflects the mass of DNA in that fraction if detection is comparable; the rate of reassociation reflects effective sequence abundance and complementarity. It is the convergence of these observations, rather than any one measurement, that permits a conclusion about abundant repeated sequences.

A common misconception is that a satellite must be AT-rich. Composition is family-specific. In a density gradient, a lower-density peak is consistent with relatively AT-rich DNA, and a higher-density satellite can be relatively GC-rich. Neither position specifies the number of copies. To separate this issue experimentally, imagine two DNA fractions with the same average GC content but markedly different half-Cot values. Their density positions may coincide even when one has many more compatible partners. In contrast, two unrelated single-copy regions could have different mean compositions and hence different buoyant densities without either being a tandem repeat. The independence of the two measurements is why the classical evidence is stronger when density fractionation and reassociation are interpreted together.

5. Minisatellites and microsatellites

Not every tandem array is an enormous satellite block. Many tandem repeats occupy comparatively short regions surrounded by other sequences. Two common names for such regions are minisatellite and microsatellite. A frequently used modern convention defines microsatellite repeat motifs as one to six bp and minisatellite motifs as longer units, commonly around ten to sixty bp. Older textbooks and different analysis systems use somewhat different boundaries. These terms emphasize unit length and array organization; a microsatellite is not automatically a rare sequence, and a minisatellite is not simply a satellite of a smaller copy number.

A microsatellite is also called a short tandem repeat (STR) or simple sequence repeat (SSR). Examples include (A)12, (CA)15 and (GATA)8. The last example contains eight tetranucleotide units and occupies 32 bp. Different individuals can have different numbers of repeat units at the same genomic locus. When such alternative repeat counts are inherited, they are alleles of that locus. Thus one genomic locus can be highly variable between individuals while containing a repetitive motif within each individual's genome.

A minisatellite is often described as a variable-number tandem repeat (VNTR). The term VNTR is also used more generally for tandem arrays with polymorphic copy number, so some contexts include STRs under that umbrella. A 25-bp unit repeated 40 times forms a 1-kb minisatellite array. If another allele has 52 units of the same motif, its array is 1.3 kb. The length difference is 300 bp. Flanking sequences, meaning the sequences on either side of the repeated region, can provide unique positional information: they let an investigator distinguish this particular locus from other arrays that happen to contain a similar motif.

Why do repeat counts change? Consider DNA replication at a perfect short tandem array. The newly synthesized strand and its template contain several adjacent sequence registers that are similar enough to pair. If the strands temporarily dissociate and then pair again at the wrong register, a loop containing one or more repeat units can form. When extra bases loop out on the newly synthesized strand, the daughter strand may contain additional units; when bases loop out on the template and are skipped during synthesis, the daughter strand may contain fewer units. Such replication slippage is a major source of microsatellite length variation. The eventual result depends on subsequent processing: mismatch repair can correct many loop errors, and an unrepaired change must be retained through further replication to become a stable mutation.

Arrays with longer units can also change by unequal recombination. During pairing of homologous regions, similar units may align out of register. A crossover between the misaligned copies can give one product an enlarged array and another a shortened array. Minisatellite instability can involve recombination-associated processes, including gene conversion, in which sequence information is transferred nonreciprocally. The exact relative importance of these mechanisms varies by locus. Both slippage and unequal exchange use the same underlying feature: several similar positions in the array provide alternative alignments.

Because some repeat lengths vary among individuals, microsatellites and minisatellites have been useful as genetic markers. The positional information comes from the flanks, while the polymorphism comes from the variable array. A single observed allele length is generally not sufficient to identify an individual or infer ancestry conclusively, because different individuals can share lengths and different sequences can have identical overall lengths. The principles of fingerprinting and population analysis are considered in their respective methods and inheritance units.

Repeat variability can be neutral or functionally important depending on the locus. For example, expansion of a trinucleotide repeat within a coding region can lengthen the encoded amino-acid tract without necessarily changing the reading frame. Expanded CAG in the coding region of the HTT gene provides a familiar example of a disease-associated polyglutamine tract. In a noncoding region, a repeat expansion can instead influence transcription or RNA behavior. An array is not harmful simply because it contains many units: the sequence, location, expression, length and cellular context determine its consequences.

Worked example 2. A locus has 184 bp of combined unique flanking DNA and a perfect four-base repeat. Alleles with total lengths 216 and 244 bp therefore contain (216 − 184)/4 = 8 and (244 − 184)/4 = 15 units. Their difference is seven units, or 28 bp. These are two alleles of one locus in a diploid individual, not automatically two distinct loci or two gene-family members. A 245-bp allele would not fit the perfect tetranucleotide-only model because 61 is not divisible by four; an interruption, indel in the flank or measurement issue would need consideration.

6. Interspersed DNA and the distinction from gene families

Many repetitive sequences in eukaryotic genomes are not concentrated in a single array. Long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) are important examples in mammals. A repeated sequence family may originate from copying a mobile element into new positions over evolutionary time. The human Alu family is a familiar SINE family, with individual units typically near 300 bp when intact. Some long LINE copies are several kb in length, although many genomic copies are truncated, mutated or no longer capable of movement. It follows that the copy number of a family is not the same as the number of currently active elements.

The word dispersed does not imply perfectly random spacing. Some insertion sites are favored, some genomic regions tolerate insertions better than others, and copies may be lost in different regions at different rates. Many old copies retain enough similarity to be recognized as a family but are no longer identical. Their reassociation depends on the similarity that remains and the hybridization stringency. A highly abundant interspersed family may contribute to a rapid kinetic fraction without being a satellite, and more divergent copies of the same evolutionary family may behave as separate effective kinetic subfamilies.

It is useful to separate gene families from repeat families. A gene family consists of related genes, usually understood through their evolutionary relationship and sequence similarity; its members can encode related products and may have acquired specialized functions. A repetitive DNA family is defined more broadly by repeated recognizable sequence. An array of a noncoding motif may be an extensive repeat family without containing repeated functional genes. Conversely, a cluster of related globin genes constitutes a gene family even if its copy number is too small to be resolved as a fast reassociating DNA fraction.

The boundaries can overlap. A tandem ribosomal DNA array contains numerous related transcription units and is also repetitive DNA. A gene family could be arranged in tandem or dispersed among chromosomes. A gene may have only a single-copy exon while containing a repeated motif in an intron; the whole locus is not usefully described by a single label if its different regions are being studied. Copy number, ancestry, genomic arrangement and function are four questions, each requiring its own evidence. The next chapter examines the evolutionary fate of duplicated genes; the later chapter on mobile elements explains how interspersed copies arise in more detail.

7. Repeated genes: ribosomal DNA and histone genes

Ribosomes are abundant molecular machines required for protein synthesis. Their RNA components must be made in considerable quantities. In many eukaryotes, the DNA that encodes the major ribosomal RNAs occurs in tandem arrays. In mammals, one transcription unit gives rise to a large precursor from which the 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs are processed. Adjacent units are separated by intergenic spacers; the repeated genomic unit includes the transcribed region and spacer, not merely the mature RNA sequences. The 5S rRNA genes are generally separate from these major arrays in mammals and are transcribed by a different RNA polymerase. Other organisms organize their rDNA differently, so the mammalian example must not be treated as a universal gene map.

Having many units allows transcription from multiple copies, which can increase the capacity for rRNA production. It also creates an organizational problem: the cell must coordinate the activity of copies, maintain the repeated arrays and prevent uncontrolled rearrangement. Not all copies need be active at a given time. The number of active transcription units depends on chromatin state and cellular demand, rather than total repeat count alone. Many rDNA arrays are associated with nucleolar organizer regions, which contribute to nucleolus formation around actively transcribed ribosomal genes. The nucleus chapter explains the relationship of the nucleolus to ribosome production.

Histone proteins provide another reason for repeated genes. When a eukaryotic cell duplicates its DNA, it must package the new DNA in chromatin. High demand for canonical histone proteins during DNA synthesis is met partly through coordinated expression of multiple histone genes. The genomic arrangement varies among organisms. In Drosophila, repeated histone gene clusters are an especially clear example: a repeat unit contains genes for several histones. In humans, canonical histone genes occur in clusters but are not all arranged as a single simple perfect tandem array. Histone variants can also be encoded at separate loci. Consequently the phrase histone repeat array should be applied to an actual gene organization rather than treated as a universal arrangement for every histone gene.

Repeated functional arrays demonstrate that increased DNA content can support biological output. But a greater number of DNA units is a potential capacity, not a guarantee of proportionally greater RNA or protein production. If transcription factors, available polymerase, processing steps or active chromatin are limiting, doubling the genomic copy number need not double the output. This distinction is useful in experimental interpretation: a DNA copy-number assay and an RNA expression assay answer different questions.

Worked example 3. A modeled rDNA array contains 150 repeat units of 40 kb. If 13 kb of each unit is transcribed into a precursor, the array occupies 6 Mb and the transcribed portions together occupy 1.95 Mb. The remaining 4.05 Mb consists of intergenic spacer DNA in this model. If 60 units are active, 40% of the units are active; this is not 40% of all nuclear DNA or necessarily 40% of maximal possible transcription. If each active unit produces precursor at the same rate, the total production is proportional to 60, not 150. The numbers are illustrative rather than measurements from a particular species.

8. Centromeric and telomeric repeats

A linear eukaryotic chromosome has regions with special structural responsibilities. Its centromere is the chromosomal region at which the kinetochore assembles. The kinetochore connects the chromosome to spindle microtubules during division. Many multicellular eukaryotes possess extensive tandem repeats in and around centromeres, including repeat-rich pericentromeric heterochromatin. In humans, native centromeres are commonly associated with alpha-satellite DNA, built from related monomers of approximately 171 bp. Certain groups of monomers form higher-order units that are themselves repeated many times. The composition of these arrays can differ among chromosomes even when the monomer family is shared.

How does a repeated DNA array contribute to chromosome organization? It provides a large physical region on which chromatin can assemble. Repeated motifs may support repeated binding sites and characteristic chromatin organization, but DNA sequence alone does not specify every functional centromere. A specialized centromeric histone H3 variant, CENP-A, is important for defining centromeric chromatin in humans. Its presence helps distinguish the active centromeric domain from surrounding repeat-rich DNA. Many copies in a large satellite array do not themselves carry an active kinetochore. Moreover, functional neocentromeres have been documented at human chromosome regions that lack the characteristic alpha-satellite array. This is direct evidence against treating the repeat sequence as an absolutely necessary or sufficient centromere determinant in humans. Centromeres of different organisms vary widely, and some use short defined sequences rather than giant satellite arrays.

Telomeres solve a different problem. They protect the natural ends of linear chromosomes from inappropriate recognition as broken DNA ends and contribute to the maintenance of those ends during cell proliferation. Human and other vertebrate telomeres contain tandem copies of the motif 5′-TTAGGG-3′ on the G-rich strand oriented toward the chromosome terminus. The complementary strand reads 3′-AATCCC-5′ when displayed opposite it in the same left-to-right direction. The duplex usually ends with a G-rich 3′ overhang. Repeated sequence motifs provide binding opportunities for telomere-associated proteins; in vertebrates the shelterin complex contributes to end protection. Telomeric length and associated chromatin influence this protective structure.

Linear end replication and nucleolytic processing can change telomere length. Telomerase, where active, uses its internal RNA template to add repeated DNA to the G-rich 3′ end. The detailed catalytic mechanism is treated with replication in Unit 3. Not all eukaryotic telomeres have the vertebrate repeat sequence; plants, fungi and other organisms can use different motifs, and Drosophila maintains chromosome ends largely using specialized retrotransposons rather than the standard telomerase-generated short-repeat arrays. These exceptions demonstrate that the biological objective is stable chromosome-end maintenance, not universal dependence on one particular six-base sequence.

Centromeric and telomeric repeats are often associated with chromatin states distinct from many gene-rich regions. Nevertheless, the two structures are not interchangeable. A telomeric array at an internal chromosome position is not necessarily a functional end, just as centromeric satellite DNA outside an active centromere does not by itself establish a kinetochore. Position, protein association and chromosome function matter. In the later chromosome architecture chapter these differences are connected to segregation and physical chromosome morphology.

Worked example 4. A perfectly homogeneous alpha-satellite region measuring 1.71 Mb contains 1,710,000/171 = 10,000 monomers. If twelve monomers constitute one higher-order unit, the array can contain 833 complete higher-order units and four remaining monomers, since 833 × 12 = 9,996. In a real centromere, sequence interruptions and variable higher-order composition make such exact arithmetic an approximation. Separately, a modeled 9-kb telomeric tract of perfect TTAGGG repeats contains 9,000/6 = 1,500 units. It would be incorrect to conclude that 1,500 protein complexes must bind, since the stoichiometry and accessibility of DNA binding sites are separate questions.

9. How repetition expands, contracts and persists

Repeated DNA creates opportunities for further repetition. In a tandem array, sequence similarity permits out-of-register pairing. A recombination event between misaligned homologous molecules can redistribute the number of units. Imagine two parental arrays with four units each, aligned with a displacement of one unit. If a reciprocal exchange occurs at equivalent-looking but non-equivalent genomic positions inside the array, one product may end with five units and the other with three. The total number of units across the two products remains eight in this simplified reciprocal case, but their distribution between individual DNA molecules changes. This mechanism is distinct from replication slippage, in which a new strand can acquire or lose bases during copying.

For larger genomic segments, duplication may generate extra copies of genes or intergenic sequences. Some copies retain similar function; others accumulate substitutions, lose functionality or become specialized. Gene conversion can homogenize repeat copies by transferring sequence information from one to another, even without changing copy number. These processes help explain why copies within some tandem arrays remain similar to each other even while the array differs between populations or species. The collective sequence behavior of a family can appear more uniform than would be expected if each copy evolved in complete isolation. This phenomenon is commonly described as concerted evolution, especially for repeated genes such as rDNA.

For dispersed elements, propagation can add copies at new sites rather than merely lengthening an existing array. Over generations, mutation and deletion reduce similarity or remove copies, while new insertion and duplication add sequence. The net result is a history of expansion and contraction. Large differences in repeat content help explain why genome sizes can differ dramatically among related species without requiring similar differences in the number of genes. This is part of the broader C-value enigma: DNA amount in the haploid genome is not a reliable measure of organismal complexity.

It is misleading to assume that all repeated DNA was deliberately produced to carry out a beneficial function. Evolution can retain sequences for multiple reasons. Some arrays are important for cellular structure or gene output; other copies may persist because their effects are weak or because their proliferation and loss reach a dynamic balance. An array may even have a beneficial function at one scale but produce instability when it expands or recombines inappropriately. The appropriate experimental approach is to distinguish the origin of a sequence, its current abundance, and the consequence of altering it. A sequence's evolutionary origin does not settle its present-day function.

Repeated DNA is also a challenge for maintaining accurate sequence information. If a broken chromosome region pairs with a homologous sequence at an inappropriate location, repair or recombination may alter genomic organization. The probability depends on repeat similarity, length, arrangement, distance and repair context. In a simplified case, recombination between direct repeats on the same DNA molecule can remove the intervening segment; recombination between oppositely oriented repeats can invert the intervening segment. These are structural outcomes of pairing geometry, not universal outcomes of every encounter. Detailed recombination mechanisms belong to Unit 3 and chromosome rearrangements to inheritance biology.

10. The discovery that not all DNA reassociates at the same rate

How could scientists estimate repetition before reading the sequence of entire chromosomes? The key insight is that complementary single strands of DNA can find each other in solution. As explained in the nucleic acid stability chapter, heating duplex DNA disrupts base pairing and separates the strands without normally breaking the covalent backbone. When conditions again favor duplex formation, single strands collide, and sufficiently complementary sequences can form a small stable paired region, called a nucleus. Pairing can then extend along the complementary region, a process sometimes described as zippering.

The slow step for many reactions is the encounter and successful nucleation of complementary strands. Imagine that a particular fragment is represented once in a genome. In a mixture of fragments obtained from many identical genomes, its complementary sequence is present, but it is only a tiny part of the total DNA. If another fragment belongs to a family repeated 10,000 times per genome, a matching strand from any sufficiently similar copy may be a productive partner. At the same total DNA concentration, there are far more suitable partners for a repeated sequence. Its chance of productive collision per unit time is consequently higher.

To investigate the composition of a genome, investigators can purify DNA and fragment it into an approximately comparable size range. Fragmentation removes the special circumstance in which a single very long chromosome molecule might contain both a unique region and an enormous repeat array. The fragments are denatured and then allowed to reassociate under controlled salt and temperature conditions. The amount of double-stranded DNA is measured at successive reaction extents. Classical methods exploited the different behavior of single- and double-stranded DNA on hydroxyapatite or differences in absorbance caused by base stacking. These measurements report paired DNA, not the exact sequence or chromosomal position of each fragment.

In their landmark 1968 study, Roy Britten and David Kohne described repeated DNA sequences on the basis of reassociation kinetics. The contrast between relatively simple DNA populations and the multiple kinetic components of many eukaryotic DNA samples showed that physical genome length could not be equated with one vast collection of single-copy sequences. A rapidly pairing component was consistent with abundant complementary sequence families. A slower component indicated sequences with fewer suitable partners. Their work provided a way to infer effective sequence complexity from solution kinetics, opening an important window into genome structure before modern sequencing.

Several controls are needed for a convincing interpretation. A sample of known simpler sequence organization serves as a reference for reassociation under the same conditions. Equal DNA fragment lengths reduce differences caused by encounter and nucleation properties. Matching salt concentration and temperature keeps duplex stability comparable. A fully denatured sample establishes the initial single-stranded baseline, and a well-paired control helps calibrate the duplex signal. The DNA should be sufficiently purified so that contaminants do not distort the absorbance or separation. Without these controls, an apparent fast component might reflect a change in reaction conditions or starting structure rather than genomic abundance.

Rapid pairing is therefore evidence of high effective partner concentration under defined conditions. It is not automatically proof of tandem organization. A dispersed family contributes many compatible partners as readily as a tandem family once the DNA has been fragmented and thoroughly mixed. To locate repeats, one needs additional information from hybridization to chromosomes or direct sequence maps.

11. The meaning of C₀t and a minimal kinetic model

Reassociation is concentration dependent. If two tubes contain the same fragmented genome at different concentrations, the more concentrated tube generally reaches a given fraction of reassociation in less time. The relevant measure combines initial concentration and elapsed time. The notation C0t (often written Cot) means initial concentration C0 multiplied by time t. The subscript is zero, not the letter O. A conventional concentration unit is mol of nucleotides per litre, and time is usually measured in seconds, giving units mol nucleotide L−1 s. It is essential to use the concentration convention supplied by the problem; a value expressed in mol of strands rather than nucleotides is not automatically interchangeable.

The underlying second-order law can be understood from a simplified population of complementary strands. Let C be the concentration of remaining single-stranded DNA, using the same concentration convention as C0. Assume equal concentrations of both complementary strand types, uniform effective fragment length, a well-mixed solution and a constant effective association coefficient k. A productive collision requires a strand of each type, so its probability is proportional to the product of their concentrations. Since both decline together, the rate of loss of single-stranded DNA is proportional to C². Factors arising from whether one counts individual strand types or total nucleotide concentration are absorbed into the effective k.

In that convention, −dC/dt = kC². Separating variables gives dC/C² = −k dt. Integrating from C0 at t = 0 to C at time t yields −1/C + 1/C0 = −kt. Rearranging gives 1/C = 1/C0 + kt. Multiplying by C0 and taking the reciprocal yields the single-stranded fraction S = C/C0 = 1/(1 + kC0t). The reassociated fraction R is the complement: R = 1 − S = kC0t/(1 + kC0t). If C is mol L−1, k has units L mol−1 s−1; kC0t is dimensionless.

The half-reassociation value C0t1/2 is the product at which half of the component has reassociated. Substitute S = 1/2: 1 + kC0t1/2 = 2, hence C0t1/2 = 1/k. A large effective association coefficient gives a small half-Cot; a small coefficient gives a large half-Cot. The corresponding elapsed half-time is t1/2 = 1/(kC0), so unlike a first-order decay process it depends on the starting concentration. The model predicts a smooth curve on an ordinary time axis. When the horizontal axis is logarithmic, equal multiplicative increases in Cot occupy equal distances, making it easier to display both fast and slow components.

Worked example 5. A homogeneous DNA component has k = 25 L mol−1 s−1. With C0 = 0.002 mol L−1, t1/2 = 1/(25 × 0.002) = 20 s. Its half-Cot is 0.002 × 20 = 0.04 mol L−1 s. Doubling concentration to 0.004 mol L−1 halves the required time to 10 s but preserves the half-Cot. At Cot 0.12, kCot = 3, so S = 1/4 = 25% and R = 75%. The calculation assumes the same effective association coefficient under both conditions.

For a perfectly homogeneous sequence family repeated n times in a given genome, the concentration of its compatible strands is approximately n times that for an equally long single-copy sequence under otherwise identical conditions. When the horizontal axis uses the total genomic initial nucleotide concentration, this increased partner concentration shifts the observed kinetic component toward lower Cot. In a simplified comparison with matched fragment length and similarity, a family repeated 1,000 times may have an effective half-Cot about 1,000-fold smaller than the single-copy reference. This proportionality is approximate. It assumes all family members are sufficiently homologous to participate and that their collision and nucleation properties are comparable. A half-Cot quoted using the concentration of a purified family instead of total DNA requires a different concentration normalization.

Worked example 6. In a model genome, a matched single-copy reference has half-Cot 200 mol L−1 s. Two families have half-Cot 2 and 0.002 in the same units and conditions. Their estimated effective repetition factors are 200/2 = 100 and 200/0.002 = 100,000. These are estimates of compatible partners under the experimental assumptions. They are not automatically exact counts of physical loci, particularly when copies are divergent or fragmented.

Why the genome-size denominator matters. In a conceptual sample of equal-length fragments, the concentration of a particular complementary sequence is proportional to its number of genomic occurrences divided by the total amount of DNA per haploid genome. If genome size changes from G to 2G but a particular single-copy locus remains present only once per haploid set, the concentration of copies of that locus at a fixed total nucleotide concentration is approximately halved. Under matched association conditions its half-Cot therefore tends to increase approximately twofold. A family whose copy number simultaneously doubles can instead retain approximately the same compatible-strand concentration per unit total DNA. This is why a two-species comparison cannot identify copy-number ratios from half-Cot ratios alone without accounting for the sizes and compositions of the genomes.

The second-order description also explains why one should not calculate the time for 90% reassociation by multiplying the half-time by the number of half-lives in a first-order decay. Starting with the same homogeneous component, solve R = x/(x+h), where h is the half-Cot and x is Cot. Rearranging gives x = hR/(1−R). Thus for 90% pairing x = 9h; for 99% pairing x = 99h. The remaining complementary strands are progressively rarer, so further pairing becomes slower as the reaction proceeds. These values assume a constant effective coefficient, no inaccessible material and a perfectly independent component.

Worked example 8. For a homogeneous component with half-Cot 0.5 mol L−1 s, 90% reassociation requires Cot = 9 × 0.5 = 4.5 mol L−1 s. At initial DNA concentration 0.005 mol L−1, this requires 900 s, or 15 min. Half-reassociation requires 0.5/0.005 = 100 s. The ninefold time ratio follows directly from the second-order model and is not the approximately 3.32-fold ratio for a first-order exponential process.

12. Reading a multicomponent C₀t curve

A real genomic sample is a mixture, so one second-order coefficient generally cannot describe the entire reaction. Suppose its DNA can be approximated by three nonoverlapping classes with initial nucleotide fractions fH, fM and fU, representing high-copy, moderate-copy and unique components. Their fractions sum to one. In a simple independent-component model, define hi as the effective half-Cot of component i when the x-axis uses total initial genomic DNA concentration. For each component, the single-stranded fraction remaining within that component is 1/(1 + x/hi), where x = C0t. The total single-stranded fraction is Stotal = Σ fi/(1 + x/hi). The total reassociated fraction is 1 − Stotal. This expression is a teaching model for independent sequence populations; genuine genomic fragments may contain mixed sequence classes and may cross-hybridize.

The fastest component contributes the first rise on a plot of reassociated fraction versus log Cot. Once it has largely paired, the curve approaches a shoulder before the next class begins to dominate. The moderately repetitive component produces the next rise. The slowest part, mostly unique and low-copy DNA, appears at the largest Cot. A graph drawn as fraction remaining single-stranded instead falls over these same regions. Always inspect the vertical axis before assigning directions. In either convention the high-copy component is leftmost when Cot increases from left to right.

A horizontal location tells us about rate, not abundance. The amount of DNA assigned to a kinetic component is represented by the vertical contribution of that component, ideally the difference between adjacent plateaus. If a curve rises from 0% to 20%, then rises to 55%, and finally reaches 100%, the approximate contributions are 20%, 35% and 45% respectively, provided the transitions are well separated. It is not valid to assign the second class 55% because that is the cumulative amount paired after the first two classes have largely reassociated. Nor is the apparent midpoint of the whole curve necessarily the half-Cot of the unique component.

Worked example 7. For the model plotted above, at Cot = 0.3 the high-copy component has paired to extent 0.3/0.301 ≈ 0.99668, the moderate component to 0.3/0.6 = 0.5, and the unique component to 0.3/100.3 ≈ 0.002991. Their weighted contributions are 0.19934, 0.175 and 0.001346. The total is 0.37568, or about 37.57%. The entire genome is not 50% reassociated simply because the moderate class has reached its own half-Cot. If the graph were plotted as single-stranded DNA, the same sample would have a value of about 62.43%.

Not every curve shows exactly three neatly separated stages. A continuum of copy numbers can produce a broad transition. Families with similar effective half-Cot values may overlap, and a very small mass fraction may be hard to resolve even if its copy number is high. A very early component can include foldback sequences: after denaturation, complementary portions of the same strand can fold together by intramolecular pairing, which does not require collision with another molecule. Such structures can form rapidly even in dilute solution. They must be distinguished from ordinary highly repetitive intermolecular reassociation, especially when the earliest experimental points are interpreted.

Comparison of two species also requires care. For homogeneous single-copy genomes with comparable sequence properties, a larger genome has a smaller concentration of each unique sequence at the same total DNA concentration, so it tends to require a larger Cot for reassociation. For genomes of the same physical size but different repetitive composition, the genome with a large highly repeated fraction often shows a larger early rise. Its late component may nevertheless have a different location depending on the amount of distinct sequence. This is how kinetic data reveal effective sequence complexity rather than merely genome mass.

Many questions plot the percentage of DNA that remains single-stranded instead of paired DNA. Suppose the remaining fraction is initially 100%, then levels near 82%, then near 37%, before approaching zero. The high-copy fraction is approximately 100 − 82 = 18%, the middle fraction is approximately 82 − 37 = 45%, and the slow fraction is approximately 37 − 0 = 37%. The graph is falling but the classification is unchanged: high-copy DNA still pairs first. If the horizontal axis is logarithmic, a separation of three decades in component half-Cot corresponds to a factor of 1,000, not three. The actual component half-Cot should be estimated halfway through its own vertical contribution. For the middle step in this example, halfway is (82 + 37)/2 = 59.5% remaining, not 50% remaining.

In a well-resolved three-component model, such halfway estimates are informative. If the transitions substantially overlap, however, locating a visual midpoint between two shoulders is unreliable. One must account for the contribution of all components at that x-coordinate. A curve that never reaches complete pairing can also represent sample damage, highly divergent sequences, inappropriate reaction conditions or insufficient reaction time; the missing fraction should not automatically be called unique DNA. A low-copy component could remain mostly unpaired in a short experiment yet become detectable when the Cot range is extended.

13. Interpreting evidence without overclaiming

Reassociation assays rely on the ability of sufficiently complementary molecules to form a stable paired structure under the experimental conditions. A repeat family may have many copies that have accumulated mismatches. At relatively stringent conditions, only the more closely matching copies will form stable duplexes, effectively reducing the partner concentration. At less stringent conditions, more divergent copies may contribute to the observed pairing. A shifted curve can therefore reflect sequence divergence as well as copy number. Changing temperature or salt concentration is not equivalent to changing the underlying genome; it alters the measurement's ability to detect related sequences.

Fragment size affects reassociation because long and short fragments do not behave identically. A fragment containing a repeat adjacent to unique DNA may start pairing through the repeated part while leaving a nonpaired tail. A method that detects a fragment as soon as some region becomes duplex can produce a different apparent fraction from one that measures the paired nucleotide mass more directly. This is why a simple multicomponent curve is best treated as an idealized description. It can be a powerful tool for examining broad abundance classes but cannot by itself give a precise catalogue of every repeated locus.

Position can be investigated independently with hybridization. A labeled probe complementary to a repeat motif may reveal a large concentrated signal at one chromosome region or multiple signals at different sites. A centromeric concentration supports the idea of a local repeat-rich domain. Widespread signals support dispersed occurrences, although dispersed clusters of tandem repeats remain possible. Signal intensity is influenced by probe accessibility, local copy number and hybridization conditions. A strong signal is not an exact copy count unless calibrated, and lack of a visible signal does not prove absence of all related sequences. Appropriate negative and positive probe controls help distinguish nonspecific binding from true matches.

Direct sequence analysis allows one to examine both the repeat motif and its neighboring sequences. Repeated DNA is challenging to reconstruct from short sequence fragments because many fragments fit several possible genomic positions. If a repeated region is longer than the available sequence reads and contains few informative differences, the order and number of copies may be ambiguous. Unique flanks and longer reads can help distinguish copies or traverse an array. The concept explains why genome assembly may underestimate repeat-rich regions. Detailed sequencing methods belong to the genomics and methods units; the essential point here is that different measurements answer different organizational questions.

Consider a study in which a low-density DNA fraction reassociates rapidly, a matching probe localizes largely near centromeres, and sequence analysis detects long arrays of related monomers. The three observations collectively support a satellite interpretation: unusual composition, abundance and tandem organization. If only the first two findings were available, repeat organization would remain uncertain. Conversely, a fast reassociating population with a widely scattered hybridization signal and recognizable individual interspersed elements would fit a dispersed repeat family. This form of reasoning is central to experimental CSIR-NET questions: decide what the result directly measures and which additional evidence is necessary for a stronger conclusion.

Distinguishing hypotheses using matched observations. Consider a sample whose rapid reassociation signal becomes much smaller after a repeat array has been experimentally shortened. If the remaining fragments were tested at the same total concentration and fragment size, a decrease in the array’s mass fraction and its effective compatible-strand concentration are both plausible consequences. The predicted fast component may become smaller and shift toward higher Cot. But a less stable hybrid due to accumulated mismatches could also shift the fast component without comparable physical shortening. A sequence map or a size measurement across the flanking regions can distinguish these explanations. Likewise, if the signal changes after a treatment that affects chromatin while purified DNA sequence and abundance remain unchanged, the simplest reassociation experiment should not be taken as a direct assay of chromatin compaction: extraction has removed much of the original chromosomal context.

14. A unified framework for examination reasoning

The central distinction throughout the chapter is between sequence identity, sequence frequency and physical arrangement. A repeated motif within a single locus can produce alleles of different lengths; repeated genes may produce related transcripts or proteins; dispersed transposon-derived copies may be structurally recognizable without retaining mobility. None of these descriptions can substitute for the others. The same region can simultaneously be repetitive, tandem, transcribed and structurally significant. Conversely, a single-copy regulatory sequence can be functional without encoding any protein.

Three distinctions prevent most calculation mistakes. First, use bp when describing physical duplex length and do not double the count for its complementary strand. Second, use haploid copy number unless the question specifies a different denominator; a diploid G1 nucleus has two sets and a replicated diploid G2 nucleus has twice its G1 DNA content, without changing the basic haploid family classification. Third, use the relative contribution of a kinetic component to read mass fraction and the horizontal shift to estimate effective abundance. A small highly repetitive fraction may be leftmost yet contribute only a modest amount of genomic DNA.

When a student confronts an unfamiliar experimental result, it helps to identify the measured property first. A density gradient measures buoyant density; a sequence map measures positions and arrangements; an allele-length measurement reports a size difference; and a Cot curve measures reassociation behavior. A change in a measurement is not a universal indication of a change in gene number. The next chapter develops gene families and the fate of duplicates, while the later chromatin and chromosome chapters explain how particular repetitive and unique regions are packaged into functional chromosome domains.

15. Part B — Original conceptual and assertion–reason questions

1. A 600-bp sequence occurs at one locus on each homolog in a diploid G1 cell. What is its haploid classification?

  1. Highly repetitive
  2. Single-copy
  3. Tandemly duplicated
  4. Dispersed family
Answer and reasoning

B. The two homologous occurrences are expected from diploidy; each haploid set has one locus. A requires many genomic occurrences; C requires adjacent duplicate units; D requires multiple separated loci.

2. Which property defines a tandem repeat?

  1. Adjacent related units
  2. High GC content
  3. Centromeric localization
  4. Protein coding
Answer and reasoning

A. Adjacency is the defining feature. GC content varies; centromeric location is common for some families but not necessary; coding status is independent.

3. A (GATA)₁₂ array is how long?

  1. 12 bp
  2. 24 bp
  3. 48 bp
  4. 96 bp
Answer and reasoning

C. Four bases per unit times twelve = 48 bp. A ignores motif length, B assumes two bases, and D incorrectly counts both strands as separate bp.

4. Which observation is sufficient to conclude a fragment family is interspersed rather than tandem at the scale examined?

  1. Rapid reassociation
  2. An unusual density peak
  3. Matching copies separated by unrelated DNA on a sequence map
  4. High RNA abundance
Answer and reasoning

C. The sequence map directly reports separated positions. Reassociation reports compatible abundance, density reports composition, and expression does not locate genomic copies.

5. Which statement about microsatellites is best?

  1. They are always centromeric
  2. All are protein-coding
  3. Their short motifs occur as tandem arrays
  4. Their lengths never vary
Answer and reasoning

C. STRs are tandem arrays of short motifs, commonly 1–6 bp. They can occur at many loci, coding or noncoding, and often vary in length.

6. A mouse satellite-like DNA fraction produces no separate density band. What follows?

  1. It cannot be repetitive
  2. Its density may overlap bulk DNA
  3. It must be unique
  4. It must be telomeric
Answer and reasoning

B. Composition similar to the main fraction can mask a band. A and C confuse density with sequence frequency; D makes an unsupported positional claim.

7. Which feature is typical of mammalian major rDNA arrays?

  1. Each unit yields only 5S rRNA
  2. Repeated units contain precursor rRNA coding regions and spacers
  3. Every copy is necessarily active
  4. They encode only histones
Answer and reasoning

B. Major rDNA units contain precursor and spacer regions. 5S is generally separately organized in mammals, not every copy is active, and histone genes encode proteins.

8. Which is the most defensible interpretation of a human neocentromere without alpha satellite?

  1. Alpha satellite always prevents segregation
  2. CENP-A is absent from every centromere
  3. Alpha satellite is not absolutely required for human centromere function
  4. Telomeric TTAGGG defines all centromeres
Answer and reasoning

C. Functional neocentromeres show the native repeat is not strictly necessary. They do not demonstrate that it prevents segregation, that centromeric chromatin is absent, or that telomeric sequence determines centromeres.

9. Which is a valid statement about reassociation?

  1. At equal total DNA concentration, more compatible copies usually lower half-Cot
  2. Half-Cot is larger for all repetitive DNA
  3. A Cot curve gives physical chromosomal order
  4. Reassociation is normally independent of concentration
Answer and reasoning

A. More compatible partners increase productive collisions. B reverses the usual trend; C confuses solution kinetics with position; D contradicts the second-order model.

10. Assertion: A highly repetitive DNA family may contribute a small genome fraction. Reason: Mass contribution depends on unit length as well as copy number.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. Both statements are correct and the reason explains the assertion. A large n multiplied by a very small L may yield a small nL/G; the alternative truth assignments or lack of connection are incorrect.

16. Part C — Original quantitative and experimental reasoning questions

1. Genome X contains 50,000 nonoverlapping copies of a 240-bp family in a 600-Mb haploid genome. What fraction is occupied?

  1. 0.2%
  2. 2%
  3. 12%
  4. 20%
Answer and reasoning

B. 50,000 × 240 = 12,000,000 bp = 12 Mb. 12/600 × 100 = 2%. A is tenfold too small, C treats Mb as percentage, D is tenfold too high.

2. A diploid nucleus has alleles of 210 and 234 bp, with fixed flanks totaling 150 bp. The repeat motif is 3 bp. Which repeat counts are correct?

  1. 20 and 28
  2. 70 and 78
  3. 30 and 42
  4. 60 and 84
Answer and reasoning

A. Subtract the flanks first: 60 and 84 bp. Divide by three to obtain 20 and 28. B ignores the flanks, C uses an incorrect divisor, D reports base lengths rather than copy counts.

3. Two four-unit arrays undergo reciprocal unequal crossover after an offset of one unit. Which idealized product pair conserves the total and shows the expected inequality?

  1. 4 and 4
  2. 5 and 3
  3. 5 and 5
  4. 2 and 3
Answer and reasoning

B. The reciprocal unequal exchange redistributes one unit, yielding five and three; their sum is eight. A shows no inequality, C creates two extra units, D loses three units, inconsistent with the specified simplified reciprocal event.

4. A single homogeneous sample reaches 50% reassociation at 0.02 mol L⁻¹ and 8 s. At 0.01 mol L⁻¹, what time is predicted for 50% pairing?

  1. 4 s
  2. 8 s
  3. 16 s
  4. 32 s
Answer and reasoning

C. Half-Cot = 0.02 × 8 = 0.16 mol L⁻¹ s. At half the concentration, 0.16/0.01 = 16 s. A reverses the effect, B treats it as a concentration-independent first-order half-time, and D assumes a fourfold change.

5. A sample has three well-separated rises in reassociated fraction from 0 to 0.15, then to 0.62, then to 1.00. Which fractions are moderate and unique respectively?

  1. 62%, 100%
  2. 47%, 38%
  3. 15%, 62%
  4. 38%, 47%
Answer and reasoning

B. The middle rise contributes 0.62 − 0.15 = 0.47; the last rise contributes 1 − 0.62 = 0.38. A uses cumulative values; C confuses plateaus with contributions; D reverses the middle and late components.

6. Under identical fragment and stringency conditions the unique reference has half-Cot 120, and family R has half-Cot 0.012. What is the approximate effective copy factor?

  1. 100
  2. 1,000
  3. 10,000
  4. 100,000
Answer and reasoning

C. 120/0.012 = 10,000. The other choices are factors of ten or more away. This is an estimate of effective compatible copies, not a direct physical count.

7. A two-component genome has fractions 0.30 and 0.70 and half-Cot values 0.01 and 100. At Cot 0.01, what fraction of the total genome has reassociated approximately?

  1. 15.007%
  2. 50%
  3. 30%
  4. 70%
Answer and reasoning

A. Fast: 0.30 × 0.01/(0.01+0.01)=0.15. Slow: 0.70 × 0.01/100.01≈0.000070. Sum ≈0.15007 or 15.007%. B uses the fast component's own midpoint as the total; C assumes fast completion; D uses the slow initial fraction.

8. A repeat-rich DNA sample loses its fast reassociation component when tested under much more stringent conditions, with no DNA sequence alteration. Which is the best interpretation?

  1. All repeats were physically deleted
  2. Only sufficiently similar copies now pair stably
  3. The genome doubled in size
  4. Every repeated sequence became a coding gene
Answer and reasoning

B. Increased stringency can exclude divergent partners, reducing effective partner concentration or duplex stability. A and C posit unsupported genomic alterations, D changes functional classification without evidence.

9. An almost instantaneous pairing component persists even after strong dilution of the sample. Which explanation should be investigated?

  1. Intramolecular foldback pairing
  2. More diploid chromosomes in the tube
  3. A unique sequence must be more abundant than repeats
  4. Increased cell division
Answer and reasoning

A. Foldback uses complementary regions of the same strand and is less dependent on intermolecular concentration. B is not a mechanism of rapid pairing at fixed dilution; C reverses abundance logic; D cannot occur in purified DNA solution.

10. A repeat probe labels many widely separated chromosome regions. Which inference is justified?

  1. The family has multiple separated sites, but each site could still contain local tandem arrays
  2. Every labeled region is an active centromere
  3. Every site has identical repeat count
  4. Every copy is mobile
Answer and reasoning

A. Hybridization gives locations of detectable sequence similarity. It cannot establish functional centromeres, exact counts without calibration, or mobility. Thus B, C and D overstate the evidence.

11. An array is 2.052 Mb long and consists entirely of 171-bp monomers grouped into 12-monomer units. How many complete higher-order units?

  1. 12,000
  2. 1,000
  3. 171
  4. 144
Answer and reasoning

B. 2,052,000/171 = 12,000 monomers. 12,000/12 = 1,000 higher-order units. A counts monomers, C is monomer length, and D is unrelated to the array length.

12. A 300-unit rDNA array has half its units active. The array expands to 450 units but the active count remains 150; per-active-unit rate is unchanged. What prediction is valid?

  1. Total precursor output rises 50%
  2. Total precursor output unchanged in the model
  3. Total output doubles
  4. All copies must now be active
Answer and reasoning

B. Output is proportional to active count times rate, both unchanged. A uses total genomic copies; C has no basis; D conflicts with the stated active count. The active fraction drops from 50% to 33⅓%.

13. Two genomes have equal size and identical unique-component half-Cot values under matched conditions. Genome A has a 35% rapid component; B has a 5% rapid component. Which conclusion follows most directly?

  1. A has seven times as many genes
  2. A has a greater mass fraction of rapidly reassociating DNA
  3. B has no repetitive DNA
  4. A must have seven times as many copies of every repeat family
Answer and reasoning

B. 35% versus 5% indicates sevenfold greater mass in the rapid component. A confuses repeat mass with genes; C contradicts B's rapid component; D overgeneralizes across families and unit lengths.

14. A DNA family has 10,000 copies of a 200-bp motif. A second family has 100 copies of a 40-kb segment. Which statement is correct?

  1. The first family contributes twice the DNA mass
  2. The second contributes twice the mass despite lower copy count
  3. Both occupy 2 Mb
  4. Both must have identical half-Cot
Answer and reasoning

B. First = 2 Mb, second = 4 Mb. Thus B. A reverses the ratio; C incorrectly computes the second; D ignores effective copy number and sequence properties.
