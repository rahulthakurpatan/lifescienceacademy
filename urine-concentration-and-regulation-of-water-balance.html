1. The physiological problem of water conservation

The kidney must solve two problems at once: elimination of solutes produced by metabolism and retention of enough water to maintain a stable internal environment. These demands can oppose each other. Excreting nitrogenous wastes requires a solvent, but a terrestrial animal cannot always replace that solvent at the rate at which metabolic waste is produced. A mammalian kidney therefore separates the regulation of solute excretion from the regulation of water excretion within physiological limits. During water shortage, it can eliminate a relatively large amount of dissolved material in a relatively small volume of urine. After water intake exceeds immediate requirements, it can eliminate the excess as relatively dilute urine. Neither response implies that all solute excretion ceases or that filtration stops.

The significance of this capacity becomes clear when the extracellular fluid is considered as the immediate environment of cells. Loss of water without a proportional loss of effective osmoles raises extracellular tonicity. Water then leaves cells, including neurons. Conversely, an excess of water relative to effective extracellular solutes can make cells swell. The renal response must therefore be coordinated with sensory mechanisms in the brain that detect changes in body fluids and with behavioural responses that replenish water. The kidney can reduce losses but cannot generate a net supply of water from nothing. Drinking, water in food and metabolic water contribute to input; evaporation, breathing, faeces and urine contribute to output.

Consider two hypothetical individuals each excreting 600 milliosmoles of solute daily. If one produces urine at 300 mOsm per kilogram of water, approximately two litres of water are required to carry those osmoles. If the other produces urine at 1200 mOsm per kilogram, approximately half a litre suffices, under the usual approximation that one litre of dilute biological fluid contains about one kilogram of water. This fourfold difference illustrates why concentrating ability is adaptive. It does not imply that 1200 is an obligatory maximum for every person or species. Actual maxima depend on physiological state, age, solute supply and renal architecture. Nor is urine concentration the same thing as total daily solute excretion: the identical solute load can be excreted at different concentrations.

The central argument of the chapter rests on two separable requirements. First, the kidney must establish and preserve a hyperosmotic medullary interstitium. Second, the collecting duct must acquire enough water permeability to allow water to move down the resulting osmotic gradient. A gradient with no permeable pathway is ineffective, and a permeable pathway without a gradient cannot support sustained water extraction. This distinction provides a useful framework for interpreting virtually every defect in urine concentration.

2. Osmolality, osmolarity and tonicity

An osmole counts osmotically active particles rather than chemical mass. Ideally, one mole of glucose supplies one osmole of particles, whereas one mole of sodium chloride yields approximately two osmoles because it dissociates into sodium and chloride ions. Real solutions are not perfectly ideal, particularly at higher concentrations, so particle interactions influence the effective osmotic behaviour. Osmolality expresses osmoles per kilogram of solvent; osmolarity expresses osmoles per litre of solution. In many physiological calculations involving plasma or urine they are numerically close enough for a stated approximation, but the distinction must not be forgotten when precise measurements are required.

Tonicity asks a more specific biological question: will a solution make a cell shrink or swell at equilibrium? The answer depends on the permeability of the membrane to the solutes in question. A solute that remains largely outside a particular cell contributes strongly to sustained water movement across its membrane. A freely permeating solute may initially draw water but lose this effect after the solute equilibrates. Urea provides an important renal example. It contributes to measured osmolality but is relatively ineffective at sustaining tonicity across many cell membranes over time. Within the kidney, however, segment-specific differences in urea permeability make it an important contributor to local osmotic water transport. There is no contradiction: the relevant membrane and timescale are different.

Water movement across an epithelium requires a driving force and a route. For a simplified membrane, the effective osmotic pressure difference can be approximated by the sum of the concentration differences of individual solutes multiplied by their reflection coefficients and by RT. The reflection coefficient ranges conceptually from zero for a solute that does not exert a sustained osmotic force across that barrier to one for an effectively impermeant solute. The full net volume movement also depends on hydrostatic pressure and membrane hydraulic permeability. These ideas explain why a measured interstitial osmolality alone cannot predict flux without knowledge of the epithelial properties.

Aquaporins facilitate the passive movement of water. They do not hydrolyse ATP to transport water, and their presence cannot reverse the direction set by the water chemical potential difference. Active salt transport elsewhere in the nephron establishes conditions that favour water movement, so passive water recovery can depend indirectly on metabolic energy. This distinction between the energy source and the immediate transport mechanism is essential for predicting the effect of inhibiting sodium transport in a nephron segment.

3. Segmental permeability along the nephron

Filtration at the renal corpuscle generates a large flow of fluid with a small-solute composition broadly comparable to plasma water. The proximal tubule retrieves much of the filtered sodium, bicarbonate, nutrients and water. Its high water permeability permits water to follow solute transport, so the remaining tubular fluid generally remains near plasma osmolality despite a considerable reduction in volume. Thus near-isosmotic fluid does not indicate that the proximal tubule is inactive; it indicates that water and solute have been recovered in similar proportions.

The loop of Henle introduces a different epithelial arrangement. In the classical mammalian model, much of the thin descending limb has high water permeability, allowing tubular fluid to become concentrated as it passes into a more concentrated medulla. This is facilitated in many descending limb segments by aquaporin-1. However, the deepest segments of long loops in some mammals have little or no AQP1 and reduced water permeability; a single constant-permeability descending tube is a teaching simplification rather than a literal description of every nephron.

The ascending limb differs functionally from the descending limb. Thin ascending segments of long loops have low water permeability and can permit passive NaCl movement. The thick ascending limb actively reabsorbs NaCl but has very low water permeability. Sodium, potassium and chloride enter the tubular cell from the lumen through NKCC2 in a 1:1:2 stoichiometric ratio. Basolateral sodium-potassium ATPase maintains a low intracellular sodium activity and drives uptake indirectly. Chloride exits basolaterally, while potassium recycling into the lumen helps sustain the cotransporter. Removal of solute without accompanying water lowers tubular fluid osmolality and increases the solute concentration of the surrounding interstitium. The thick ascending limb consequently has two coupled roles: a medullary gradient-generating role and a tubular dilution role.

The early distal convoluted tubule further removes NaCl through NCC with relatively low water permeability. Beyond this region the connecting tubule and collecting duct provide adjustable water handling. Collecting duct principal cells change their apical water permeability in response to vasopressin. The final urine concentration is therefore not fixed by the fluid osmolality at the loop bend. In a water-loaded individual, dilute fluid entering the distal nephron can remain dilute through the collecting system. In dehydration, water can be removed along the collecting duct as it traverses the increasingly concentrated medulla.

A key analytical distinction is between tubular fluid, surrounding interstitial fluid and vascular blood. Three different samples taken at the same medullary depth can have different compositions and are not interchangeable. The direction of movement across each wall depends on gradients and the wall's permeability. Exam diagrams often omit the interstitium or place two tubules next to each other with arrows; the arrows represent exchange through the tissue environment rather than a direct connection between the lumens.

4. Countercurrent multiplication and the single effect

Countercurrent refers to the opposite directions of flow in adjacent channels. In a nephron loop, fluid descends towards the papilla and then turns to ascend towards the cortex. A local transverse difference in osmolality between the ascending tubular fluid and the surrounding interstitium can be generated by salt transport in the water-impermeable thick ascending limb. The descending limb can lose water into this interstitium. Because fresh fluid continuously enters the loop from the proximal tubule, and fluid already within the loop advances, the local transverse difference is repeatedly applied to fluid at successive positions.

To understand multiplication, imagine a simplified loop initially containing fluid at 300 mOsm per kilogram at every depth. Salt removal from its ascending limb makes that limb more dilute than the interstitium. The descending limb loses water until its luminal fluid approaches the local interstitial osmolality. If flow then advances one level, fresh fluid enters the descending limb from above while concentrated fluid near the bend enters the ascending limb. Renewed salt removal establishes another transverse difference, and water again leaves water-permeable descending segments. Repetition establishes a longitudinal gradient: progressively deeper levels can reach higher osmolalities than superficial levels, even though the local difference is relatively modest.

The single effect and the multiplied gradient refer to different spatial dimensions. The single effect is the local separation between adjacent fluid compartments at approximately the same depth. The corticomedullary gradient is the difference between superficial and deep interstitial regions. A student should not multiply one fixed numerical difference by an arbitrary count of nephron segments and treat the result as a quantitative prediction. The magnitude of the actual gradient depends on fluxes, permeability, solute and water entry, medullary architecture, urea distribution and vascular removal. The familiar stepwise teaching model demonstrates the principle, not a complete mass-balanced simulation.

Energy enters the system principally through active transport in the thick ascending limb, especially the basolateral ATPase that maintains the sodium gradient. The descending limb does not actively pump water to produce concentration. If oxygen availability or ATP supply becomes insufficient to maintain epithelial ion gradients, active salt transport declines, compromising the medullary gradient and the ability to dilute tubular fluid. If NKCC2 is selectively inhibited while other processes initially remain intact, salt recovery in the thick ascending limb decreases, delivery of salt downstream increases and the outer medullary interstitium becomes less concentrated. Even when vasopressin makes collecting ducts water-permeable, the reduced driving force limits urine concentration.

The outer medulla has thick ascending limbs that provide a well-established mechanism for generating its gradient. The inner medulla, by contrast, does not contain thick ascending limbs throughout its depth. A diagram showing active NKCC2-mediated salt pumping at the papillary tip incorrectly transfers an outer-medullary mechanism to a region with different epithelial segments. This anatomical restriction motivates separate consideration of inner-medullary urea accumulation and thin-limb transport.

5. The vasa recta and countercurrent exchange

The medulla requires a blood supply both for nutrient delivery and for removal of water and solutes reabsorbed by tubular epithelia. A simple network of rapidly flowing capillaries would tend to remove concentrated interstitial solutes and erode the gradient. The vasa recta, vascular structures associated with juxtamedullary nephrons, provide a hairpin arrangement that reduces this dissipative effect. Descending vasa recta travel into increasingly concentrated regions, while ascending vessels return toward the cortex.

As blood travels down the medulla, water tends to leave the descending vessels and diffusible solutes can enter them. On the return path, blood encounters progressively less concentrated interstitium. Solute can diffuse out while water enters the ascending vessels. The two limbs consequently exchange solutes and water with their environment in opposing directions. Solutes transferred into blood on the descent may be returned to the medullary tissue on ascent, reducing their net loss. At the same time, the ascending vessels remove net water reabsorbed from renal tubules and collecting ducts.

Countercurrent exchange is therefore primarily a gradient-preserving process rather than the active generator of the outer-medullary gradient. If all epithelial salt transport stopped, a passive vascular exchanger would not create an indefinitely maintained gradient on its own. Conversely, active tubular transport without an appropriate vascular arrangement would have to oppose a much larger solute washout. The distinction resembles the difference between providing an energy-driven process and reducing a leak in that process, although the living system is more complicated because the vascular supply must also support tissue metabolism.

Increasing medullary blood flow can increase net solute removal and reduce concentrating capacity when other conditions are similar. Reducing flow can favour retention of solute, but the effect has limits: extremely low flow impairs oxygen delivery and may compromise energy-dependent transport. Medullary circulation thus serves a balance between solute retention and tissue viability. The kidney does not simply shut off medullary perfusion during dehydration. In experiments manipulating vascular flow, blood flow, oxygen supply and filtration must be distinguished before a change in urine concentration is attributed solely to washout.

6. Urea recycling and the inner medullary gradient

Urea is produced during nitrogen metabolism and is transported to the kidney in the blood. It is readily filtered and undergoes segment-dependent reabsorption and secretion. In the collecting duct, water can be removed without an equivalent immediate loss of urea, raising luminal urea concentration. This concentration increase is particularly important upstream of the terminal inner medullary collecting duct, where regulated urea transport pathways allow urea to enter the surrounding tissue.

UT-A1 and UT-A3 facilitate urea movement across the inner medullary collecting duct epithelium. Vasopressin increases urea permeability in this region as well as water permeability in principal cells. Urea then accumulates in the inner medullary interstitium, where it contributes to measured osmolality and supports water recovery. Some interstitial urea can enter thin limbs and subsequently return to the collecting duct through tubular flow; vascular exchange also helps retain urea within the medulla. UT-A2 in thin descending segments and UT-B in vascular structures are relevant components of this network.

Recycling is not the same as permanent retention of all urea. At steady state, nitrogen production must be matched by net elimination. The same urea molecules may make several passages through the medullary exchange system before appearing in final urine. This behaviour is analogous to increasing the residence time of an osmole in a local compartment without requiring its indefinite retention in the whole organism. Reduced urea supply, for example under low-protein intake, can diminish maximal concentrating capacity because one major component of the inner-medullary osmotic environment becomes less available. Changes in nutrition also affect obligatory solute excretion, so the effects on final volume must be considered separately.

The classical passive inner-medullary hypothesis proposes that differential permeabilities permit NaCl to leave thin ascending limbs while urea-rich interstitial fluid supports appropriate local gradients. The physiological importance of urea and the absence of active thick ascending transport at the papillary tip are established. However, the complete quantitative mechanism producing the observed inner-medullary gradient remains incompletely resolved, and contributions vary among species and nephron segments. Modern interpretations incorporate three-dimensional organisation, heterogeneous permeabilities and local exchanges. A NET-level account should distinguish robust principles from the detailed hypothesis rather than declare the entire inner-medullary mechanism completely established.

7. Vasopressin receptors and aquaporin regulation

Arginine vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (ADH), is a peptide synthesized primarily by magnocellular neurons in the hypothalamic supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. It is transported along axons to the posterior pituitary, from which it enters the systemic circulation following neuronal activation. The posterior pituitary is therefore a major release site rather than the primary site of hormone synthesis. This distinction connects neuroendocrine anatomy with the kidney response.

At renal collecting duct principal cells, vasopressin binds V2 receptors on the basolateral membrane. The receptor couples to Gs, which stimulates adenylyl cyclase. ATP is converted to cyclic AMP, and the rise in cAMP activates protein kinase A. Signalling modifies trafficking and phosphorylation of AQP2-containing vesicles, increasing the abundance of AQP2 water channels in the apical plasma membrane. This creates a pathway from tubular lumen into the cell. Water leaves across the basolateral membrane, facilitated by AQP3 and AQP4. The ultimate movement into the medullary circulation follows the local driving forces; none of these aquaporins actively pumps water.

The rapid response relies substantially on redistribution of pre-existing channels, whereas longer-term changes in vasopressin exposure can alter total AQP2 abundance and other transport proteins. Removal of the hormone decreases apical channel abundance by changing membrane trafficking. This temporal distinction explains why a response can occur without waiting for new gene transcription and why chronic changes in hormonal status can nevertheless remodel concentrating ability.

V2 receptor function should not be confused with V1a-mediated actions on vascular smooth muscle. The receptor subtype, cellular location and intracellular signalling machinery determine the response to a shared extracellular hormone. V2 signalling is particularly important for antidiuresis. A disruption at the receptor, G protein, cAMP signalling, AQP2 trafficking or the water channel itself may reduce the kidney's response despite a normal or elevated circulating concentration of vasopressin.

The two requirements established at the start of the chapter can now be applied experimentally. In a collecting duct exposed to vasopressin, increasing apical water permeability allows faster osmotic water transport when an effective gradient exists. If luminal and interstitial effective osmolalities are identical and hydrostatic driving forces are negligible, increasing permeability alone does not create a sustained directional flux. Similarly, a large gradient cannot produce the expected water recovery through an epithelium that remains nearly water-impermeable. Vasopressin also affects urea transport and some other renal transport processes; its actions are therefore broader than AQP2 alone.

8. Osmoreceptors, thirst and volume-sensitive control

The brain continuously integrates information about body fluid composition. Osmosensitive neurons in regions including the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis and subfornical organ participate in the response to effective extracellular osmolality. These regions have access to circulating signals and communicate with hypothalamic networks controlling vasopressin release and thirst. When extracellular tonicity rises, osmotic water movement alters the volume and mechanical state of sensitive cells and can increase the activity of pathways that promote antidiuresis and drinking.

The output is an example of negative feedback. Water loss increases the effective solute concentration of extracellular fluid, stimulates vasopressin release and thirst, promotes renal water retention and encourages water intake. The resulting restoration of body water tends to reduce the original stimulus. Following excess water intake, plasma tonicity tends to fall, vasopressin secretion is suppressed and the collecting duct becomes less water-permeable, allowing water excretion. The exact response is graded, not an on-off switch at a universal threshold. Individual variation, physiological conditions and measurement methods shift the relationship.

Signals about effective arterial circulation also influence vasopressin release. Reduced stretch in cardiovascular sensory pathways and other circulatory signals can stimulate the hormone during substantial volume depletion. This response can override the usual osmotic tendency, because preservation of adequate tissue perfusion is an immediate physiological priority. An isotonic haemorrhage, for instance, need not initially raise plasma osmolality; it can nevertheless increase vasopressin through volume-related pathways. The detailed regulation of sodium content and arterial pressure through renal hormones is developed in the next chapter.

Thirst complements renal control because water conservation alone cannot fully compensate for continuing losses from evaporation, respiration and unavoidable urine production. Drinking can also suppress thirst before absorbed water has restored systemic osmolality, through anticipatory sensory and gastrointestinal signals. The response illustrates how neural feedforward control can coexist with slower feedback from circulating fluid composition. The homeostatic variable is not simply the volume of fluid in the stomach. Rather, behavioural and hormonal responses combine predictions about incoming water with measurements of the existing internal environment.

9. Water diuresis, antidiuresis and solute diuresis

After a water load, secretion of vasopressin falls and apical AQP2 abundance declines. The thick ascending limb and early distal tubule can still remove salt without equivalent water reabsorption. Consequently, hypotonic tubular fluid arrives at the later nephron and much of its water is retained in the lumen. Final urine volume increases and osmolality decreases. This is water diuresis: water is being excreted in excess of the volume that would accompany an isotonic equivalent of the solute excretion.

During antidiuresis, vasopressin promotes collecting duct water permeability and supports inner-medullary urea handling. Fluid entering the collecting duct may initially be hypotonic, but water recovery makes the remaining luminal fluid progressively more concentrated as it passes through the medulla. High urine osmolality and low urine flow usually occur together under a fixed solute load, although the exact relationship changes if solute excretion changes. The response need not require a large fall in glomerular filtration. Fine regulation of the fraction of filtered water excreted can produce substantial changes in final urine volume.

A different mechanism produces osmotic or solute diuresis. If a large quantity of poorly reabsorbed solute remains in the tubular lumen, it retains water and increases urine flow. This can occur with glucose when filtered load exceeds reabsorptive capacity, or with a deliberately administered non-reabsorbed osmole in an experimental preparation. High flow alone therefore does not establish vasopressin deficiency or low collecting duct water permeability. A urine sample can have high osmolality and still be produced at a high flow rate if solute excretion is sufficiently high.

Consider an illustrative comparison. A person excreting 600 mOsm each day in urine at 100 mOsm per kilogram produces about six litres of urine. A person excreting 1800 mOsm each day at 600 mOsm per kilogram produces approximately three litres. Both have elevated urine volume relative to a typical resting output, but the second excretes considerably more solute and has more concentrated urine. The appropriate interpretation requires both flow and concentration. A concentration is a ratio, while an excretion rate is an amount per time. Confusing these quantities is a frequent source of incorrect examination predictions.

10. Osmolar clearance and free-water clearance

Clearance is the hypothetical volume of plasma cleared of a substance per unit time. For the aggregate of measured urinary osmoles, osmolar clearance is conventionally expressed as C_osm = U_osm × V / P_osm. Here U_osm is urine osmolality, P_osm is plasma osmolality, and V is urine flow rate. If osmolalities are in the same units, their ratio is dimensionless and clearance has the units used for V, commonly mL/min. The usual calculation treats volumetric flow and water mass flow as approximately interchangeable for dilute physiological fluids.

Why does this formula work? The solute excretion rate is approximately U_osm times V. Dividing this quantity by plasma osmolality identifies the volume of plasma-equivalent isotonic fluid that would contain the excreted solute load. This hypothetical isotonic volume need not be the volume actually excreted by the kidney. The difference between actual urine flow and the isotonic equivalent is the conventional free-water clearance, C_H2O = V − C_osm. Combining the equations gives C_H2O = V(1 − U_osm/P_osm).

If urine osmolality is below plasma osmolality, the ratio is less than one and free-water clearance is positive. The kidney excretes water beyond the isotonic equivalent of its solute output. If urine and plasma osmolality match, the calculated clearance is zero. If urine osmolality is greater than plasma osmolality, free-water clearance is negative, indicating that the kidney excretes less water than the isotonic equivalent. The magnitude of negative clearance is sometimes expressed as free-water reabsorption, T_H2O = C_osm − V, in the concentrating state. Negative clearance is not a negative urine volume.

Take a hypothetical water-loaded subject with plasma osmolality 300 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 75 mOsm/kg and urine flow 8 mL/min. The ratio is 0.25; osmolar clearance is 2 mL/min and free-water clearance is +6 mL/min. In a second illustrative state with urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, the same plasma osmolality and a urine flow of 0.7 mL/min, osmolar clearance is 2.1 mL/min and free-water clearance is −1.4 mL/min. Solute output is similar in these examples despite the marked difference in urine flow. The signs of free-water clearance capture the difference between diluting and concentrating responses.

Free-water clearance is an accounting construct, not a direct measurement of water crossing one membrane or the absolute change in whole-body water. Total body balance also depends on intake and nonrenal losses. Moreover, conventional osmolar clearance includes urea and other osmoles that may not influence systemic tonicity in the same way as sodium and potassium salts. An electrolyte-free water calculation addresses a related but distinct question about water handling relative to these electrolytes. In a problem that specifies ordinary free-water clearance, the osmolality formula must be used unless another definition is explicitly stated.

11. Experimental evidence and perturbation reasoning

A physiological model becomes persuasive when independent measurements support its predicted relationships. Micropuncture allows collection of small samples of tubular fluid from accessible nephron segments. If investigators compare solute concentrations and appropriate volume markers between proximal and distal sampling sites, they can infer water and solute recovery. A decrease in osmolality along an ascending segment supports salt removal in excess of water removal, but concentration alone cannot establish an absolute transport rate without a measure of flow. Sampling accessibility also constrains which regions can be investigated directly.

Isolated perfused tubules offer a different type of evidence. A tubular segment is exposed to controlled luminal and bath solutions, allowing water or solute permeability to be examined while selected variables are changed. To test hormonal effects on water permeability, an investigator can compare otherwise matched collecting ducts under a fixed osmotic gradient with and without vasopressin. Measuring net water movement establishes a functional response. Applying a V2 receptor antagonist or interfering with downstream signalling can test whether the observed response depends on that pathway. Appropriate controls include epithelial integrity, exposure time, temperature and equivalent starting osmotic gradients.

Localization of AQP2 in the apical membrane or intracellular vesicles helps connect epithelial permeability to cellular structure. The strongest mechanistic inference comes from combining localization with functional flux measurements, rather than assuming that the presence of a protein necessarily proves its activity. Genetic or experimental loss of AQP2 function can establish that normal water transport depends on the channel, while a rescue of function can strengthen causal interpretation. Such evidence must be considered alongside possible compensatory changes in other transport proteins.

For a prediction exercise, consider a hypothetical preparation with a functioning medullary gradient but impaired V2 receptors. Exogenous vasopressin would have a limited effect on collecting duct water permeability, whereas a preparation lacking endogenous hormone with intact receptor and downstream machinery could respond to added hormone. In a third preparation with disrupted thick ascending salt transport, an intact V2 response might fail to restore normal maximal urine osmolality because the osmotic driving force is reduced. A failure to concentrate urine can thus arise upstream, downstream or in the vascular preservation of the gradient. The mechanistic location of the failure determines which additional observation will distinguish competing explanations.

12. Comparative physiology and integrated water conservation

The capacity to concentrate urine varies considerably among mammals. Many desert-adapted rodents possess relatively extensive renal medullae and long loops in juxtamedullary nephrons, features associated with the ability to generate substantial longitudinal gradients. Long loops do not alone guarantee maximal concentration: transport protein distribution, urea supply, vascular organisation and regulation of collecting duct permeability must also support the gradient. Relative medullary thickness provides a useful comparative anatomical indicator, but species-specific exceptions and differences in body size complicate simple predictions.

Most teleost fishes lack mammalian-type loops of Henle. Freshwater fishes experience osmotic entry of water and generally eliminate relatively dilute urine while recovering ions; marine teleosts face osmotic water loss, drink seawater and use extrarenal ion excretion, particularly through gill ionocytes, as part of their water balance strategy. These patterns cannot be explained by simply assigning a mammalian renal concentration mechanism to every vertebrate. Birds possess both looped and loopless nephrons and have additional water-saving mechanisms associated with uric acid excretion and postrenal modification. Reptilian kidneys lack mammalian-style loops, and many reptiles rely on nitrogen excretion as uric acid and extrarenal or postrenal mechanisms for water conservation.

The same ecological challenge may therefore be solved by changes in different organs and processes. A low obligatory urinary water loss can be achieved through a reduction in the amount of freely dissolved nitrogenous waste, through a high concentrating capacity or through recovery of water beyond the kidney. Comparison requires awareness of the physical form of excreted nitrogen, the salinity of the environment, routes of water intake and nonrenal water losses. The mammalian loop mechanism is one evolved strategy within a broader set of physiological adaptations.

An integrated prediction for dehydration begins with water loss from the body. If that loss exceeds effective solute loss, extracellular tonicity rises; neural osmoregulatory responses stimulate drinking and vasopressin release. The collecting duct becomes more water-permeable, urea transport supports inner-medullary osmotic conditions and the kidney reduces urinary water loss. If substantial circulatory depletion accompanies dehydration, volume-related hormonal signals reinforce conservation. As water is replaced, feedback diminishes the hormonal response. The next node examines how renal sodium balance, circulating volume and arterial pressure are regulated alongside this system. The distinction between total sodium content and sodium concentration will be particularly important: water balance strongly affects concentration, whereas sodium balance contributes to extracellular volume.

Original examination practice

1. Part B — Which structure mainly generates the outer-medullary single effect?

Answer: Thick ascending limb

Active NaCl recovery with low water permeability separates solute from water.

2. Part B — Which membrane contains the vasopressin-regulated AQP2 channel?

Answer: Apical membrane of collecting duct principal cells

V2 signalling promotes AQP2 insertion at the luminal boundary; AQP3/4 facilitate basolateral exit.

3. Part B — Where is vasopressin synthesized?

Answer: Hypothalamic magnocellular neurons

The posterior pituitary releases the hormone transported from neuronal cell bodies.

4. Part B — What is the primary role of the vasa recta in concentrating urine?

Answer: Limit washout by countercurrent exchange

The vascular hairpin conserves interstitial solutes while removing net reabsorbed water.

5. Part B — What is the sign of free-water clearance when urine is hypotonic to plasma?

Answer: Positive

The volume excreted exceeds the plasma-equivalent isotonic volume of its solutes.

6. Part B — What supplies the energy indirectly driving NKCC2 uptake?

Answer: Basolateral sodium-potassium ATPase

The pump maintains low intracellular sodium; NKCC2 uses this gradient.

7. Part B — What property is especially important in the terminal inner medullary collecting duct?

Answer: Regulated urea permeability

Urea movement into the medulla contributes to the osmotic environment.

8. Part B — Does a high urine osmolality necessarily imply high daily solute excretion?

Answer: No

Excretion equals concentration multiplied by flow.

9. Part C — Plasma osmolality is 300 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 150 mOsm/kg and flow 4 mL/min. Calculate both clearances.

Answer: C_osm = 2 mL/min; C_H2O = +2 mL/min

The ratio is 0.5; multiply by flow and subtract from total flow.

10. Part C — A preparation has a large medullary gradient but no functional V2 receptors. Predict response to vasopressin.

Answer: Limited restoration of collecting duct water permeability

The receptor-mediated signalling step is missing despite an available gradient.

11. Part C — A selective NKCC2 inhibitor reduces medullary osmolality. Why may added vasopressin fail to restore maximum concentration?

Answer: The osmotic driving force has been reduced

Water permeability and the medullary gradient are separate requirements.

12. Part C — Two animals excrete 600 mOsm/day at 200 and 1000 mOsm/kg respectively. Estimate daily urine volumes.

Answer: 3 L and 0.6 L

Divide daily osmoles by urine osmolality, using the approximate kg/L conversion.

13. Part C — An isolated duct has equal effective osmolalities on both sides and equal hydrostatic pressure. What happens when AQP2 increases?

Answer: Permeability increases without sustained net osmotic water flow

A water channel cannot generate a gradient.

14. Part C — After isotonic blood loss, plasma osmolality initially remains stable. Can vasopressin increase?

Answer: Yes

Reduced circulatory stretch and effective arterial volume can stimulate secretion independently of tonicity.

15. Part C — A solute-loaded subject has urine osmolality 600 mOsm/kg and flow 5 mL/min with plasma osmolality 300 mOsm/kg. Is free-water clearance positive?

Answer: No; it is −5 mL/min

Osmolar clearance equals 10 mL/min. High flow alone is not evidence of water diuresis.

16. Part C — An experimental increase in medullary blood flow reduces urine concentration despite intact vasopressin signalling. Explain.

Answer: Enhanced medullary solute washout

Vascular exchange preserves a gradient that excessive flow may dissipate.

Revision and mastery

Separate gradient generation, gradient preservation and adjustable water permeability. Follow the brain–kidney feedback loop and use urine flow together with urine osmolality to calculate water handling.
