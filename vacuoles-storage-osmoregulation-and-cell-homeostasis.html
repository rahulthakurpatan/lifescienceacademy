1. Why a cell needs a vacuole

A young plant cell that will eventually become part of a leaf must solve two problems at the same time. It needs enough water to expand, but it also needs to maintain a cytoplasmic environment in which enzymes can work. If the growing cell simply filled its cytoplasm with water, its proteins, metabolites and other essential components would become diluted. If it instead filled the entire cell with concentrated, metabolically active cytoplasm, growth would demand a large investment in protein, membranes and energy. The central vacuole provides a solution: the cell can increase its volume by expanding an aqueous compartment that is separated from the cytoplasm.

Imagine a cell that takes up a large amount of potassium, nitrate and sugar from its surroundings or its own metabolism. Each substance may be useful, but its concentration in the cytosol cannot rise indefinitely without disturbing osmotic balance, enzyme function and membrane potentials. A selectively permeable internal membrane makes it possible to move some of these substances out of the cytosol without losing them from the cell. When demand increases, a suitable transporter can return a stored substance to the cytosol. Storage is therefore not a passive consequence of having a large cavity. It depends on regulated transport in both directions.

A third problem arises when a cell must break down damaged proteins, membranes or material delivered through vesicles. Many of the enzymes that perform these tasks work most efficiently at an acidic pH. The cytosol, however, is maintained closer to neutrality. A vacuole can retain hydrolases in a separate lumen and establish a chemical environment favorable for digestion. The same general architectural idea solves all three problems: a lipid bilayer creates a compartment, and proteins within that bilayer control exchanges with the surrounding cytoplasm.

The word vacuole can be misleading because it suggests an empty space. A living vacuole contains water, dissolved ions, metabolites, enzymes and, in certain cells, pigments, insoluble deposits or storage proteins. What makes these diverse structures biologically comparable is their membrane boundary and specialized contents, not a single universal function. A freshwater protist may use a vacuolar complex to expel excess water rather than retain it; a seed cell may use a protein storage vacuole as a reserve for germination; a fungal cell may use its vacuole for digestion and nutrient buffering simultaneously. The following sections explain how differences in membrane transport, cargo and environmental conditions produce these different outcomes.

The necessary background is the lipid bilayer, selective permeability, active transport, osmosis and vesicle fusion. A membrane protein may move a solute against its electrochemical gradient by using chemical energy directly or coupling its movement to that of another solute. Water usually moves down its water chemical potential difference through the bilayer or water channels. A membrane that can regulate solutes can therefore regulate water movement indirectly. This connection will recur throughout the chapter.

2. Central vacuole and the organization of a plant cell

In a typical mature vegetative plant cell, a large central vacuole occupies much of the volume, often around eighty to ninety percent, although the proportion is highly dependent on tissue, development and water status. The living cytoplasm forms a relatively thin layer between the vacuole and the plasma membrane. The nucleus, chloroplasts where present, mitochondria and other organelles occupy this peripheral cytoplasm or are located along strands of cytoplasm that cross the vacuolar region. Such strands are bounded by tonoplast where they meet the lumen; they are not open tunnels of cell sap.

The wall, plasma membrane and tonoplast have distinct roles. The extracellular cell wall resists expansion and supplies mechanical strength. The plasma membrane separates the protoplast from the extracellular environment and controls entry and exit at the cell surface. The tonoplast is the single lipid bilayer enclosing the vacuolar lumen. Thus, a molecule moving from the apoplast into the vacuole generally has to cross the plasma membrane and then the tonoplast, unless it is carried by a suitable membrane-trafficking pathway. The term single membrane means one bilayer, not a single layer of phospholipid molecules. The vacuole does not possess the double envelope, independent genome or ribosomes characteristic of mitochondria and chloroplasts.

The cell sap is an aqueous mixture with composition different from that of the cytosol. Potassium is commonly an abundant cation; nitrate, chloride, phosphate and organic acid anions may contribute to its charge balance. Sugars, amino acids and other metabolites occur in amounts that differ among tissues. Some vacuoles contain high concentrations of specialized compounds, and some include crystalline or dense protein deposits. Lumen composition cannot be inferred from water content alone. A large transparent compartment may have considerable dissolved material that is invisible in ordinary bright-field microscopy.

Young meristematic cells often contain smaller, multiple vacuolar compartments. As differentiation proceeds, these compartments can enlarge and fuse or remodel into a dominant central vacuole. The traditional picture of one large vacuole describes many mature plant cells well, but is not a rule that every plant cell must obey. Different vacuoles may coexist, particularly during seed development or transitions between storage and vegetative growth. The shape is dynamic because tonoplast can undergo fusion, fission, stretching and membrane turnover.

How does a microscopist distinguish a real vacuole from a space created by poor fixation? A living cell can be examined using transmitted light or fluorescence with a tonoplast-localized marker. A fluorescent outline that surrounds a region largely devoid of cytoplasmic marker provides spatial evidence for a membrane-enclosed compartment. Electron microscopy can reveal the bounding membrane, but preparation may alter water-filled structures and their apparent shape. The most convincing interpretation combines a membrane marker, a lumen marker and an intact-cell observation rather than treating a clear patch in a micrograph as proof of an empty organelle.

3. Vacuole diversity, formation and cargo delivery

Plant vacuoles are often described as lytic vacuoles and protein storage vacuoles. A lytic vacuole receives degradative enzymes, usually maintains an acidic lumen and participates in the turnover of macromolecules and cellular structures. A protein storage vacuole accumulates proteins that can be mobilized when the seed germinates. The classification concerns a compartment's prevailing function and composition; it is not an assertion that one type can never contain digestive enzymes or that the other type can never store nutrients. During development, identity can change. A protein storage vacuole may become more hydrolytically active during germination as its reserves are consumed.

Vacuoles participate in the endomembrane system. The preceding chapters on ER and Golgi explain how soluble secretory proteins are inserted into the ER lumen and how sorting information guides them to different destinations. The same principle applies to many vacuolar hydrolases and storage proteins. They enter the ER, travel through sorting compartments including the Golgi or trans-Golgi network, and are delivered to the vacuole, often via prevacuolar or endosomal compartments. Some plant storage cargo reaches vacuoles by routes that do not require the conventional passage through the Golgi. These exceptions matter because observing a protein in the vacuole does not uniquely identify the route it used.

Soluble proteins and tonoplast proteins present different targeting problems. A soluble hydrolase must end up inside the vacuolar lumen. A transporter must become incorporated in the tonoplast with the appropriate orientation so that its binding sites face the right compartments. Sorting signals and recognition machinery determine delivery, while membrane fusion preserves orientation. When a carrier vesicle fuses with a vacuolar membrane, the lumen of the vesicle becomes continuous with the vacuolar lumen and the membrane becomes part of the vacuolar boundary. A soluble hydrolase does not need to cross the hydrophobic interior of the tonoplast as a folded molecule. This is an example of topology rather than active transport of a small solute.

An important distinction from mammalian lysosomal targeting is that the mannose-6-phosphate mechanism is not the general plant vacuolar targeting rule. Plant cells can recognize vacuolar sorting determinants in their protein cargo through appropriate receptors. Yeast also uses its own sorting systems, such as a receptor for carboxypeptidase Y. The shared concept is recognition of destination information, not identity of every signal. Predict that mutation of a sorting signal can cause a soluble protein to be secreted or mislocalized even when the protein itself is enzymatically normal. By contrast, a proton-pump defect can leave the protein correctly located but reduce its activity.

The vacuole is continuously maintained rather than synthesized once and left unchanged. Fusion of incoming vesicles adds membrane and cargo; membrane recycling, turnover and fission can redistribute membrane and its transport proteins. Inheritance of vacuolar material in dividing cells and changes in cell shape require this remodeling. The details vary among plants and fungi, but the organelle-level lesson is consistent: identity arises from selective delivery, retention, removal and chemical homeostasis, not from the appearance of the compartment alone.

4. Tonoplast acidification and the energy source for transport

Most lytic plant vacuoles and fungal vacuoles maintain a lumen more acidic than their cytosol. A typical illustrative plant value is near pH 5–6, while a cytosolic value near 7 is common; the actual pH can differ substantially between species, tissues, vacuole types and physiological states. An acidic lumen is a steady state maintained by membrane transport. It is not simply a residue of organic acids entering the organelle. Without continual energy input, protons tend to move down their electrochemical gradient through leak pathways and coupled transporters.

Two proton pumps are particularly important in higher plant tonoplasts. The vacuolar H⁺-ATPase, abbreviated V-ATPase, hydrolyzes ATP on the cytosolic side and transfers protons from cytosol into the vacuolar lumen. Its peripheral V₁ sector catalyzes ATP hydrolysis, while the membrane V₀ sector provides the pathway for proton movement. Coupling between these sectors converts chemical energy into directed ion transport. This is a primary active transport process: the transported species can move uphill because ATP hydrolysis provides the energy. Unlike the mitochondrial ATP synthase acting during respiration, its normal role here is to spend chemical energy to establish an ion gradient.

The H⁺-translocating inorganic pyrophosphatase, or V-PPase, uses hydrolysis of inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) to drive proton movement into the vacuole. PPi is formed as a by-product of many biosynthetic reactions. Using it as an energy source for transport provides another connection between metabolism and vacuolar physiology. The relative importance of these two pumps differs among cell types and developmental conditions. Fungal and animal lysosome-like compartments commonly rely on V-ATPase; do not assume that every vacuole in every organism contains a plant-type V-PPase.

Both pumps move positive charge across the membrane. If only H⁺ moved inward, the vacuolar side would become electrically positive relative to the cytosol and further proton pumping would become increasingly unfavorable. Charge-compensating movements, such as anion entry or cation exit through appropriate transport proteins, permit more sustained acidification. The membrane potential and pH gradient are therefore coupled. A counterion pathway does not have to pump protons itself to influence lumen pH. The equilibrium of charges in the bulk solutions remains approximately neutral even though a very small net charge separation across the membrane generates a voltage.

A proton gradient is not a measure of how many total acid molecules are in the lumen. pH measures hydrogen ion activity, and buffers bind or release protons. Thus, a buffered vacuole may require a great deal of proton transport to change its pH by one unit. A simple pH measurement cannot determine pumping rate unless buffer capacity, volume and proton leak are also known. A steady pH means that net acidification and deacidification balance; it does not mean that the pumps have ceased working.

An important experimental distinction is between acidification and membrane potential. A protonophore increases proton permeability, dissipating the proton electrochemical gradient even if the pump remains catalytically active. Inhibiting V-ATPase reduces a source of the gradient, but a plant vacuole with an active V-PPase may maintain part of it. Inhibiting an anion pathway may reduce acidification by increasing electrical opposition. A fall in luminal acidity is therefore evidence that acid homeostasis has changed, not proof that a particular pump is absent. The next section shows how the energy stored in this gradient is used to move other solutes.

5. Secondary transport, channels and vacuolar ion homeostasis

A proton gradient is a source of free energy because the chemical potential of protons differs on opposite sides of the tonoplast. A secondary active transporter can couple the energetically favorable return of protons to the cytosol to an otherwise unfavorable movement of another solute into the lumen. A plant NHX-type cation/H⁺ exchanger is an important example. Depending on the isoform and conditions, it can exchange Na⁺ or K⁺ with H⁺. It does not directly hydrolyze ATP; the primary pumps restore the proton gradient consumed by the exchange. Distinguish this mechanism from an ATP-driven Na⁺ pump and from a passive Na⁺ channel.

The term antiport describes two coupled species traveling in opposite directions. Suppose protons leave an acidic vacuole and potassium enters. The exchanger can contribute to potassium accumulation, but its direction and maximum achievable gradient depend on the ion activities, coupling ratio and voltage. For an ideal electroneutral exchange of one monovalent cation for one proton, the electrical terms cancel. The energy gained by moving H⁺ down its chemical gradient can pay for concentrating the cation. The resulting storage increases vacuolar osmotic capacity and allows the cytosol to maintain a potassium concentration appropriate for its enzymes and electrical properties.

Calcium demonstrates why storage also matters for signaling. Cytosolic free Ca²⁺ is generally kept very low compared with calcium in many storage compartments. A brief rise in cytosolic calcium can serve as a signal; a persistently high concentration may disrupt cellular function. Vacuolar Ca²⁺/H⁺ exchangers (such as plant CAX proteins) and other calcium transport systems can help sequester calcium, whereas suitable release pathways permit calcium to return to the cytosol. Both sequestration and release are physiologically important. The energy requirement for transport of a divalent ion is not necessarily the same as for a monovalent ion, and it would be incorrect to assume all calcium exchangers have a universal one-to-one stoichiometry.

Anion transport contributes to both osmotic storage and charge balance. Nitrate can accumulate in the vacuole as a reserve and be made available for assimilation later. Certain plant CLC transporters function as anion/proton exchangers rather than simple chloride channels; their substrate preferences differ. The important exam principle is that the identity and direction of a membrane transporter cannot be deduced from a family name alone. Chloride and other anions may also enter or leave through passive pathways where the electrochemical gradients permit. Channels allow facilitated diffusion and do not create a sustained uphill gradient by themselves.

Organic solutes are also selectively distributed. Some tonoplast transporters use proton coupling to accumulate sugars, organic acids or other metabolites; others allow regulated export. Certain ATP-binding cassette transporters use ATP directly to accumulate specific organic compounds, including conjugated xenobiotics. This is primary transport despite the presence of a pre-existing proton gradient. If the gradient collapses, not every route of solute uptake must stop. Conversely, inhibition of an ATP-dependent transporter need not abolish the transport of a substrate that has an alternative route.

Water channels of the tonoplast, especially the tonoplast intrinsic protein (TIP) aquaporins, accelerate water movement. They do not use ATP to propel water against its water potential gradient and do not determine the final equilibrium by themselves. They control the speed of response when the cell changes lumen solute concentration or when the external medium changes. Some aquaporins also conduct particular small neutral solutes, but this is not a universal property. A tonoplast may therefore contain channels, secondary active carriers and primary pumps whose distinct mechanisms work together. The homeostatic result is not determined by any one protein alone.

6. Quantitative reasoning: what a proton gradient can accomplish

The energetics of vacuolar storage can be understood using the electrochemical potential introduced in membrane transport. Define c as the cytosol and v as the vacuolar lumen. For moving a solute of charge number z from c to v, the molar free energy change is ΔG = RT ln(aᵥ/a꜀) + zF(ψᵥ − ψ꜀). Here a is activity, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is absolute temperature, F ≈ 96,485 C mol⁻¹ and ψ is electric potential in volts. Concentration may approximate activity in dilute ideal examples. A positive ΔG requires an energy source; a negative ΔG favors the indicated movement. A membrane protein changes the rate or couples the process to another reaction; it cannot change the thermodynamic sign of an uncoupled transfer.

Consider a plant vacuole at pH 5.5 and cytosol at pH 7.0. Because pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺), the ratio of lumen to cytosolic proton activity is 10^(7.0−5.5) = 31.6. The chemical contribution for pumping one mole of H⁺ into the lumen at 298 K is RT ln(31.6), or about 8.56 kJ mol⁻¹. If the lumen is 20 mV positive, the electrical contribution is 96,485 × 0.020 = 1.93 kJ mol⁻¹. The complete transfer costs approximately 10.49 kJ per mole of protons. Reversing the transport direction reverses both signs. A larger positive lumen potential therefore increases the cost of inward proton pumping under these conditions.

Now consider an ideal exchanger moving one K⁺ into the lumen for every H⁺ leaving it. Write the chemical and electrical free energies of both transfers and add them. Because equal charges travel in opposite directions, zFΔψ for K⁺ cancels −FΔψ for H⁺. The net free energy is RT ln[(a(K⁺)ᵥ/a(K⁺)꜀)/(a(H⁺)ᵥ/a(H⁺)꜀)]. At equilibrium ΔG = 0, so the two activity ratios are equal. In our example, the ideal limiting potassium activity ratio is 31.6. If cytosolic potassium activity is approximated by a concentration of 10 mM, the theoretical limiting lumen concentration is about 316 mM. At a lumen concentration of 100 mM, the coupled reaction remains favorable because the potassium ratio of ten is less than 31.6.

This is a model, not a prediction that actual plant vacuoles maintain exactly that concentration. Real cells have additional channels, leaks, ion binding, variable pH, multiple exchange pathways and limited energy supply. At high concentrations, activities can differ appreciably from concentrations. For a non-electroneutral exchanger the voltage terms do not cancel, and for different coupling stoichiometries the equilibrium expression changes. In an exam, identify the direction and number of each transported species before substituting values.

The same logic clarifies a common mistake concerning pH. A shift from pH 5 to pH 6 is a tenfold decline in proton activity, not a decrease of one-sixth. If both cell compartments were at pH 7, a one-for-one electroneutral K⁺/H⁺ exchanger would have no pH-driven ability to establish a K⁺ concentration ratio greater than one. A voltage alone would not rescue this ideal electroneutral exchange because its electrical terms cancel. Other electrogenic or ATP-driven mechanisms could still concentrate potassium. The distinction is crucial when interpreting experiments in which one transporter appears to retain some activity after a proton gradient has been reduced.

7. Cell sap as a nutrient reserve, pigment store and chemical defense

A vacuole separates the total amount of a substance within the cell from its immediately available cytosolic concentration. When nutrients are abundant, the cell may transfer some into a vacuolar pool. When external supply falls or demand rises, release from the pool can support metabolism. A nitrate reserve, for example, allows nitrate uptake and nitrate assimilation to proceed at different rates. The nitrogen remains within the cell even though most stored nitrate is not yet incorporated into amino acids or proteins. An assay of whole-cell nitrate cannot reveal how much is available in the cytosol without information about compartmentation and transport.

Sugars and organic acids can make up substantial parts of the sap in particular fruits, leaves or storage tissues. The vacuole may hold sucrose, glucose, fructose or malate in amounts that reflect the tissue's metabolism and transporter expression. In CAM plants, temporary storage of malic acid in vacuoles during the night links compartmentation to the daily pattern of carbon metabolism; detailed CAM biochemistry is covered in plant physiology. The organelle-level lesson is that an organic acid reserve can be accumulated and subsequently mobilized on a schedule while cytosolic metabolic reactions remain regulated. Free proton activity, total malate content and total titratable acidity are related but distinct measurements because buffering and dissociation matter.

Seed protein storage vacuoles illustrate a different physical form of storage. Storage proteins can reach very high concentrations and may be packed in dense aggregates or specialized inclusions. Some seed vacuoles contain phytate-rich globoids that bind phosphate and mineral cations. During germination, metabolic demand changes before a young seedling can supply all its own carbon and nitrogen. Mobilization of stored proteins and other reserves provides amino acids and nutrients. Increased digestive capacity at this stage is useful rather than harmful because the stored material has served its original purpose. The timing of mobilization is therefore as important as the amount stored.

Vacuoles also accumulate many water-soluble pigments, particularly anthocyanins. These pigments may contribute to red, purple or blue coloration of petals, fruits and leaves. Their color depends on chemical structure, pH, interactions with other compounds and sometimes metal ions. A change in hue after a change in vacuolar pH is plausible even without a change in pigment amount. By contrast, chlorophyll resides primarily in chloroplast thylakoid membranes, and many carotenoids are found in plastids. A colored plant compartment cannot automatically be identified as a vacuole; the nature of the pigment and the localization evidence matter.

Chemical defense benefits from separation of potentially reactive substances from sensitive cellular enzymes. Plants may store alkaloids, phenolics, glucosinolates or other defensive compounds in particular vacuoles or cells. In some defense systems, a stored precursor and the enzyme that activates it are separated until tissue damage brings them together. This arrangement limits self-injury and allows a rapid response to herbivory. It is not correct to imply that all plant defense compounds are stored exclusively in vacuoles or activated by the same mechanism.

The capacity to isolate unwanted material also contributes to detoxification. Certain metals can be sequestered with ligands, and some xenobiotics or their conjugates are transported into vacuoles. Accumulation can lower the cytosolic free concentration and reduce toxicity even if the total amount measured in the cell rises. This does not destroy the toxic element, nor does it guarantee unlimited tolerance. Transport capacity, energy cost, binding capacity and eventual leakage determine how effective sequestration can be. A useful experimental question is whether a tolerant cell has reduced total uptake or increased vacuolar partitioning; the two mechanisms can produce similar survival with quite different distributions of the substance.

8. Water balance, turgor and economical cell expansion

A large central vacuole and a mechanically resistant cell wall work together. When solutes accumulate within the protoplast, water tends to enter if the external water potential is greater than the internal water potential. As the vacuole enlarges, the protoplast presses outward on the wall. The wall resists that expansion and develops a balancing mechanical stress. The resulting positive hydrostatic pressure within the cell is called turgor pressure. Turgor is not generated by a molecular water pump in the tonoplast. It is a consequence of solute-dependent water movement acting against a resistant boundary.

The vacuole contributes a large fraction of cell water and many osmotically active solutes. Its volume can change much more than the volume of the cytoplasm during normal hydration and dehydration. Maintaining a large vacuolar compartment therefore allows a cell to grow without a proportional increase in protein-rich cytoplasm. Nevertheless, irreversible enlargement requires more than water uptake. The wall must yield or remodel in a controlled manner, and new tonoplast and plasma membrane must be supplied as the cell expands. Turgor is a driving force for expansion, not a complete explanation of cell growth.

At the scale of a cell, neglecting gravitational and matric terms for a simple dilute solution, water potential can be written Ψw = Ψs + Ψp. Solute potential Ψs is zero for pure water at the chosen reference state and becomes more negative as solute particle concentration rises. Hydrostatic pressure potential Ψp is positive for a turgid cell relative to the reference. Water flows from higher to lower water potential across a permeable boundary. At equilibrium, equal water potentials can coexist with unequal solute concentrations because the pressure term compensates.

A useful application is a cell in an external solution with Ψw = −0.20 MPa. If its effective intracellular solute potential is −0.70 MPa at equilibrium, Ψp = −0.20 − (−0.70) = +0.50 MPa. The pressure is positive even though the cell water potential is negative. Suppose the wall is removed and the resulting protoplast cannot sustain comparable hydrostatic pressure. The same osmotic difference may cause swelling until dilution reduces the gradient or membrane failure occurs. An isolated vacuole likewise lacks the wall's mechanical constraint. Its response cannot be equated directly with that of a whole plant cell.

For an ideal dilute solution, osmotic pressure π is approximately iCRT and Ψs ≈ −iCRT, with i the number of osmotically effective particles per solute formula unit under the assumed dissociation model. A 0.20 mol L⁻¹ non-dissociating solute at 298 K gives π ≈ 0.20 × 0.008314 × 298 = 0.496 MPa. The value of R here is expressed in L MPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. The sign for solute potential is negative, not positive. A 0.10 M ideal fully dissociated NaCl solution has approximately the same osmotic particle concentration, although real ionic solutions are nonideal. This numerical relationship helps a student understand why the identity and number of particles, not the mass of solute alone, affect water storage.

At the two internal membranes, plasma membrane and tonoplast, water potential differences may be transient while water redistributes. Over a sufficiently long time with both boundaries water-permeable, the relevant water chemical potentials approach equilibrium unless maintained flows continue. Treating the vacuolar solution as the entire cell is an approximation that may be useful for a large central vacuole but should not erase the cytoplasmic compartment. The rate of water movement is affected by membrane permeability, especially aquaporins; the final osmotic equilibrium in a simple closed system is governed by the thermodynamic conditions, not by the number of aquaporins.

9. Plasmolysis, deplasmolysis and a living-cell experiment

An accessible demonstration of the central vacuole's function is to observe a living plant epidermal cell in a solution of a nonpenetrating solute. In a sufficiently dilute external medium, the cell tends to be turgid, its protoplast lies close to the cell wall and the large vacuole occupies much of its interior. Increasing external solute concentration lowers the water potential outside the cell. If it becomes lower than that within the cell, water leaves the protoplast. The vacuole loses volume and turgor decreases.

At incipient plasmolysis, the turgor pressure is approximately zero and the protoplast is just beginning to separate from the wall. With a stronger hypertonic treatment, the protoplast retracts further while the wall largely retains its outline. This visible separation is called plasmolysis. The wall is not the semipermeable barrier responsible for water loss; it is the plasma membrane, together with water redistribution across the tonoplast, that governs the response. Connections to the wall may persist in some regions, and the retraction can be irregular rather than forming an ideal sphere.

If the cells remain viable, replacing the concentrated solution with a more dilute one reverses the water potential difference. Water can enter, the vacuole expands, and the protoplast moves back toward the wall. This reversal is called deplasmolysis. Prolonged exposure to an extreme solute concentration can injure membranes or metabolism, so reversibility should not be assumed for every treatment. A cell that fails to recover may have sustained damage, but other explanations, including inadequate equilibration, should be excluded before assigning a mechanism.

A classical use of plasmolysis is estimation of the effective osmotic concentration of cell sap. If an external nonpenetrating sucrose solution of 0.30 M produces incipient plasmolysis at 25 °C, a simplified equilibrium model gives Ψs ≈ −0.30 × 0.008314 × 298 = −0.743 MPa for that cell at incipient plasmolysis. The approximation assumes pressure is zero, the solute is effectively nonpenetrating, and the volume has equilibrated. It estimates the cell's osmotic condition at that stage, not necessarily its original turgid concentration. The cell may have lost water before incipient plasmolysis, concentrating its sap.

In a tissue, individual cells may reach incipient plasmolysis at different external concentrations. A class experiment may therefore count the proportion of cells showing visible plasmolysis after a standardized time. The concentration associated with about fifty percent of cells is a population estimate, not proof that every cell has the same solute potential. If the solute gradually crosses the plasma membrane, a time-dependent recovery can occur even without transferring to fresh water. Using a solute that is effectively nonpenetrating over the observation period and measuring at matched times improves interpretation.

The same reasoning distinguishes flaccidity from cell death. A flaccid cell has little turgor, but may still possess functional membranes and enzymes. An epidermal cell with a shrunken vacuole may recover on rehydration. Turgor loss does not necessarily mean irreversible injury. These distinctions explain why plants can wilt reversibly and why microscopy provides better evidence than a single measurement of cell size. Whole-plant transport and drought responses are broader topics; here the important point is the direct link between vacuole volume, water potential and wall-supported pressure.

10. Vacuolar adjustments during salt, nutrient and pH stress

The homeostatic role of a vacuole becomes especially apparent when conditions change. Consider a plant cell exposed to an increase in external NaCl. There are two related problems: the external water potential decreases, tending to draw water out, and excess cytosolic Na⁺ can interfere with cellular reactions and potassium-dependent processes. Moving part of the incoming Na⁺ into the vacuole through suitable transporters can help reduce cytosolic Na⁺ activity while contributing to osmotic adjustment. Potassium and compatible organic solutes must still support cytosolic function. Salt tolerance is therefore not simply a matter of increasing total sodium content.

Compartmentation gives a cell more options than either excluding all incoming ions or allowing them to remain in the cytosol. An intact tonoplast can maintain a high total vacuolar concentration with a lower cytosolic concentration. Consider two cells with equal total sodium: one has most sodium inside the vacuole, the other has most in the cytosol. Their total sodium assays are identical, but their biochemical stress can be very different. Conversely, a cell that excludes sodium at the plasma membrane may have less total sodium than a cell that tolerates it by sequestration. Distinguish these strategies when interpreting survival and ion measurements.

Changes in vacuolar pH can influence several downstream functions simultaneously. A reduced proton gradient can weaken proton-coupled solute uptake, alter hydrolase activity and change the retention of organic acids or pigments. The effects on cytosolic pH depend on other buffering and transport systems; the cytosol need not immediately acquire the same pH as the lumen. If a cell loses a single transporter, effects may be selective. For example, impaired nitrate transport may reduce vacuolar nitrate accumulation without an obvious primary acidification defect. A generalized proton-pump defect can affect many transport pathways because they share the same energy source.

Nutrient scarcity illustrates a complementary response. When a stored pool is needed, transport out of the vacuole must be stimulated or inward storage must decline. If a mutant accumulates an unusually large amino acid reserve but grows poorly during nitrogen starvation, defective mobilization is a possible interpretation. The cell may possess the resource but cannot access it. Measuring both the vacuolar pool and the cytosolic pool over time would discriminate between deficient accumulation and deficient release.

The vacuole is also a buffer of physical volume. Rapid water loss can be accommodated partly by vacuolar shrinkage, whereas replenishment restores volume. A sudden perturbation of the tonoplast can make these adjustments slower or more damaging. In guard cells, vacuolar solute and water changes contribute to the cell-volume and turgor shifts underlying stomatal movement. The direction of opening or closure depends on guard-cell mechanics and interactions with the tissue; the vacuole alone is not a stomatal motor. This is a useful link between organelle-level storage and a physiological process without replacing the detailed plant physiology treatment.

A crucial limit is that sequestration requires intact membrane selectivity and a sustained supply of energy. If the gradient collapses, solute leakage and altered transport can reduce the reserve and disturb the cytosol. If the vacuolar membrane ruptures, the normal distinction between sap and cytosol is lost. Rupture is not equivalent to the physiological opening of a regulated ion channel. The former removes a compartment boundary and can expose the cytosol to acids and hydrolytic enzymes; the latter alters the movement of selected species while the boundary remains intact.

11. Lytic vacuoles, controlled digestion and recycling

A lytic vacuole performs a function similar to that of an animal lysosome. Its lumen contains hydrolytic enzymes, including proteases, nucleases, glycosidases and phosphatases. These enzymes break macromolecules into smaller products using water. Many have acidic pH optima, which makes the acidified lumen favorable for digestion. Not every enzyme has the same optimum and not every vacuole has identical pH. The combination of selective delivery, enzyme activation and pH determines the organelle's degradative capacity.

A substrate may arrive from outside the cell through endocytosis or from within the cell through autophagic delivery. In macroautophagy, cytoplasmic material is enclosed by a double-membrane autophagosome whose outer membrane fuses with the vacuole. The inner membrane and its enclosed cargo then become available for breakdown in the lumen. This is distinct from the vacuole directly swallowing every cytosolic protein. Other forms of autophagic delivery also exist, including direct uptake of material by remodeling of the vacuolar membrane. Here the emphasis is on the vacuole as the final degradative compartment rather than on the detailed regulation of autophagy.

Digestion is useful only if the products can be reused or otherwise handled. Hydrolysis of proteins releases peptides and amino acids; nucleic acid degradation releases smaller nucleotide-derived components; phosphatases may release phosphate. Specific transporters then permit useful small products to leave the lumen. A defect in a protease and a defect in an amino acid exporter can both impair nutrient recycling, but their immediate consequences differ. In the first case undegraded protein accumulates; in the second case the protein may be digested normally but its products remain trapped. This distinction is a frequent pattern in mechanistic exam questions.

Consider a fluorescent protein whose signal is sensitive to acidification or proteolysis. If its fluorescence disappears after arrival in the vacuole, several interpretations are possible. It may have been degraded, its fluorescence may have been quenched at low pH, or its concentration may have fallen below detection. Evidence for digestion is stronger if disappearance is accompanied by appearance of identifiable degradation products and if a hydrolase-deficient condition preserves the intact substrate. Fluorescent microscopy is therefore powerful but not automatically a quantitative enzyme assay.

The acidic lumen also permits activation or processing of some enzyme precursors and storage protein precursors. Enzyme delivery, maturation and substrate digestion are different steps. For example, a vacuolar processing enzyme can cleave certain proteins into mature forms; failure of such cleavage is not necessarily the same as failure of all bulk degradation. The distinction between processing and digestion is whether the cleavage produces a functional product or primarily contributes to its breakdown and recycling.

Under controlled conditions the tonoplast protects cytosolic macromolecules from the luminal environment. Acidity is one level of protection, because many acid hydrolases are less active at near-neutral pH, but compartmentation is the main structural safeguard. It would be unsafe to claim that all vacuolar enzymes instantly become harmless when released into the cytosol. In some plant programmed cell death contexts, tonoplast rupture and release of hydrolytic contents contribute to cellular dismantling. The detailed regulation of cell death belongs elsewhere; the relevant point is that loss of the boundary can fundamentally change the outcome of otherwise useful digestive activity.

12. Fungal vacuoles: an integrated model of storage and degradation

The yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has one or more vacuolar compartments whose size and number depend on growth and environmental conditions. These are lysosome-like acidic organelles, but their roles extend beyond macromolecule digestion. They help store ions, amino acids and phosphate, regulate intracellular pH and respond to changes in osmotic conditions. Yeast offers a useful bridge between the large plant central vacuole and the smaller lysosomes of animal cells. Its vacuole demonstrates that one compartment can simultaneously have digestive and storage functions without the presence of a large photosynthetic plant cell.

The yeast V-ATPase establishes an acidic lumen that supports many vacuolar functions. Uptake of basic amino acids such as arginine and lysine into the vacuolar pool can reduce fluctuations in cytosolic amino acid concentration. During nutrient limitation, stored molecules may be mobilized and autophagic material degraded to support survival. This behavior is not equivalent to protein storage in a plant seed: the fungal vacuole often stores free amino acids as well as degrading macromolecules. The chemical form of the reserve matters because free amino acids can be exported without first requiring cleavage of a storage protein.

Phosphate storage illustrates another important principle. Yeast vacuoles accumulate inorganic polyphosphate, a polymer of phosphate residues joined by phosphoanhydride bonds. This allows the organelle to retain a substantial phosphorus reserve and can assist in binding positively charged ions. A polymer contributes fewer independent dissolved particles than the same number of fully separate monomers under a simple ideal model, although the true osmotic behavior depends on charge and counterions. Polymerization is thus one way to organize a reserve chemically; it does not make the phosphate disappear. When needed, breakdown of polyphosphate helps supply phosphate for other cellular processes.

The vacuole can also sequester metal ions and participate in detoxification. Yeast strains lacking a relevant transporter may accumulate excess metal in the cytosol, even if the organelle still contains normal hydrolases. Strains lacking V-ATPase function often display multiple phenotypes because lumen pH supports ion transport, enzyme processing and other functions. To distinguish a generalized pH defect from a specific storage defect, compare measured lumen pH, enzyme activity and distribution of the stored species. Growth alone cannot localize the cause.

Vacuole morphology changes during osmotic stress. In yeast, hyperosmotic exposure can promote fragmentation of the vacuole into smaller compartments; hypotonic conditions can favor fusion and enlargement, with the precise response dependent on conditions. Such changes reflect active membrane remodeling, not simply new organelles spontaneously appearing in the cytosol. Fusion can combine lumen and membrane, whereas fission divides them. A mutant that fails to fuse its vacuoles may have fragmented morphology despite normal acidification. Conversely, a normal-shaped vacuole can have a serious chemical defect. Structural and functional assays therefore complement rather than replace each other.

Yeast genetic studies are valuable because changing a single gene and measuring the resulting cargo localization or pH can distinguish alternative mechanisms. A sorting mutant may secrete a normally vacuolar enzyme, a protease mutant may retain undigested substrate in the correct compartment, and an acidification mutant may show poor activity of otherwise correctly delivered enzymes. These phenotypes recapitulate the three problems of membrane identity, chemical environment and catalytic function introduced earlier. They also help explain why discoveries made in yeast can inform the study of mammalian lysosomes without requiring every component to be identical.

13. Contractile vacuoles: removing excess water in freshwater protists

A freshwater protist such as Paramecium lives in an environment that is usually much more dilute than its cytoplasm. Water tends to enter through its surface membrane by osmosis. Unlike a typical plant cell, a wall-less protist cannot rely on a rigid cell wall to support substantial turgor. Continuous unregulated water entry would increase cell volume and could eventually damage the plasma membrane. Many freshwater protists solve this problem by collecting and periodically expelling excess water through a contractile vacuole complex.

The complex is not simply a hollow sac that periodically squeezes. In Paramecium, radial collecting channels feed a central reservoir associated with an organized membrane system. Other protists use differently shaped collecting tubules, vesicles or reservoirs. During the filling phase, fluid accumulates from the cytoplasm into the complex. During the discharge phase, the reservoir communicates transiently with the cell surface at an appropriate region and releases its contents. The cycle then begins again. Terms such as diastole and systole describe filling and emptying, respectively, but they do not mean that the structure contains muscle.

Water collection must be explained by the same physical principles used for the plant vacuole. In many studied contractile vacuole complexes, V-ATPase contributes to a proton gradient that powers ion accumulation in collecting membranes. Appropriate solutes generate an osmotic driving force for water entry, and water channels can facilitate movement. Some ions may subsequently be recovered rather than continually lost to the environment. The exact solutes, transport pathways and membrane dynamics vary among species. It is therefore more accurate to describe energy-dependent creation and regulation of a fluid-collecting compartment than to say that ATP directly pumps water.

The discharge phase is a mechanical and membrane-trafficking event. The reservoir must connect with the plasma membrane and empty its contents while the cell maintains its integrity. In different organisms, pressure, membrane remodeling, cytoskeletal interactions and fusion machinery contribute differently. The name contractile vacuole should not be interpreted as evidence for a universal actomyosin-powered contraction of the reservoir wall. Formation of a temporary discharge pathway and controlled changes of shape are sufficient to explain the basic function at this level.

The rate of the cycle depends on the osmotic challenge. If the external medium becomes more dilute, water entry often increases and the complex must increase its average output. Depending on the organism, the adjustment can involve frequency, discharged volume, or both. If the external medium becomes less dilute and approaches the cytosolic osmotic concentration, the need for water expulsion decreases. A contractile vacuole may discharge less frequently or become less conspicuous. Do not conclude that every marine protist lacks a contractile vacuole or that every freshwater protist has the same architecture; the distribution reflects evolutionary diversity, not an absolute habitat rule.

Food vacuoles are a different type of compartment in many protists. When a protist engulfs food particles, the resulting food-containing vacuole can mature and receive digestive enzymes. Nutrients released by digestion are taken into the cytoplasm, and undigested residue may eventually be discharged. A food vacuole is therefore primarily associated with nutrient acquisition and digestion; a contractile vacuole is primarily associated with water balance. The fact that both may appear as rounded membrane-bound structures in a microscope does not make their functions interchangeable. Time-lapse observation of periodic filling and discharge can help distinguish the osmoregulatory structure from a digestive one.

14. Measuring contractile vacuole output and predicting cell volume

The ability of a contractile vacuole to regulate volume can be analyzed using a simple balance equation. Let V be whole-cell volume, Jᵢₙ the net rate of water entry across the cell surface other than the vacuolar discharge, Q the volume expelled per discharge and f the discharge frequency. Averaging over many cycles, dV/dt = Jᵢₙ − fQ, provided other volume changes are negligible. Jᵢₙ and fQ must be expressed in the same units, such as cubic micrometers per minute. At steady average volume, Jᵢₙ = fQ. This does not imply that no water enters the cell: it means the average rates of gain and loss match.

Suppose a protist expels 30 µm³ per discharge and completes eight discharges per minute. Average output is 240 µm³ min⁻¹. If it has two independent contractile vacuoles, each with the same output, the total is 480 µm³ min⁻¹. A student must determine whether the observed frequency refers to one reservoir or the entire cell before doubling the result. If the environment changes and the measured output remains 240 µm³ min⁻¹ while net water entry becomes 360 µm³ min⁻¹, the initial average volume gain is 120 µm³ min⁻¹ in the model. After two minutes, the increase would be 240 µm³ if both rates remained unchanged. Real volume changes alter concentrations and may trigger regulation, so indefinite linear swelling cannot be assumed.

A second complication is that a full reservoir is not always emptied completely. For an approximately spherical vacuole of radius r, volume is 4πr³/3. If its radius immediately before discharge is 3 µm and after discharge is 1 µm, the net expelled volume is approximately 4π(27−1)/3 = 108.9 µm³. At five discharges per minute this gives 544.5 µm³ min⁻¹ using rounded values. Radius must be cubed; doubling radius changes a sphere's volume eightfold. Assuming the post-discharge radius is zero would overestimate expelled volume by about 4.19 µm³ per cycle. The result is valid only if spherical geometry is a reasonable approximation and changes in connected collecting channels are negligible.

Now consider an experiment that records discharge frequency in media of different osmolality. The frequency falls as external osmolality rises. This is consistent with reduced osmotic water entry, but frequency alone does not measure total water output. The vacuoles could expel larger amounts per event, maintaining nearly constant total output. Measuring both volume per event and frequency permits an estimate of the actual removal rate. Measuring whole-cell volume at the same time helps establish whether that rate is sufficient to preserve homeostasis.

If a treatment reduces discharge frequency while reservoir volume continues to increase, a discharge or fusion defect is a reasonable hypothesis. If the reservoir stays small and rarely discharges, fluid collection may instead be impaired. Both defects reduce output, but their different morphology helps localize the step. If an inhibitor also reduces cellular ATP and motility, broad cellular toxicity is an alternative explanation. A matched control and recovery after washout can help distinguish a specific functional defect from irreversible injury. The essential examination skill is to identify the variable measured, relate it to the balance equation and avoid treating a change in one observed feature as proof of an entire mechanism.

15. Experimental evidence and diagnosing tonoplast dysfunction

Vacuolar function can be studied by combining localization, biochemical measurements and physiological perturbations. Isolated vacuoles provide a relatively direct system for examining tonoplast transport. In a typical conceptual experiment, a membrane fraction is supplied with ATP or PPi on the correct side, and a pH-sensitive dye reports acidification of the enclosed compartment. If either energy source causes acidification under suitable conditions, two energy-coupled pathways may be present. Inhibiting one while testing the other helps distinguish them. The orientation and integrity of vesicles must be known because an inverted membrane may have the opposite apparent transport direction.

A pH-sensitive probe must be calibrated before the signal can be converted to pH. A single fluorescence intensity can change with probe amount, optical path length, photobleaching or sequestration. Ratiometric probes or calibration against known pH values help separate a real chemical change from a difference in probe concentration. Even after the pH is known, the free proton activity does not tell the full buffering capacity. To infer a transport rate, the experiment must relate the time-dependent pH signal to the volume and buffering of the compartment. For NET-style reasoning, the distinction between an endpoint measurement and a flux measurement is often more important than the technical details.

Suppose intact vacuoles are tested under three conditions, with the same cytosolic-side pH and ionic composition. ATP produces a marked fall in lumen pH; PPi produces a smaller fall; ATP plus a V-ATPase inhibitor produces little acidification, while PPi remains effective. The results are consistent with distinct ATP-dependent and PPi-dependent proton-pumping mechanisms. They do not establish the exact number of protons moved per ATP or PPi because final pH also reflects buffering and leak. A protonophore abolishing sustained acidification with either substrate would support the role of membrane impermeability in maintaining the gradient. This illustrative experiment is not a reported historical dataset.

A second conceptual experiment compares two plant cell lines in a salt treatment. The wild type retains more sodium in its vacuolar fraction and less in the cytosolic fraction. A mutant shows the opposite partitioning, but both cell lines contain similar total sodium. The mutant also shows reduced growth. The data support a defect in intracellular compartmentation as one possible explanation, rather than reduced initial sodium entry. If lumen pH is normal, a defect in a specific sodium transport or retention pathway becomes more plausible. If lumen pH is elevated, an upstream proton-pump defect is another hypothesis. Neither conclusion follows from the total sodium assay alone.

An enzyme localization experiment provides a third diagnostic route. Imagine a storage-protein precursor normally arrives in the vacuole and is cleaved there. In mutant A, the precursor appears in the extracellular medium. In mutant B, it accumulates inside the vacuole without being cleaved. A likely interpretation is mis-sorting in A and failed processing in B, with defective lumen pH or processing enzyme activity among the possible causes in B. The same phenotype of reduced mature vacuolar protein has different mechanistic origins. Measuring the precursor location and the compartment's acidity makes the distinction testable.

Finally, microscopy after a sudden osmotic change can distinguish altered water permeability from changed equilibrium conditions. A mutant with fewer functional tonoplast aquaporins may show slower vacuolar volume changes after an identical osmotic step, yet ultimately reach a similar volume if solute content and pressure are unchanged. A mutant deficient in solute accumulation may instead reach a different final volume because the driving forces differ. Measuring both the time course and the endpoint tests these alternatives. No single assay can establish every aspect of organelle function. Combining structure, transport, chemical environment and physiological outcome produces a stronger causal explanation.

16. Comparing plant vacuoles, fungal vacuoles, protist vacuoles and lysosomes

The central plant vacuole, fungal vacuole and animal lysosome share an important organizational principle: a single bilayer encloses a chemically distinct aqueous lumen, and membrane proteins regulate exchanges with the cytosol. Many of these compartments contain hydrolytic enzymes and rely on V-type ATPases to maintain acidity. This similarity helps explain why fungal vacuoles are often called lysosome-like and why plant lytic vacuoles can perform functions analogous to those of animal lysosomes. The shared organization does not imply that their sizes, transport proteins, sorting signals or primary cellular tasks are identical.

A typical animal lysosome is comparatively small and participates in degradation of endocytic and autophagic cargo. Animal cells commonly have numerous lysosomes and can mobilize products through specialized lysosomal transporters. It is incorrect to describe animal lysosomes as exclusively digestive with no involvement in storage, ion handling or nutrient sensing. However, most animal cells do not rely on a giant lysosome and a rigid wall to develop turgor for tissue support. The large central vacuole of a plant cell performs a distinctive mechanical and storage role in addition to any lytic role.

Fungal vacuoles combine strong degradative functions with nutrient and ion storage, as well as osmotic adaptation. A protein storage vacuole in a seed has a different prevailing role during maturation: it accumulates reserves to be used later. The contractile vacuole complex of a freshwater protist has a fundamentally different transport outcome. While the plant central vacuole may retain large volumes of water, the contractile vacuole periodically releases collected water to the exterior. The apparent contradiction disappears when the mechanical context is considered. The wall-supported plant cell benefits from retaining water for turgor; a wall-less freshwater protist benefits from expelling excess water to avoid uncontrolled swelling.

Peroxisomes, the subject of the following node, also have a single membrane but carry out a different class of chemistry. They contain oxidases and catalase associated with oxidative metabolism and hydrogen peroxide handling rather than serving principally as acidic hydrolytic compartments. A single membrane is insufficient to identify an organelle. A marker for catalase activity supports peroxisomal identity, while acid hydrolases and lumen acidification support a lytic compartment, with appropriate localization controls. A plastid or mitochondrion has a double envelope and its own genetic machinery; a vacuole normally lacks both characteristics. Gas vesicles of some prokaryotes, despite occasionally being called gas vacuoles, are protein-walled buoyancy structures rather than the lipid-bilayer organelles considered here.

The most reliable examination strategy is to begin with the cell type and the functional problem. Ask what the membrane encloses, which species must enter or leave, where the energy comes from, and what would happen if the boundary failed. The answers distinguish a proton-pumping defect from a water-channel defect, a digestive defect from a trafficking defect, and a storage organelle from a periodic water-discharge organelle. The vacuole is a dynamic participant in homeostasis precisely because its membrane couples a stable compartment identity with controlled exchange. This principle also prepares us to understand why the next organelle, the peroxisome, maintains a distinct chemical environment for oxidation.

17. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Choose the best answer before opening the explanation. These are original examination-style questions, not previous-year papers.

1. Which boundary directly separates plant vacuolar sap from cytosol?

A. Cell wall
B. Plasma membrane
C. Tonoplast
D. Nuclear envelope

Answer and reasoning: C. The tonoplast is the vacuolar bilayer. The wall is extracellular, the plasma membrane encloses the protoplast, and the nuclear envelope encloses nuclear contents.

2. Which energy source directly drives the plant vacuolar H⁺-PPase?

A. ATP
B. PPi
C. NADH
D. K⁺ gradient

Answer and reasoning: B. PPi hydrolysis drives this pump; ATP drives V-ATPase. NADH and potassium are not its direct energy substrates.

3. A TIP aquaporin increases primarily which property?

A. ATP synthesis
B. Water permeability
C. Cytosolic protein synthesis
D. Hydrostatic pressure independent of water movement

Answer and reasoning: B. A TIP facilitates passive water movement; it neither synthesizes ATP nor dictates pressure independently of osmotic conditions.

4. Which pair is correctly matched?

A. Anthocyanin–often vacuole
B. Chlorophyll–tonoplast
C. Catalase–typical lytic vacuole marker
D. Vacuolar DNA–ribosomes

Answer and reasoning: A. Anthocyanins are commonly stored in vacuoles. Chlorophyll is in chloroplast thylakoids, catalase supports peroxisome identification and vacuoles lack their own genome/ribosomes.

5. A yeast vacuole can store which nutrient form?

A. Only intact starch granules
B. Polyphosphate and basic amino acids
C. Only chlorophyll
D. Only free glucose

Answer and reasoning: B. Yeast vacuoles store polyphosphate and amino acids in addition to other solutes. The word only makes A, C and D especially inappropriate.

6. Which statement about lytic and protein storage vacuoles is most accurate?

A. Mutually exclusive permanent identities
B. Both contain their own chromosomes
C. Functional identities may change during development
D. Both lack membrane transporters

Answer and reasoning: C. Storage vacuoles can gain lytic function during germination. A is too absolute; B and D contradict basic compartment organization.

7. In a freshwater Paramecium, the principal role of the contractile vacuole is:

A. Water removal
B. Photosynthesis
C. ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation
D. DNA replication

Answer and reasoning: A. It periodically expels excess fluid. Photosynthesis, respiratory ATP generation and DNA replication are not its primary functions.

8. Which is the best description of a plant NHX-type exchanger?

A. A water pump
B. A proton-coupled cation antiporter
C. A DNA helicase
D. A constitutively outward ATP synthase

Answer and reasoning: B. It couples cation movement to opposite H⁺ movement. It is not a direct water pump, helicase or ATP synthase.

9. Assertion: A cell may contain more total sodium yet have lower cytosolic sodium activity. Reason: Sodium may be preferentially stored in the vacuole.

A. Both true, reason explains
B. Both true, reason does not explain
C. Assertion true, reason false
D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning: A. Compartmentation decouples whole-cell amount from cytosolic activity. The reason directly supplies the mechanism.

10. Assertion: A V-ATPase defect necessarily eliminates every plant vacuolar proton-pumping pathway. Reason: Some plant tonoplasts possess V-PPase.

A. Both true, reason explains
B. Both true, reason does not explain
C. Assertion true, reason false
D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning: D. V-PPase provides an alternative energy source for pumping protons. The defect can be serious without removing all acidification.

11. Assertion: A plant cell may become flaccid without dying. Reason: Loss of turgor may be reversed by water uptake if membranes remain functional.

A. Both true, reason explains
B. Both true, reason does not explain
C. Assertion true, reason false
D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning: A. Flaccidity is a mechanical condition rather than proof of irreversible damage. Rehydration can restore pressure.

12. Assertion: The contractile vacuole is a specialized food-digesting compartment. Reason: It periodically expels excess water.

A. Both true, reason explains
B. Both true, reason does not explain
C. Assertion true, reason false
D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning: D. Digestive food vacuoles and contractile vacuoles have different primary roles.

18. Part C — Mechanisms, data and calculations

Choose the best answer before opening the explanation. These are original examination-style questions, not previous-year papers.

1. A vacuole has pH 5.2 and cytosol pH 7.2. What is the ratio of free H⁺ activities?

A. 2
B. 10
C. 100
D. 0.01

Answer and reasoning: C. aᵥ/a꜀ = 10^(7.2−5.2) = 100. A incorrectly uses a linear pH difference; B is a one-unit difference; D reverses direction.

2. For pH 5.5 versus pH 7.0 and a lumen potential of +20 mV at 298 K, estimate the cost of moving one mole of H⁺ into the lumen.

A. −10.49 kJ
B. +6.63 kJ
C. +10.49 kJ
D. +1.93 kJ

Answer and reasoning: C. Chemical cost = RT ln(31.6) = 8.56 kJ; electrical cost = F(0.020) = 1.93 kJ. Both oppose entry. A reverses signs, B subtracts the electrical cost, and D ignores pH.

3. An ideal electroneutral one K⁺/one H⁺ antiporter operates with lumen pH 6 and cytosol pH 7. What K⁺ activity ratio v/c is its equilibrium limit?

A. 0.1
B. 1
C. 10
D. 100

Answer and reasoning: C. The proton ratio is 10, and the K⁺ ratio equals it at zero net ΔG. A reverses the gradient, B ignores it, and D assumes two pH units.

4. A cell equilibrates with external Ψw = −0.35 MPa. Internal Ψs = −0.90 MPa. What is its pressure potential?

A. −1.25 MPa
B. −0.55 MPa
C. +0.55 MPa
D. +1.25 MPa

Answer and reasoning: C. Ψp = Ψw − Ψs = −0.35 + 0.90 = +0.55 MPa. The alternatives reflect incorrect subtraction or signs.

5. At incipient plasmolysis in 0.25 M ideal sucrose at 298 K, estimate the cell's effective solute potential.

A. −0.619 MPa
B. +0.619 MPa
C. −6.19 MPa
D. 0 MPa

Answer and reasoning: A. Ψs = −CRT = −0.25 × 0.008314 × 298 ≈ −0.619 MPa. B reverses sign, C mis-scales units and D confuses zero pressure with zero solute potential.

6. A TIP-deficient plant cell takes twice as long to reach the same final volume as wild type after an osmotic step. Which interpretation is most directly supported?

A. Lower water permeability
B. Lower final osmotic equilibrium volume
C. Complete loss of cell wall
D. ATP-driven water transport

Answer and reasoning: A. The rate changes while the endpoint does not. B contradicts the observation. C and D are neither implied nor necessary.

7. A plant cell mutant accumulates vacuolar protein precursor in the lumen, but the mature cleaved product is absent. What is the most direct affected step?

A. ER entry
B. Vacuolar delivery
C. Processing after arrival
D. DNA replication

Answer and reasoning: C. Cargo has reached the lumen, so A and B are not the immediate defect. Processing enzyme function or lumen conditions are plausible. D is unrelated.

8. In isolated vacuoles ATP acidifies the lumen to pH 5.5, PPi to pH 6.0. A selective V-ATPase inhibitor blocks the ATP response but not PPi. Which conclusion follows?

A. PPi is a substrate of V-ATPase
B. Two distinguishable energy-dependent processes are supported
C. ATP moves water directly
D. The PPi pathway necessarily pumps fewer protons per substrate molecule

Answer and reasoning: B. Differential inhibitor sensitivity supports distinct pathways. A contradicts selectivity; C confuses solute and water transport; D cannot be inferred from final pH without flux and buffering measurements.

9. Wild type and mutant each contain 100 arbitrary units of sodium; 80 are vacuolar in wild type, 30 in mutant. Assuming only vacuolar and cytosolic pools, compare cytosolic amounts.

A. 20 and 70
B. 80 and 30
C. 100 and 100
D. 20 and 30

Answer and reasoning: A. Subtraction gives 20 and 70; the mutant contains 3.5 times the cytosolic amount. B lists the vacuolar amounts, C confuses total and pool, and D mixes compartments. Amount alone does not specify concentration without volume.

10. A contractile vacuole discharges 25 µm³ eight times per minute. What is average fluid removal?

A. 3.125 µm³/min
B. 33 µm³/min
C. 200 µm³/min
D. 200 µm³/s

Answer and reasoning: C. Qf = 25 × 8 = 200 µm³/min. A divides, B adds unlike quantities, and D uses the wrong time unit.

11. A spherical reservoir changes radius from 4 µm to 2 µm at discharge. Estimate net expelled volume.

A. 8.38 µm³
B. 234.6 µm³
C. 268.1 µm³
D. 56 µm³

Answer and reasoning: B. 4π(4³−2³)/3 = 4π(56)/3 ≈ 234.6 µm³. A uses a radius difference, C neglects residual volume, D omits the geometric factor.

12. In a dilute medium net water entry is 300 µm³/min, vacuolar output is 220 µm³/min. Initial average volume change is:

A. −80 µm³/min
B. 0
C. +80 µm³/min
D. +520 µm³/min

Answer and reasoning: C. dV/dt = 300−220 = +80 µm³/min. A reverses gain and loss, B assumes steady state, D adds them. This is an initial rate, not an indefinite prediction.

13. In medium X a protist expels 50 µm³ four times/min; in medium Y, 20 µm³ ten times/min. Which output is larger?

A. X
B. Y
C. Equal
D. Impossible with these measurements

Answer and reasoning: C. Both give 200 µm³/min. Frequency alone would incorrectly favor Y; volume per event alone would incorrectly favor X. Net cell volume also depends on water entry.

14. A contractile vacuole remains enlarged for long periods while discharge events nearly disappear. Which step is most plausibly defective?

A. Initial collection only
B. Outlet formation or discharge
C. Photosynthesis
D. Nutrient polymerization

Answer and reasoning: B. Reservoir filling persists, suggesting impaired emptying. A predicts a small or poorly filled reservoir. C and D do not explain the observed cycle. A direct discharge measurement could test the inference.

15. Yeast cells digest vacuolar protein normally but free amino acids accumulate inside the vacuole during starvation. What defect best explains this?

A. Hydrolase targeting
B. Amino acid mobilization/export
C. Formation of peptide bonds in the lumen
D. Absence of a vacuolar boundary

Answer and reasoning: B. Normal digestion with retained products points to impaired exit. A would compromise digestion, C does not explain retained free amino acids, and D contradicts selective retention.

16. A mutant's vacuolar pH rises from 5 to 6. Its pigment hue changes, proton-coupled uptake declines, and hydrolase activity falls. Which hypothesis unifies these observations?

A. A specific anthocyanin biosynthetic defect alone
B. Reduced acidification or increased proton leak
C. Increased lumen H⁺ activity
D. Increased chloroplast number

Answer and reasoning: B. A tenfold decline in free proton activity can affect pH-sensitive pigment state, secondary transport and acid hydrolases. A cannot readily explain all three, C reverses the pH relation, and D is unrelated.

17. A 10 pL vacuole contains 100 mM nitrate. A 2 pL cytosol contains 10 mM nitrate. Estimate the fraction of the combined nitrate amount in the vacuole.

A. 50%
B. 83.3%
C. 90.9%
D. 98.0%

Answer and reasoning: D. Amount is concentration × volume: relative amounts 1000 and 20 mM·pL, so 1000/1020 ≈ 98.0%. A ignores differences, B considers volume alone, C considers concentration alone.
