1. The distribution problem: why pumping is not enough

The heart provides the energy that drives blood through the body, but an efficient pump alone does not guarantee that each tissue receives the flow it requires. A resting skeletal muscle has a relatively small requirement for oxygen compared with the same muscle during contraction. The brain needs a fairly continuous supply even while the animal is sleeping. Skin blood flow can change markedly as heat exchange requirements change. These organs share a common arterial supply, yet their requirements need not change together. A vascular network solves this distribution problem by placing adjustable resistances between the common supply and the individual exchange regions.

Perfusion is the supply of blood to a tissue. It is often expressed per unit mass of tissue, such as millilitres per minute per 100 grams, so that organs of different size can be compared. Flow through an entire organ, in millilitres per minute, is a different quantity. A large tissue may receive more total blood than a small organ while receiving less per gram. Moreover, the delivery of a substance depends on its concentration as well as on blood flow. If arterial oxygen content is 0.20 mL oxygen per mL blood, a flow of 100 mL/min delivers 20 mL oxygen/min to that region. The amount actually taken up is smaller unless extraction is complete. The distinction between delivery and uptake connects this chapter to the Fick principle in the previous node.

The simplest representation of a mammalian systemic circulation is a pump followed by large conducting arteries, smaller distributing arteries, arterioles, capillary networks, venules and veins. These vessel categories do not merely describe size. Each category specialises in a different combination of conduction, pressure buffering, resistance adjustment, exchange or storage. The structure of a vessel is best understood by asking which of these tasks dominates there. For example, the thin wall of a capillary reduces the distance between plasma and tissue fluid, whereas the relatively muscular wall of an arteriole permits active adjustment of lumen size.

A complete circulation has no permanent upstream and downstream reservoirs that remain independent of one another. Blood leaving the systemic circulation returns to the right heart and passes through pulmonary vessels before it returns to the left heart. At steady state, the total flow through the systemic circuit equals the flow through the pulmonary circuit. The individual organ flows in the systemic circuit need not equal one another, because the organs are largely connected in parallel. Distinguishing these two levels of organisation is essential when a problem asks about a change in one organ rather than a change in the entire circulation.

Blood pressure is a mechanical variable, not a direct measure of nutrition or oxygen delivery. A pressure recorded in the aorta provides information about the driving conditions for systemic blood flow, but it cannot reveal every local resistance or every capillary exchange area. An organ whose supplying artery is constricted can be underperfused even when the central pressure is high. Conversely, a tissue can maintain its flow during a modest fall in arterial pressure if it reduces its resistance. The later chapters explain the regulation of pressure; the present chapter establishes the physical and structural reasons that make such regulation necessary.

A useful way to develop the distribution argument is to distinguish the circulatory variables that apply to the animal as a whole from those that apply to a particular organ. Cardiac output is a whole-system rate, whereas renal blood flow, muscle blood flow and cutaneous blood flow are component rates. The heart cannot assign a separate stroke volume to every organ. Instead, the pressure developed in the central arterial system acts at the origins of a set of branching pathways. The resistance of each pathway determines the fraction of the available flow that it receives under the prevailing pressure conditions. These fractions are not anatomical constants. They can change as the physiological state changes, which is an important property of the closed circulation.

One might imagine that the ideal distribution would always give the greatest possible flow to every organ. This is not physiologically realistic. Generating flow requires mechanical work, the total volume of circulating blood is finite, and every tissue does not require maximal delivery at the same time. A vascular system must accommodate changes in demand without necessarily increasing pump work in direct proportion to the number of organs involved. Control of local resistance permits prioritisation of perfusion, while the compliant vascular compartments help match the transient imbalance between supply and return. A successful adjustment in one tissue must therefore be examined in relation to the remainder of the circulation.

When interpreting the consequences of a low tissue blood flow, consider the reason that the tissue needs blood. For an actively contracting muscle, oxygen demand depends on the rate of metabolic activity. An endocrine organ may need flow both to supply its own metabolism and to carry secreted hormones away. A kidney receives a substantial flow for filtration and transport functions in addition to satisfying its own metabolic requirements. For the skin, thermal exchange may be a dominant determinant of flow under particular environmental conditions. A single relationship between tissue mass and normal flow is therefore inadequate for all organs. This is why a comparison of perfusion per unit mass should be interpreted in the light of function.

There is also a distinction between the instantaneous rate of perfusion and its time-averaged value. In many vessels the instantaneous flow oscillates during each heartbeat. If the circulation is examined over a sufficiently long interval during a stable physiological state, average inflow and outflow from a region can be compared. If its blood volume is changing, however, the instantaneous inflow and outflow need not be equal. For example, a compliant vein can receive more volume than it releases for a short interval. Conservation requires that the difference appear as an increase in its contained volume. The concept is essential for understanding both pressure buffering by arteries and volume storage by veins.

This reasoning also identifies a necessary distinction between circulating blood volume and the amount of blood that passes through a tissue over time. A small blood volume may be circulated repeatedly. A flow of five litres per minute does not mean that five additional litres have been produced by the heart during each minute. The volume describes a stock, while flow describes a rate of transfer. Multiplying a flow by an elapsed time estimates the total volume crossing the chosen boundary over that interval. In a closed circuit the same molecules may cross it repeatedly. Keeping track of this distinction prevents dimensional errors in later calculations and connects vascular physics directly to conservation of matter.

2. Vessel structure and functional division

The innermost cellular lining of a blood vessel is the endothelium. Endothelial cells separate the circulating blood from the vessel wall, provide a selectively permeable interface and participate in the regulation of vascular tone. In arteries and veins the endothelium belongs to the tunica intima. The tunica media contains smooth muscle cells and extracellular matrix, including elastic material in proportions that differ with vessel type. The outer tunica adventitia consists chiefly of supportive connective tissue and may contain nerves and small vessels that nourish the walls of large vessels. The arrangement permits the lumen to remain continuous while mechanical properties vary along the vascular tree.

Elastic arteries, such as the aorta and its major proximal branches, contain abundant elastic lamellae. Their wall stretches as blood is ejected during systole and recoils between ejections. This behaviour helps transfer part of the energy of ventricular contraction to the arterial wall and then to blood moving during diastole. Collagen fibres limit excessive distension at higher wall strains. Therefore, arterial pressure and arterial volume do not usually have a perfectly linear relationship over a wide pressure range. The relevant mechanical behaviour at a particular pressure is the local slope of that relationship.

Muscular arteries distribute blood towards particular organs. Their media contains substantial smooth muscle that can alter lumen diameter, although their aggregate resistance under normal circumstances is generally smaller than that of the smaller resistance vessels. An arteriole has a much narrower lumen and smooth muscle arranged around it. A modest shortening of its smooth muscle can greatly decrease lumen radius. Because of the steep dependence of resistance on radius, arterioles are especially effective sites for controlling the pressure transmitted to downstream capillaries and the flow entering them.

Capillaries are specialised exchange vessels. A typical capillary wall consists of endothelial cells supported by a basal lamina, with pericytes associated with many capillary types. Unlike a muscular artery, it does not have a continuous multilayered smooth muscle coat. The absence of such a coat facilitates short diffusion distances. Nevertheless, capillaries are not passive holes through which everything in the plasma freely passes. The endothelial barrier, cell junctions, surface glycocalyx and tissue-specific structures determine how easily different substances move.

Small venules receive blood from capillaries. Postcapillary venules are important sites of leukocyte exit into tissues during inflammation. Larger veins have smooth muscle and connective tissue in their walls, but they normally operate at much lower internal pressures than the large systemic arteries. They can contain a large volume of blood and accommodate changes in blood volume with comparatively small changes in pressure. Many limb veins contain valves that limit backward flow; valves are not universal in the venous system.

Arteries and veins are classified according to the direction of blood flow relative to the heart rather than oxygen content. Pulmonary arteries carry blood from the right ventricle towards the lungs, and pulmonary veins return it to the left atrium. This exception to the familiar oxygen-rich systemic artery pattern is an important reminder that naming a vessel and describing its physiological contents are separate tasks. The same principle applies when examining comparative circulation in nonmammalian vertebrates.

The layers of a vessel wall have different mechanical roles that can be understood by considering a ring cut across a large artery. Blood pressure acts outward on the wall, stretching its circumferential components. If elastic structures are intact, they tend to return the ring towards its less distended state after the pressure decreases. The smooth muscle cells can also develop active tension, changing the equilibrium between the distending force and the forces generated by the wall. Collagen provides a strong passive restraint, especially at relatively large extensions. The vessel diameter observed at a given pressure therefore reflects both the mechanical properties of the tissue and the activity of its cells.

A simplified wall mechanics relation helps explain why vessel dimensions matter. For a thin-walled cylindrical vessel, circumferential wall tension per unit axial length T is approximately Ptm r, where Ptm is the transmural pressure and r is the lumen radius. To see its origin, consider a longitudinal plane dividing a cylinder into halves. The pressure acts over the projected rectangular area of one half, and the circumferential tension at the two cut edges balances the separating force. The factors of two cancel, leaving the product of pressure and radius. If the wall has thickness h, a simple estimate of average circumferential stress is Ptm r/h. The first quantity is a force per length; the second is a force per area. The model assumes a thin uniform wall, neglects axial effects and is not an exact description of a thick artery.

Imagine two ideal vessels exposed to the same transmural pressure, one having twice the radius of the other and the same wall thickness. In this simple approximation, the larger vessel experiences twice the circumferential tension and stress. A thicker wall reduces stress for a given tension, although it does not eliminate the force created by pressure. Large arteries thus need sufficient mechanical support despite their substantial elastic content. A capillary does not require the same wall organisation because its radius is far smaller and its pressure is generally lower. This is one way to connect vessel structure to mechanical requirements without assuming that wall thickness by itself determines hydraulic resistance.

The endothelial lining also influences vessel function beyond serving as a physical boundary. Blood moving along the vessel exerts tangential traction on the endothelial surface. Endothelial cells can respond to these mechanical conditions by changing signalling activity. Some of the resulting mediators act on the underlying smooth muscle, modifying lumen radius. The cellular regulation of tone is developed in node 7.B.08, but its mechanical significance belongs here: the geometry governing hydraulic resistance is partly determined by a biological response to the fluid flowing through it. This provides a feedback route between haemodynamics and vessel behaviour.

Vessel preparation experiments help distinguish passive mechanical behaviour from active contraction. A vessel segment can be exposed to controlled distending pressures and its diameter measured at each pressure. Under conditions that minimise active smooth muscle contraction, the resulting curve gives information about passive wall mechanics. Under conditions that permit tone, the diameter at the same pressure can be smaller. A pressure-diameter relation obtained without knowing the activation state cannot establish which part of the change came from extracellular matrix mechanics and which came from muscle. The correct experimental comparison controls pressure, temperature, activation and vessel geometry rather than comparing unrelated segments at arbitrary conditions.

3. Pressure gradients, flow and resistance

Pressure is force per unit area. In a liquid it acts on the boundary in contact with the liquid, but a pressure difference along a vessel is needed to drive sustained flow against frictional resistance. Imagine two horizontal tanks connected by a narrow tube. If the pressure at each end of the tube is identical, there is no net flow through the tube after transient movements have ceased. Raising the pressure at the first end while keeping the second unchanged produces a flow towards the second. The tube does not have to widen for this to occur.

The basic haemodynamic relationship is Q = ΔP/R, where Q is the volume flow per unit time, ΔP = P1 − P2 is the driving pressure difference and R is the hydraulic resistance. If Q is expressed in mL/min and ΔP in mmHg, the corresponding resistance unit is mmHg·min/mL. Resistance is defined by the ratio of pressure difference to flow at the relevant operating conditions. A linear relationship is obtained when R does not change with Q or pressure. Real vessels can change diameter, and real blood can change its apparent viscosity, so a measured resistance can depend on the conditions under which it was measured.

A tissue receives blood at an arterial pressure of 85 mmHg and drains at a venous pressure of 5 mmHg. If its resistance is 0.8 mmHg·min/mL, the calculated flow is 80/0.8 = 100 mL/min. Raising venous pressure to 25 mmHg with arterial pressure and resistance held constant gives a driving pressure of 60 mmHg and a flow of 75 mL/min. This result demonstrates why a high upstream pressure does not necessarily ensure adequate perfusion. The pressure at the outlet is part of the problem.

In a vertical blood column, gravitational potential energy must also be considered. A static column of fluid has a hydrostatic pressure difference ρgh, where ρ denotes fluid density in kg/m³, g is gravitational acceleration in m/s² and h is vertical height in metres. Blood of density approximately 1060 kg/m³ produces a pressure difference of about 1040 Pa for a height difference of 0.10 m, or about 7.8 mmHg using 133.3 Pa per mmHg. A pressure measurement below the level of the heart in a standing person therefore differs from one at heart level partly because of the intervening blood column. In a static fluid no flow occurs despite the pressure difference, because gravitational potential offsets the pressure contribution. For flow between different elevations, the relevant mechanical energy includes both pressure and elevation.

Transmural pressure is different from the pressure difference along a vessel. It is the difference between lumen pressure and surrounding pressure at a particular location. Positive transmural pressure tends to distend a vessel, while a sufficiently low or negative transmural pressure can permit a compliant vessel to narrow or collapse. This is particularly important for thin-walled veins. A change in surrounding tissue pressure can affect flow by changing the geometry even if the two endpoint pressures have not changed. Thus a vascular resistance is not necessarily an independent fixed component.

The pressure-flow relationship can be extended to an energy argument. When a fluid volume ΔV moves from a region of pressure P1 to one of pressure P2 at the same elevation, the pressure difference can perform mechanical work approximately equal to ΔP multiplied by ΔV. Dividing by the time gives hydraulic power ΔP Q. In SI units pressure is in pascals, equivalent to newtons per square metre, and flow is in cubic metres per second. Their product is newton metres per second, or watts. The result represents the rate of pressure work delivered across the selected segment. In a simple steady resistive system, it is converted to heat through viscous dissipation.

An illustrative systemic pressure difference of 90 mmHg is approximately 12,000 Pa. A cardiac output of 5 L/min equals 8.33 × 10⁻⁵ m³/s. The pressure power corresponding to these two quantities is about one watt. This is not the total metabolic energy expenditure of the heart; it omits the energetic costs of excitation, contraction, internal mechanical work and inefficiencies, as well as contributions from pulsatile motion and other details. It is an approximate hydraulic output under a simplified steady representation. The calculation demonstrates that the heart continually supplies energy even when the total blood volume is unchanged.

Using Q = ΔP/R, the power expression can also be written Q²R or ΔP²/R. These forms provide useful contrasting predictions. If resistance doubles and a pump maintains the same flow, the required pressure difference doubles and the hydraulic power delivered across that resistance doubles. If the pressure difference is held fixed instead, the flow falls to half and hydraulic power delivered across that component also falls to half. The fact that increased resistance can have either effect on power is not a contradiction. The operating condition is different. For a living organism, neither pressure nor output is necessarily fixed; the heart and vascular network determine them jointly.

It is tempting to describe pressure as something consumed by blood cells as they move along a vessel. This wording can create confusion. Pressure is a mechanical state variable describing forces transmitted through the fluid. Mechanical energy is dissipated as fluid flows against viscous resistance. A cell arriving at a lower-pressure region has not necessarily lost oxygen, nor does a change in pressure quantify metabolic exchange. The two types of energy transfer occur simultaneously in the circulation but obey different physical relationships. Pressure measurements therefore cannot replace measurements of oxygen concentration, uptake or metabolism.

Experimental determination of resistance needs care when pressure and flow vary. If a pump is used to impose several flows through a rigid test segment and endpoint pressures are measured, a plot of ΔP versus Q gives an approximate resistance from its slope when the relation is linear. In an actively contractile vessel, the slope may change with the imposed conditions. A ratio ΔP/Q at one operating point and the incremental slope d(ΔP)/dQ near that point can differ. A student interpreting a curved pressure-flow graph should state which measure is being compared. Extrapolating a slope measured at one pressure to the entire operating range may predict an incorrect flow.

The same approach clarifies why a pressure reading made with a stationary liquid-filled apparatus must be referred to a defined level. If the transducer is positioned below the measurement site, the connecting fluid column adds hydrostatic pressure to the recorded signal. A reading corrected to the vessel level is required for comparison with readings taken elsewhere. This is a physical correction, distinct from any biological reflex response. The clinical measurement techniques and neural responses to posture will be treated in the next node, where these principles will help separate measurement effects from genuine physiological changes.

4. Ideal tube flow and Poiseuille's law

A quantitative understanding of radius dependence begins with a simple fluid model. Consider a straight rigid circular tube of length L and internal radius a containing an incompressible Newtonian fluid of dynamic viscosity η. The fluid flows steadily, with no slip at the stationary tube wall. A Newtonian fluid has a shear stress proportional to the shear rate. The pressure difference ΔP is positive from inlet to outlet and the velocity profile is fully developed, so the axial velocity depends on radial position but not on the axial position.

To obtain the velocity profile, imagine an inner cylinder of fluid of radius s and length L. Its two ends experience a net pressure force πs²ΔP. The viscous shear force on its curved boundary balances that force at steady state. The magnitude of the shear stress at radius s is therefore ΔP·s/(2L). The shear stress is zero at the centre and largest at the wall. Since fluid speed decreases outwards, the Newtonian relation can be written −η du/ds = ΔP·s/(2L). Integration from the wall, where u(a) = 0, gives u(s) = ΔP(a² − s²)/(4ηL). The profile is parabolic: fluid at the centre moves most rapidly, and the idealised fluid immediately touching the wall is stationary.

The flow through a thin annulus of radius s and thickness ds is u(s)·2πs ds. Integrating over all annuli from zero to a yields Q = πa⁴ΔP/(8ηL). The hydraulic resistance for this model is R = 8ηL/(πa⁴). In SI units η is measured in Pa·s, L and a in metres, ΔP in Pa, Q in m³/s, and R in Pa·s/m³. The fourth power arises from the combination of greater cross-sectional area and reduced influence of wall friction when radius increases. It does not imply that the centreline velocity itself has a fourth-power dependence: at fixed ΔP, L and η, the centreline velocity is proportional to a².

A vessel whose radius is reduced to 80% of its original value has an ideal resistance ratio of 1/(0.8⁴) = 2.4414. If the driving pressure remains fixed, the flow becomes 0.4096 of its previous value. If instead the flow is actively maintained by increasing the driving pressure, the required pressure difference becomes 2.4414 times the original. These are different experimental boundary conditions. A common error in examination problems is to apply the fixed-pressure flow prediction to a situation in which flow was explicitly held constant.

For a worked dimensional example, take a tube of radius 0.50 mm and length 20 mm containing a Newtonian fluid of viscosity 0.004 Pa·s. With ΔP = 400 Pa, use a = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ m and L = 0.020 m. The radius to the fourth power is 6.25 × 10⁻¹⁴ m⁴. Substitution gives Q ≈ 1.227 × 10⁻⁷ m³/s, equivalent to about 7.36 mL/min. This is a hypothetical rigid-tube calculation, not a measurement from an actual vessel. A real blood vessel may have an elastic wall, branch, carry pulsatile blood or contain cells whose influence on viscosity changes with diameter.

Poiseuille's relationship is nevertheless a powerful reasoning tool because arterioles can modify radius quickly without changing their length substantially. Smooth muscle contraction does not remove blood from the circulation; it changes the conditions under which blood is delivered. The radius dependence illustrates why narrowing at one segment can redistribute perfusion throughout a network. The actual magnitude depends on how much of the original total resistance was located in that segment.

The fourth-power relationship can be explored further by examining several independent modifications of the ideal tube. If length doubles while radius and viscosity remain fixed, each unit length contributes the same resistance and the total resistance doubles. If viscosity doubles, the shear stress required to maintain a given velocity gradient also doubles, leading to a doubling of resistance. Changing radius has a much stronger effect for the same fractional change, but this comparison assumes that the other variables are independent. In a biological vessel, changing the radius may change the effective viscosity, and changing pressure may change the radius itself.

A useful small-change approximation follows by taking logarithms of the ideal resistance expression. The fractional change in resistance for small independent changes is approximately the fractional change in viscosity plus the fractional change in length minus four times the fractional change in radius. Thus a 1% increase in radius predicts approximately a 4% decrease in resistance if the other quantities stay fixed. For large changes the exact fourth-power relation should be used instead. A 25% increase in radius gives an exact resistance ratio of 1/1.25⁴, or about 0.4096, rather than a reduction of 100%. The linear approximation is a local sensitivity estimate, not a formula for large perturbations.

The parabolic profile also permits calculation of wall shear stress. As derived earlier, the magnitude of the shear stress at the wall is ΔP a/(2L). Combining this with Poiseuille's expression gives τw = 4ηQ/(πa³). At fixed flow and viscosity, a smaller radius therefore produces a larger wall shear stress. At fixed pressure difference and length, the original expression shows that a smaller radius produces a smaller wall shear stress because the flow falls sharply. Both are idealised results under different constraints. Endothelial responses to flow cannot be predicted correctly by saying that every narrower vessel automatically has a higher shear stress. The boundary conditions determine the predicted magnitude.

Consider a hypothetical arteriole whose initial resistance accounts for one quarter of the total resistance of an organ bed, with all other resistance represented by a fixed component. If that arteriole doubles its own ideal resistance, the total resistance rises from four arbitrary units to five, rather than doubling. The predicted organ flow at fixed driving pressure becomes four fifths of the original. In contrast, if the same resistance-changing segment initially accounted for three quarters of the total, doubling it would raise total resistance from four units to seven, reducing flow to four sevenths. The whole-organ effect is therefore strongly dependent on the distribution of resistance before the intervention.

A simple experimental demonstration of the radius relationship uses tubes with known internal radii and equal lengths. A constant pressure difference is applied to each, the discharged fluid volume over a measured interval is recorded, and the experiment is repeated with the same Newtonian fluid at controlled temperature. Plotting logarithm of flow against logarithm of radius should yield a slope near four if the ideal assumptions are satisfied. A slope substantially different from four could indicate measurement error in radius, entrance effects, nonlaminar conditions or changes in fluid properties. The slope alone cannot identify which assumption failed.

This experiment also illustrates the importance of radius measurement. A small fractional error in radius creates a roughly fourfold larger fractional error in a resistance predicted using the fourth power. If two nominally identical tubes have small but unmeasured differences in their internal dimensions, attributing their flow difference to fluid viscosity could be misleading. An exam question based on such a dataset should be approached by considering the measurement uncertainty as well as the biological hypothesis. The goal of a model is to organise observations and test assumptions, rather than to force every observation to obey an ideal equation.

5. Series and parallel vascular networks

A single organ contains successive vessel segments as well as numerous parallel branches. The ideal resistance relationships follow from conservation of volume and the pressure-flow equation. In a series arrangement the same steady flow Q traverses each segment, provided no flow branches away and there is no net storage. If the segment resistances are R1 and R2, their pressure drops are QR1 and QR2. The total pressure drop is Q(R1 + R2), so the equivalent resistance is their sum. A series component with large resistance produces a large fraction of the pressure fall.

Suppose the resistance of a proximal artery is 1 unit, that of its arteriolar region is 4 units and that of the downstream region is 2 units. A driving pressure of 70 units produces a flow of 10 units, and the pressure drops in the three regions are 10, 40 and 20 units respectively. If the arteriolar resistance doubles to 8 units, the total becomes 11 and the flow becomes 70/11 ≈ 6.36. The arteriolar pressure drop becomes about 50.9, not 80, because the flow has also changed. The downstream pressure decreases if outlet pressure is fixed. This reasoning explains how a resistance vessel can protect the exchange bed from high upstream pressure at the cost of reducing flow.

In a parallel network each branch connects to common upstream and downstream pressure nodes. The pressure difference is the same across the branches, whereas the flows add. The conductance of a branch is G = 1/R, and the equivalent conductance is the sum of the individual conductances. Thus 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + … . Two equal branches of resistance R have equivalent resistance R/2. Opening an additional route increases the equivalent conductance even though the additional route itself introduces another physical segment containing friction.

Imagine two tissue beds with resistances of 2 and 4 mmHg·min/mL, supplied by a pressure difference of 60 mmHg. The branch flows are 30 and 15 mL/min and the total is 45 mL/min. Their equivalent resistance is 60/45 = 4/3 mmHg·min/mL. If the first bed dilates so its resistance becomes 1, its flow rises to 60 mL/min, while the second bed stays at 15 mL/min under the specified fixed pressures. The new equivalent resistance is 0.8. If the pump were instead unable to increase total flow, the common pressure difference would change. It is therefore unsafe to assume both constant cardiac output and constant arterial pressure unless the problem explicitly supplies a compensating mechanism.

Individual capillaries have very small radii, but a tissue contains many routes in parallel. Consequently, the resistance of an entire capillary bed cannot be inferred from the resistance of one capillary. With N identical capillaries in parallel, each having resistance Rc, the equivalent resistance is Rc/N. Recruitment of perfused capillary pathways, where it occurs, may increase exchange surface area and alter the distribution of red cells even if the geometry of each pathway is unchanged. Physiological microvascular networks are heterogeneous and their vessels have different lengths, diameters and local haematocrits; the equal-resistance model is useful only as a first approximation.

Resistance networks become particularly informative when pressure at an intermediate node is of interest. Let an upstream resistance Ra connect a supply pressure Pa to an exchange region at pressure Pc. Let a downstream resistance Rv connect that exchange region to an outlet pressure Pv. At steady state, without appreciable volume loss between the two resistances, Q = (Pa − Pv)/(Ra + Rv), and Pc = Pv + QRv. Substitution gives Pc = (PaRv + PvRa)/(Ra + Rv). The intermediate pressure is a resistance-weighted combination of the endpoint pressures. Notice that the upstream pressure is weighted by the downstream resistance. This is because a large downstream resistance requires a substantial pressure upstream of it to drive flow.

For a hypothetical system with Pa = 100 mmHg, Pv = 10 mmHg, Ra = 3 and Rv = 1 mmHg·min/mL, total flow is 22.5 mL/min and Pc is 32.5 mmHg. Doubling Ra to 6 while keeping all other quantities fixed gives Q = 90/7, about 12.86 mL/min, and Pc about 22.86 mmHg. Doubling Rv instead, starting from the original system, gives Q = 90/5 = 18 mL/min and Pc = 46 mmHg. Both changes reduce flow, but they move the exchange-region pressure in opposite directions. This is a foundational prediction for reasoning about capillary filtration and the effects of vascular constriction.

A more complex parallel model can explain redistribution when there is a shared upstream resistance. Suppose a source pressure drives blood through a proximal conduit before the flow divides between two tissue beds. Dilation in the first bed reduces the equivalent resistance of the parallel portion. The total flow through the common conduit rises if source and outlet pressures remain fixed, producing a larger pressure drop across the common conduit. The pressure available to both beds can therefore fall. Under those conditions, the second bed may receive less flow even though its own resistance has not changed. This differs from the ideal comparison in which the pressures immediately at the branch points are held fixed.

For a numerical illustration, let the source-to-outlet pressure difference be 100 units and the common upstream resistance be 1 unit. Initially the two bed resistances are each 4 units, giving a parallel equivalent of 2. The total resistance is 3, total flow is 33.33 and the pressure drop across the parallel portion is 66.67. Each branch therefore receives 16.67 units of flow. If one branch resistance decreases to 1, the equivalent parallel resistance becomes 0.8 and total flow becomes 55.56. The pressure difference across the branches is now 44.44, so the dilated bed receives 44.44 and the unchanged bed receives 11.11. The sum of branch flows agrees with the common conduit flow, allowing an independent conservation check.

This result explains why a conclusion about one tissue cannot be transferred automatically to another. When a question asks whether local vasodilation increases flow in an adjacent unchanged bed, the answer depends on where pressures are maintained and whether the pathways share an appreciable supply resistance. It also provides a conceptual foundation for understanding competition between perfusion territories without requiring specialised clinical models. The source, common conduit, branches and outlet must be identified before applying simple parallel formulas.

Finally, a real capillary bed does not have precisely equal pressure at every junction. Some channels are short, others long, and the distribution of cells at bifurcations can be uneven. A calculation using equal parallel resistances is a useful baseline against which a heterogeneous network can be compared. It predicts that doubling the number of identical open channels halves their equivalent resistance, but does not imply that doubling capillary number automatically doubles oxygen uptake. Exchange surface, blood oxygen content, transit time and tissue metabolism remain additional determinants.

6. Flow velocity, continuity and the pressure profile

Volume flow is not the same quantity as linear velocity. The mean velocity through a vessel cross-section equals Q/A, where A is the lumen cross-sectional area. For a branching region carrying the same total flow at steady state, use the sum of the cross-sectional areas of all perfused channels at that level. This is a conservation relationship: the volume moving across a surface in a given time equals the area integral of the velocity normal to that surface. The expression Q = A·v̄ follows from the definition of cross-sectional mean velocity.

Suppose cardiac output is 5.0 L/min, or 83.33 cm³/s, and an illustrative aortic cross-sectional area is 4 cm². The mean velocity is about 20.8 cm/s. If the collective area of the systemic capillary cross-sections is 2500 cm², the corresponding network mean velocity is 0.0333 cm/s, or 0.333 mm/s. These figures are illustrative, not universal anatomical measurements. They show how slow movement in individual capillaries is compatible with the same total volume passing through the entire systemic network per minute.

Blood moves more slowly through the collective capillary bed because the sum of the lumen areas is large, not because individual capillary radii are large. The large area makes the average velocity small while supporting widespread contact between blood and cells. In a hypothetical capillary of length 0.6 mm with mean velocity 0.3 mm/s, an approximate passage time is 2 s. Real red-cell transit times are distributed rather than identical, and exchange depends on barrier properties and concentration gradients as well as transit time.

The pressure profile is governed by resistance rather than total cross-sectional area alone. Mean pressure falls along the systemic circulation as mechanical energy is dissipated. The fall across the resistance vessels is generally prominent, while pressure at the venous end is comparatively low. The largest pressure decline need not occur in the region where velocity is smallest. In an ideal steady series circuit, the pressure decline across each part is proportional to its resistance. This distinction allows a student to interpret separate graphs of vascular pressure, average velocity and total area without treating them as interchangeable.

A narrow stenotic segment within a single vessel can create a different velocity pattern from the collective capillary bed. If the same flow passes through the stenosis, a reduced area increases velocity at the narrowing. But narrowing can also increase the resistance of the entire pathway and thereby reduce total flow. For a tube of half radius, the area becomes one quarter. At fixed flow, mean velocity is four times greater. At fixed pressure drop across an ideal tube whose entire radius halves, flow becomes one sixteenth and mean velocity one quarter. The apparent contradiction disappears once the experimental conditions are specified.

Velocity measurements in physiology are made in different ways, and interpreting them requires knowledge of what the measurement represents. A flow measurement may estimate the total volume of fluid passing through a cross-section per unit time. A velocity measurement may instead follow a particular red cell, sample a region of moving blood or infer speed from an instrument signal. The two measurements can be converted using lumen area if the measured speed represents the cross-sectional average. If an instrument primarily detects the fastest central stream, treating that speed as the mean can overestimate volume flow.

The ideal parabolic profile derived from Poiseuille's law offers a simple example. The centreline speed is ΔP a²/(4ηL), while the mean speed is Q/(πa²) = ΔP a²/(8ηL). The centreline speed is therefore twice the mean speed in this particular model. This factor of two is not universal for blood flowing through arteries. Pulsatile profiles, a blunt cell-rich core in small vessels, noncircular geometry and developing flow can change the relationship. An examination problem that explicitly states a parabolic profile justifies using the factor, whereas a measured peak velocity without profile information does not.

Doppler ultrasound provides a standard example of inferring velocity from a physical signal. Moving scatterers such as red cells produce a frequency shift in ultrasound reflected towards the detector. The shift depends on the component of their velocity along the ultrasound beam. For simple backscatter geometry, Δf ≈ 2f0 v cos θ/c, where f0 is the transmitted frequency, c is sound speed in the medium and θ is the angle between the beam and flow direction. The two-way propagation accounts for the factor of two. The equation shows why a beam nearly perpendicular to flow produces a small shift despite substantial true velocity.

Suppose f0 = 5 MHz, c = 1540 m/s, θ = 60 degrees and v = 0.4 m/s. The predicted frequency shift magnitude is about 1299 Hz. The same velocity observed at a beam angle of zero degrees produces about 2597 Hz. These values are hypothetical and illustrate angle dependence rather than clinical interpretation. For a comparison using the same instrument, a change in angle may mimic a change in blood speed. Controlling geometry or correcting for it is therefore important. Even an accurate estimate of velocity still requires area and suitable averaging to infer volume flow.

Transit time offers another way to think about velocity. If a small tracer moves through a pathway without leaving the circulating fluid, its arrival downstream provides information about how quickly blood traverses the route. In a heterogeneous network a sharp input may produce a broad distribution of arrival times. Some blood follows faster pathways and some slower ones. A single mean time may describe the centre of this distribution while hiding biologically important differences between capillaries. The distribution can affect how effectively an exchange surface is used.

When solving a velocity problem, first locate the cross-section in question. The area of one capillary is not the area of the capillary bed. The area of the aorta is not the sum of all arterial areas. The total flow through a collection of vessels is the sum of their individual flows, while the appropriate aggregate mean speed is that total divided by their combined cross-sectional area. If individual speeds differ, this aggregate is area-weighted rather than a simple arithmetic mean. These distinctions turn an apparently simple continuity equation into a reliable tool for biological reasoning.

7. Laminar flow, disturbances and Reynolds reasoning

In steady laminar flow adjacent fluid layers move in an orderly manner. The ideal circular-tube velocity profile is smooth, with faster movement near the centre. At sufficiently large inertial effects, small disturbances can grow and produce irregular eddies and fluctuations in velocity. Such turbulent or disturbed flow dissipates mechanical energy differently from fully developed laminar flow. In a vessel that is pulsatile, curved or branching, the distinction is not always a simple binary one: flow can contain complex secondary motions or periodic disturbances without being fully turbulent throughout.

The Reynolds number is Re = ρvD/η, where ρ is fluid density, v is mean velocity, D is internal diameter and η is dynamic viscosity. It is dimensionless because the dimensions of the numerator and denominator cancel. It compares inertial effects with viscous effects. A higher value indicates that inertia is relatively more important. An ideal long straight pipe often develops turbulence near Reynolds numbers of a few thousand, but no single threshold can be transferred uncritically to living arteries. Pulsation, entrance conditions, irregular wall geometry, curvature and the non-Newtonian nature of blood affect the onset and character of disturbances.

For an illustrative large vessel, use density 1060 kg/m³, mean velocity 0.30 m/s, diameter 0.020 m and viscosity 0.004 Pa·s. Re is 1060 × 0.30 × 0.020 / 0.004 = 1590. Doubling velocity while holding other variables fixed doubles Re to 3180. A reduction in viscosity has a similar tendency to raise Re. However, an increase in radius under a fixed volumetric flow reduces velocity by the inverse square of radius, so increasing diameter in that particular comparison reduces Re. The formula must be used together with the conditions that determine velocity.

Flow through an abrupt narrowing can create high velocity and a jet downstream. The jet may separate from the wall at an expansion and produce recirculation and disturbances. These changes may increase energy loss. Sound generated by disturbed cardiovascular flow is a physiological example of mechanical energy being transferred to vibrations, although not every audible sound requires fully developed turbulence. Normal valve closure sounds are distinct from this mechanism.

A pressure difference measured across a narrowing cannot automatically be interpreted using the resistance of a uniform tube of that diameter and the full vessel length. The narrowing may be short, the entrance and exit geometry may dominate part of the loss, and the flow may not have time to develop a parabolic profile. An examination question that explicitly specifies Poiseuille flow permits the ideal relationship; a question about a real stenosis invites recognition of the model's limitations.

The dimensionless Reynolds number can be understood from the governing forces. In a fluid of characteristic speed v and dimension D, inertial effects scale approximately with density times speed squared divided by a length, whereas viscous effects scale approximately with viscosity times speed divided by the square of that length. Dividing these scales gives ρvD/η. The expression is not a new physiological mechanism; it is a compact way to compare two tendencies already present in any flow. At small Reynolds number, viscous forces suppress velocity disturbances efficiently. At larger values, inertia can sustain disturbances for longer and complex flow becomes more likely.

The same equation explains why scale matters. Consider two geometrically similar tubes carrying the same fluid at the same mean speed. The larger diameter yields a larger Reynolds number. Now consider the different experiment in which both tubes carry equal volume flow Q. Substituting v = 4Q/(πD²) into Re gives 4ρQ/(πηD). Under this condition, the smaller tube has a larger Reynolds number. These statements appear opposite only if the experimental constraint is omitted. In a branching vascular network, neither constant speed nor constant flow across every individual branch can be assumed without analysing the geometry.

For an illustrative comparison, a large artery of diameter 20 mm and mean velocity 0.30 m/s has a Reynolds number of 1590 using the previously specified fluid properties. A small vessel of diameter 20 micrometres and mean velocity 0.001 m/s would have a Reynolds number of approximately 0.0053 under the same nominal viscosity and density. The viscosity assumption is imperfect in the small vessel, but the orders of magnitude make the physical point: viscosity dominates inertia very strongly at microvascular scale. Consequently, changes in red-cell mechanics and effective resistance are more important there than a simplistic search for turbulent flow.

The Bernoulli relation is sometimes used to interpret ideal changes in pressure when speed changes. For steady inviscid incompressible flow along a streamline without external work, pressure plus kinetic energy per unit volume plus gravitational potential energy per unit volume remains constant: P + ρv²/2 + ρgz = constant. In a horizontal narrowing with negligible losses, an increase in velocity is accompanied by a decrease in static pressure. Real vascular flow loses energy through friction and, in some circumstances, flow separation. Therefore a downstream expansion need not recover all the pressure lost through acceleration and dissipative processes.

For a hypothetical horizontal ideal streamline, suppose speed increases from 0.5 to 1.0 m/s. With density 1060 kg/m³, the predicted static pressure decrease is 0.5 × 1060 × (1.00 − 0.25) = 397.5 Pa, roughly 3 mmHg. This is not the same calculation as Poiseuille's pressure drop, which describes viscous dissipation in developed flow through a uniform tube. The two equations answer different questions. A complete energy balance in a real vessel can involve both conversion between pressure and kinetic energy and irreversible mechanical energy loss.

A flow visualisation experiment can illustrate the onset of disturbance using a transparent model vessel and a visible tracer. With controlled flow and geometry, the tracer may remain in an orderly stream under some conditions but mix more strongly after an abrupt expansion under others. Comparing the measured pressure losses provides information beyond the visual pattern. Controls include the same fluid viscosity, inlet geometry and mean flow when the narrowing alone is being varied. Such a model does not duplicate pulsatile arterial flow, but it helps students distinguish observed disturbances, the Reynolds tendency and measured energy dissipation as related but distinct evidence.

8. Blood rheology and the influence of red cells

Blood contains suspended red cells, white cells and platelets in plasma. Its viscosity depends not only on the viscosity of the plasma but also on the volume fraction, shape, flexibility and collective behaviour of cells. Haematocrit is the fraction of whole blood volume occupied by red cells. Increasing haematocrit commonly increases apparent viscosity under otherwise comparable conditions. This increases the pressure difference needed to sustain a given flow. The biological consequence is more complex than simply saying that red cells obstruct flow, because the same cells carry most of the oxygen in blood.

At relatively low shear rates, red cells can form reversible aggregates that influence the resistance to deformation of the suspension. At higher shear rates, aggregates tend to disperse and the cells align or deform. These changes contribute to shear-thinning behaviour: the apparent viscosity is lower at higher shear rates over relevant ranges. A fluid may show approximately constant apparent viscosity under a restricted set of conditions, making the Newtonian approximation useful there, even though it is not strictly Newtonian.

The diameter of a small vessel can become comparable with the size of an erythrocyte, which is roughly 7–8 micrometres in its unstressed disc shape. The cells cannot then be treated as infinitely small suspended particles. Their deformability permits passage through very narrow vessels. In many microvessels red cells tend to concentrate towards the axial region, producing a relatively cell-depleted plasma layer near the wall. Because the near-wall region is important for viscous resistance, the arrangement can reduce the apparent viscosity relative to what would be predicted by a homogeneous suspension.

The Fåhraeus effect describes the reduction of tube haematocrit relative to discharge haematocrit in small tubes, associated with red cells travelling faster on average than the bulk fluid. The Fåhraeus–Lindqvist effect describes the diameter-dependent reduction of apparent blood viscosity as tube diameter decreases over much of the microvascular range. The two phenomena are related but are not synonyms. At diameters approaching single-file passage of cells, the apparent viscosity can rise again because of the increasing mechanical constraints on cell movement. Results measured in smooth glass tubes do not transfer exactly to living microvessels, which have an endothelial surface layer and complex branching geometry.

Classical experiments by Fåhraeus and Lindqvist in 1931 examined the relationship between apparent blood viscosity and tube diameter. Their significance was to demonstrate that a bulk viscosity value cannot simply be inserted into a rigid-tube equation at every vascular scale. A teaching version of this experiment would compare the pressure-flow relation in tubes of known length and diameter, holding blood composition and temperature as constant as possible. Water or a standard Newtonian solution provides a control for the apparatus. Apparent viscosity can be inferred from the measured resistance using the ideal geometric factor, while recognising that this is an effective parameter rather than proof that blood obeys a Newtonian constitutive law.

If two samples have identical haematocrit but different red-cell deformability, their resistance in narrow passages can differ. Measuring only haematocrit would not identify this cause. Similarly, a lower haematocrit can reduce viscosity while also decreasing oxygen content. The outcome for oxygen delivery depends on both the change in perfusion and the change in oxygen content. The hydraulic and respiratory functions of blood are thus connected but not interchangeable.

Red-cell distribution in a small vessel has consequences beyond the apparent viscosity of the blood. When blood reaches a branch point, the fraction of red cells entering one branch need not be identical to the fraction of total volume entering that branch. A daughter vessel receiving a relatively cell-depleted stream may have a lower discharge haematocrit than another daughter vessel. Conservation still requires that the total red-cell flux leaving the parent be accounted for across the daughter branches if no cells are lost or stored. The haematocrit of a branch can therefore differ from the haematocrit sampled in a large upstream vessel.

To make this quantitative, consider a hypothetical parent flow of 100 µL/min carrying a discharge haematocrit of 0.40. The red-cell volume flux is 40 µL/min. If one branch receives 30 µL/min of whole blood at haematocrit 0.20, its red-cell flux is 6 µL/min. The other branch receives the remaining 70 µL/min and, by conservation, 34 µL/min of red-cell volume. Its discharge haematocrit is 34/70, about 0.486. The flow-weighted average across the two branches equals the parent discharge haematocrit, as it must. A simple arithmetic average of the two haematocrit values would not satisfy the appropriate mass balance.

Tube haematocrit and discharge haematocrit answer different questions even within one vessel. Imagine a short length of tube examined at an instant. The fraction of its enclosed volume occupied by red cells is tube haematocrit. If a sample of all fluid leaving the tube over a time interval is collected and analysed, the red-cell fraction is discharge haematocrit. When red cells move faster on average than the bulk suspension, they spend less time within the tube than a corresponding volume of more slowly moving plasma. This produces a difference between the instantaneous content and the transported fraction. Understanding it requires distinguishing stored quantity from flux, a recurring theme in circulation.

A useful relation can be written for a uniform segment of length L. Let A be its cross-sectional area, HT the tube haematocrit and vc the mean red-cell speed. The red-cell volumetric flux is approximately HT A vc. Total volumetric flux is A v, where v is the bulk mean velocity. The discharge haematocrit HD is their ratio, giving HD = HT vc/v. Thus HT/HD = v/vc. When vc is greater than v, tube haematocrit is lower than discharge haematocrit. This relationship is a simplified steady-state balance, not an independent empirical law describing how the velocities arise.

The biological importance of cell deformability can be tested by comparing pressure-flow measurements in channels of different diameters while controlling cell concentration. If the resistance of two suspensions differs primarily in narrow channels but not in large channels, one plausible explanation is that the ability of the cells to deform through confined spaces differs. Other explanations, such as aggregation, plasma viscosity or interactions with the wall, must be controlled before concluding that deformability is solely responsible. A complementary observation of cell passage through narrow channels can strengthen the interpretation. Such reasoning links a macroscopic pressure-flow difference to a cellular property.

This also explains why a decrease in whole-blood viscosity is not necessarily physiologically beneficial in isolation. Consider two blood samples with different red-cell concentrations. If the lower concentration permits 20% greater flow but reduces arterial oxygen content by 30%, the oxygen delivery ratio is 1.2 × 0.7 = 0.84. Delivery is reduced by 16% in this simplified comparison despite greater perfusion. Actual tissues may alter extraction and the organism may compensate through cardiac output or other changes. A correct analysis identifies which variables were measured and which are being held constant before assigning biological advantage.

9. Compliance, capacitance and elastic recoil

Compliance describes the change in volume resulting from a change in transmural pressure. For small changes, C = ΔV/ΔPtm, with units such as mL/mmHg. If the pressure-volume curve is nonlinear, the local differential compliance is dV/dPtm. Distensibility is a related but normalised quantity: compliance divided by the reference volume. A large vessel can therefore have a large absolute compliance while being less distensible than another vessel considered relative to its own volume.

Veins generally have high compliance in their normal operating pressure range. A considerable volume can be accommodated with a small pressure increment. They serve as an important blood reservoir. The term capacitance refers to their volume-holding behaviour, whereas compliance specifies the slope of their pressure-volume relationship. These concepts are closely related but not identical. Active venoconstriction can change the pressure-volume relationship and reduce the volume stored at a particular pressure. The details of venous return were introduced in the preceding node.

Elastic arteries have lower compliance than veins but their compliance is crucial because they receive pulsatile output directly from the ventricle. Consider two hypothetical arterial systems that receive the same additional retained volume of 30 mL during a short period, with outflow accounted for. If the first has compliance 1.5 mL/mmHg, the pressure increment is 20 mmHg. If the second has compliance 0.75 mL/mmHg, the increment is 40 mmHg. This example assumes local linearity of the pressure-volume relation and holds the retained volume fixed. It does not claim that pulse pressure always equals stroke volume divided by compliance because blood also leaves the arterial system during ejection.

An artery can be stiff either because of its material properties or because it is operating on a steep part of its nonlinear pressure-volume curve. Elastic fibres bear load at smaller strains and progressive recruitment of collagen fibres limits expansion at greater strains. Consequently, raising baseline pressure can itself reduce the incremental compliance of an artery without requiring structural remodelling. Interpreting an experimental pressure-volume curve requires distinguishing the location on the curve from changes in the curve itself.

The mechanical wave of pressure travels along an artery faster than the mean forward motion of the blood. A pulse detected at a peripheral site is the arrival of a propagating mechanical disturbance, not the arrival of a particular parcel of blood ejected from the ventricle at that instant. Changes in arterial stiffness can change the propagation speed and the timing of reflected waves. This is another instance in which flow velocity and pressure behaviour must be kept separate.

Compliance must be defined relative to a reference state because biological vessels have nonlinear volume-pressure curves. At a low pressure, a thin-walled vein may have a flattened cross-section and can change volume substantially by changing shape, without greatly stretching its wall. As pressure rises, the lumen becomes more rounded. Additional increases in volume then require more wall stretch and compliance may fall. The result is a steep volume response in one range and a more gradual response in another. Consequently, a single numerical venous compliance cannot describe every possible pressure or activation state.

A distinction often used in explaining vascular storage is that between unstressed and stressed volume. In a simplified vascular compartment, unstressed volume is the volume accommodated before appreciable elastic distending pressure is generated. Additional volume stretches the compartment and contributes to stressed volume. The pressure-volume relation can be represented approximately as Ptm = (V − V0)/C over a limited positive-pressure range, where V0 is the reference unstressed volume. This equation is not suitable for describing every shape change or collapse of a vessel. It is a conceptual representation of how volume and elastic pressure can be linked.

Suppose two model venous compartments have the same compliance of 100 mL/mmHg but different reference volumes. One contains 800 mL with V0 = 600 mL, and the other contains 800 mL with V0 = 700 mL. The predicted transmural pressures are 2 and 1 mmHg respectively. Identical total volume therefore does not imply identical distending pressure. If active smooth muscle changes the effective reference volume, pressure can change even without an immediate change in total blood volume. This is a useful bridge to the relationship between vascular capacitance and venous return, without repeating the complete cardiac output framework.

A related mechanical consideration is the pressure external to a vein. When skeletal muscle contracts around a limb vein, the pressure outside the vessel can increase. This may compress the vein and displace some blood. A valve can prevent much of the backward movement so that repeated compression and release support directed return. The effectiveness depends on the arrangement of valves, the timing of muscle activity and the availability of an outflow path. A vein with no suitable pressure gradient cannot act as an independent pump merely because it is compliant.

In arteries, a pressure-volume plot can also distinguish a change in compliance from a change in the operating point. Imagine measuring volume at pressures of 80, 100 and 120 mmHg. If volume increments over successive 20 mmHg intervals become smaller, the local compliance is falling as the vessel is distended. To test whether a change in wall composition has altered compliance, compare pressure-volume relations at matched pressure and activation state, rather than comparing one vessel at low pressure with another at high pressure. A stiffer response at a matched pressure is stronger evidence for altered wall mechanics than a difference in pulse pressure alone.

Mechanical energy storage by a compliant vessel is distinct from volume storage. For a locally linear reservoir with compliance C, an increase in stored volume from zero relative to its reference state to ΔV increases pressure linearly. The work done in distending it is the integral of pressure with respect to volume, equal to (ΔV)²/(2C) in this ideal model. Equivalently it is C(ΔP)²/2. This energy can be partly returned as the reservoir recoils, although real vessel walls exhibit dissipative behaviour. The heart supplies the original energy; compliance determines how that energy is temporarily distributed over the heartbeat.

10. The Windkessel mechanism

The simplest Windkessel model represents the large arteries as a compliant reservoir and the peripheral circulation as a resistance. During ventricular ejection, some of the incoming volume passes through the peripheral resistance immediately while the rest increases the arterial volume and pressure. During diastole, the aortic valve is closed and the ventricle supplies essentially no forward flow into the aorta, yet elastic recoil continues to drive blood into the peripheral circulation. The model explains how intermittent cardiac ejection can support relatively continuous peripheral flow.

Let arterial pressure relative to the downstream reference pressure be P. If compliance C is constant and peripheral resistance is R, volume conservation gives C dP/dt = Qin − P/R. The left-hand side represents the rate of volume storage in the compliant reservoir. Qin is the flow from the heart, and P/R is the outflow through the peripheral resistance. During an idealised diastolic interval Qin = 0, so dP/dt = −P/(RC). Integration gives P(t) = P(0)e^(−t/RC). If absolute arterial pressure is used instead, the reference downstream pressure must be subtracted before applying the exponential relationship.

The product RC is a time constant. With R in mmHg·s/mL and C in mL/mmHg, RC is in seconds. After one time constant the pressure above downstream pressure is about 37% of its initial value. For R = 1 mmHg·s/mL and C = 1.5 mL/mmHg, the time constant is 1.5 s. At 0.75 s, the relative pressure is e^(−0.5), approximately 0.607. Halving compliance while keeping resistance fixed halves the time constant, and at the same elapsed time the relative pressure becomes e^(−1), approximately 0.368. These calculations compare theoretical decays from the same starting pressure, not necessarily actual arterial waveforms in individuals.

The Windkessel model also distinguishes stored volume from flow. In a periodic steady state, the net change of arterial volume over one cardiac cycle is zero. Therefore the total volume entering the arterial reservoir over a cycle equals the total volume leaving it over that cycle, even though their instantaneous flow rates differ. The arterial wall temporarily stores volume and elastic energy; it does not create additional blood or provide net energy independently of the heart.

A two-element Windkessel model is a lumped approximation, treating the arterial network as a single compliant compartment. It neglects wave transit times, branching, local inertance, reflections and the nonlinear dependence of resistance and compliance on pressure. It can explain the overall smoothing of flow and approximate diastolic pressure decline, but detailed prediction of peripheral pulse shape requires more elaborate descriptions. For examination purposes, it is important to recognise both the power and the limitations of this compact model.

The Windkessel equation makes an important prediction about the relation between average pressure and average flow. Integrate its conservation equation over one full cardiac period T in a periodic steady state. The integral of C dP/dt is zero for constant C because pressure returns to its starting value. Therefore the integral of inflow equals the integral of outflow. Dividing by T gives mean inflow equal to mean P/R when R is constant. Since mean inflow is cardiac output in this representation, mean pressure relative to the outlet equals cardiac output multiplied by peripheral resistance. The relationship is a cycle-averaged consequence of conservation and a resistive outflow, rather than a statement that pressure is constant throughout the cycle.

Suppose two ideal arterial systems have the same average cardiac output and peripheral resistance, but different compliance. Their mean pressure relative to the same outlet is predicted to be the same by the simple model. Their pulsations need not be the same. Lower compliance tends to produce greater pressure changes for a given retained volume and a faster pressure decline when inflow stops. Thus a stiff reservoir can have a higher systolic and lower diastolic pressure than a more compliant reservoir under some otherwise comparable conditions while maintaining similar mean pressure. A change in mean pressure cannot be attributed to compliance alone without considering the heart, resistance and operating conditions.

The time constant also permits estimation from a hypothetical diastolic trace. Suppose arterial pressure is 100 mmHg at the beginning of the chosen interval and the downstream reference is 10 mmHg. After 0.5 s the pressure is 70 mmHg. The relative pressures are therefore 90 and 60 mmHg. The ratio is 2/3. If the ideal exponential model is applicable, RC = −0.5/ln(2/3), which is about 1.23 s. If resistance is independently estimated as 0.8 mmHg·s/mL, compliance is approximately 1.54 mL/mmHg. These inferred values depend on the model assumptions. Neglecting the downstream reference would give a different estimate because 70/100 is not the correct ratio in the stated equation.

One difficulty in estimating arterial compliance is that the Windkessel decay identifies the product RC rather than the two parameters separately. Many combinations of resistance and compliance generate the same time constant. A slow decline could result from a relatively high resistance, a relatively high compliance or both. To infer compliance, additional information about resistance is needed. This is an example of an identifiability problem in physiological modelling. A numerical fit may reproduce a pressure trace closely while leaving more than one possible biological explanation for the fitted parameter.

A classical physical demonstration can use a pulsatile source, a compliant chamber and a downstream flow resistance. Recording inflow and outflow allows comparison between the case with an elastic chamber and one with a nearly rigid chamber. The elastic chamber should permit continued outflow between inflow pulses. If the average inflow rate and downstream resistance are controlled, changes in the pulsatile component can be attributed more confidently to storage. The apparatus does not reproduce arterial wave propagation, but it reveals the basic conservation and recoil mechanism in a form that can be observed directly.

The word Windkessel originated from the idea of an air chamber used to smooth intermittent flow in pumping systems. An arterial system is not an air-filled chamber and its compliant behaviour arises from distension of vessel walls. The analogy is useful because both systems temporarily store mechanical energy and volume, but it becomes misleading if taken to imply that arteries contain a separate compressible gas reservoir. Otto Frank's mathematical treatment of arterial pressure helped establish the connection between elastic storage and peripheral resistance. The simplified equation remains an accessible introduction to cardiovascular system modelling.

11. Microcirculation: matching transport to exchange

Microcirculation includes small arteries and arterioles, capillaries and venules in the tissue. The exchange region must provide sufficient surface area and maintain suitable concentration gradients while avoiding excessive loss of circulating fluid. Water, ions, gases, nutrients, waste products and macromolecules differ in their ability to cross the endothelial barrier. Therefore the path used by a particular substance depends on its properties and the type of capillary.

Continuous capillaries have a relatively uninterrupted endothelial lining, with junctions and transport pathways whose permeability varies by organ. The specialised endothelium of brain capillaries has tight junctions and restricted paracellular transport, contributing to the blood-brain barrier. Fenestrated capillaries contain endothelial pores that facilitate transfer of water and small solutes; examples include several endocrine and absorptive tissues. Discontinuous or sinusoidal vessels, such as those in the liver, have much greater permeability to many large molecules. The glomerular capillary of the kidney is fenestrated but forms only one part of a highly selective filtration barrier that includes a basement membrane and podocyte structures.

Diffusion is central to the exchange of respiratory gases. Fick's diffusion law relates the rate of transfer to the effective surface area, concentration gradient, diffusion coefficient and diffusion distance. For a simplified planar barrier the magnitude of flux per unit area is DΔc/Δx. Multiplying by area gives a total transfer rate. Increasing the distance from capillary to cell reduces diffusive transfer at fixed concentrations and coefficient. This explains why capillary density and the organisation of tissue influence exchange. It also helps explain why interstitial swelling can impair oxygen transfer even when some blood continues to reach the tissue.

Diffusion is distinct from the pressure-driven movement of water across capillary walls. Water moving as a bulk flow may carry permeable solutes with it, a process known as convection or solvent drag. Large proteins can be retained to a variable extent depending on barrier structure. Endothelial transcellular transport and paracellular passage may also contribute to the movement of different solutes. A single statement that substances pass through pores cannot describe all these routes.

The capillary network is supplied and drained through vessels whose resistances determine the pressures within the exchange region. Raising downstream venous pressure tends to raise capillary pressure when other factors are held constant. Arteriolar constriction tends to decrease capillary pressure under comparable fixed inlet and outlet pressures. These two effects may both reduce tissue blood flow while having opposite consequences for the hydrostatic drive for fluid filtration. Therefore flow and filtration cannot be inferred from one another without knowing how the change was produced.

Local metabolites, changes in tissue oxygen demand and mechanical effects on vessel walls can alter perfusion. During muscle activity, local vasodilation increases delivery of oxygen and substrates and assists removal of metabolic products. The detailed mediators, autoregulation and integrated control belong to node 7.B.08. Here the key principle is that adjusting resistance changes both the flow and the pressure distribution within the exchange bed.

Different forms of capillary exchange can be compared by examining the properties of a tracer. A small lipid-soluble molecule can cross endothelial cell membranes relatively readily under suitable conditions. A water-soluble small molecule may use a paracellular or other specialised route whose availability depends on the tissue. A large plasma protein generally experiences stronger restriction across many continuous capillary beds. The endothelial surface is not a single uniform membrane with identical permeability to every molecule. Changes in one tracer's movement therefore cannot be assumed to represent every other component of blood.

If a tissue is perfused with a tracer at known arterial concentration and the venous concentration is measured, a concentration difference indicates net removal from or addition to the circulating fluid within the tissue. For a substance entering at concentration Ca and leaving at Cv, the net uptake rate is Q(Ca − Cv) when inflow and outflow volume rates are approximately equal and tissue storage is stable. The formula follows from conservation and resembles the Fick principle used to calculate cardiac output. It does not, by itself, identify whether removal occurred by diffusion, transport into cells, chemical reaction or storage in another compartment.

Consider a hypothetical tracer delivered at 2 units/mL, with venous concentration 0.8 units/mL and tissue flow 50 mL/min. Delivery is 100 units/min and venous export is 40 units/min; net uptake is 60 units/min. The extraction fraction is (2 − 0.8)/2 = 0.6. If flow doubles and extraction fraction falls to 0.4, the new uptake is 100 × 2 × 0.4 = 80 units/min. Greater flow can increase total uptake despite a lower fraction removed from each unit volume. A lower venous extraction fraction should not automatically be interpreted as a lower total rate of exchange.

Capillary recruitment and altered red-cell distribution can change the effective surface for exchange. The number of anatomical capillaries present in a region is not necessarily the number carrying red cells at a particular moment. Some pathways may have very low or intermittent red-cell flux. When tissue activity increases, the distribution of perfusion can change, improving access to exchange surfaces. The mechanism and extent vary across tissues, and not every capillary network contains discrete muscular precapillary sphincters of the type shown in simplified diagrams. A student should distinguish a useful conceptual representation from a universal anatomical claim.

An experimental comparison of exchange between tissues could use tracers of different molecular size, measure the concentration delivered and recovered and determine blood flow. A higher extraction fraction for the smaller tracer might be consistent with greater permeability, but it could also reflect different binding, cellular uptake or distribution volumes. Selecting tracers with well-understood transport behaviour and measuring the time course helps separate these possibilities. If a change in capillary perfusion occurs at the same time, exchange must be normalised appropriately before concluding that the endothelial barrier itself has changed.

The functional importance of exchange geometry is particularly clear when one compares an organ with closely spaced capillaries to one with longer distances between cells and the nearest perfused capillary. The total blood supply to both organs could be identical, yet oxygen delivery at the cellular level might differ because local diffusion distances and the distribution of flow are different. Haemodynamics and microvascular exchange are thus complementary levels of analysis: the first establishes how blood reaches an exchange surface, while the second asks how transported material reaches its biological target.

12. Capillary filtration, glycocalyx and revised Starling reasoning

The classical Starling formulation separates hydrostatic and colloid osmotic influences on transvascular water movement. With outward filtration defined as positive, Jv = Kf[(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)], where Pc and Pi are capillary and interstitial hydrostatic pressures, πc and πi are colloid osmotic pressures associated with proteins in plasma and interstitial fluid, Kf is the filtration coefficient and σ is a protein reflection coefficient. If pressure is in mmHg and Kf in mL/min/mmHg, Jv is in mL/min for the specified exchange surface. The reflection coefficient is dimensionless and ranges from zero for an ideal freely permeating solute to one for a completely reflected solute.

Consider hypothetical classical values Pc = 25, Pi = 0, πc = 25, πi = 5 mmHg and σ = 1. The effective outward driving pressure is 25 − 20 = 5 mmHg. If Kf is 0.1 mL/min/mmHg, the estimated filtration rate is 0.5 mL/min. Raising Pc by 5 mmHg while holding the other quantities fixed doubles the net driving pressure to 10 mmHg and the calculated outward flux to 1.0 mL/min. This is a deliberately simplified problem in which the protein concentrations and the barrier properties remain fixed; it should not be mistaken for a prediction of long-term tissue fluid accumulation.

The classical formulation is useful for identifying the competing forces, but the interpretation of the effective osmotic barrier has changed. The endothelial glycocalyx is a hydrated surface layer containing membrane-associated glycoproteins, proteoglycans and their associated molecules. It helps regulate the passage of macromolecules, provides a cell-surface interface with flowing blood and forms an important part of the effective semipermeable barrier. Protein concentration immediately beneath this layer may differ from that in the surrounding bulk interstitial fluid.

In the revised formulation, the effective colloid osmotic difference across the glycocalyx involves plasma and the subglycocalyx region. Writing πg for the subglycocalyx colloid osmotic pressure, a simplified revised relation uses σ(πc − πg) in place of σ(πc − πi). During steady outward filtration, fluid movement can help keep the protein concentration of the subglycocalyx region relatively low. When filtration falls or reverses, the local protein environment changes, reducing the tendency for sustained absorption. The effective osmotic gradient is therefore coupled to flow and barrier structure rather than being simply prescribed by bulk tissue protein concentration.

The older textbook picture of filtration at the arterial end of every capillary and sustained reabsorption at the venous end of every capillary is not a universal description of steady-state exchange. In many ordinary systemic beds, there is net filtration along much of the exchange surface and lymphatic return balances the filtered fluid. Transient absorption can occur following a sudden fall in capillary pressure, and specialised absorptive vascular beds can sustain absorption under their particular conditions. Levick and Michel's 2010 review explains these developments and their experimental basis. Students should use the equation specified in a numerical problem, while avoiding the universal arterial-filtration/venous-reabsorption claim in mechanistic reasoning.

An experiment comparing fluid movement before and after a step decrease in capillary pressure illustrates the distinction. If all colloid osmotic concentrations were constant, the classical equation would predict that a negative net driving pressure produces continuing absorption. In a vessel where the subglycocalyx protein environment changes in response to flow, absorption can be strongest initially and then decline. Measuring the time course, rather than a single immediate rate, allows the competing interpretations to be distinguished. Appropriate controls include stable perfusate protein concentration and checks that the barrier has not been damaged by the measurement procedure.

The filtration coefficient Kf can be interpreted as the product of the hydraulic conductivity of a barrier and the effective area available for exchange, provided the chosen description treats the barrier as approximately uniform. Hydraulic conductivity describes the ease with which water crosses a unit area under a given effective pressure difference. An increase in total filtration coefficient can therefore arise from a more permeable barrier, a greater perfused exchange area, or both. A measurement of increased filtration at fixed driving pressure does not distinguish these explanations without additional evidence.

The reflection coefficient requires a separate interpretation. A value near one indicates that the solute contributes strongly to osmotic opposition because it is substantially excluded by the barrier. A value near zero indicates that the solute's concentration difference provides little effective sustained osmotic opposition in the ideal description. Reducing the reflection coefficient for plasma proteins weakens their effective ability to oppose outward water movement. It may also alter protein distribution outside the vessel over time. The reflection coefficient should not be confused with hydraulic conductivity, which determines the proportionality between driving pressure and water flow.

For a hypothetical comparison, take a hydrostatic difference of 20 mmHg, an effective colloid osmotic difference of 15 mmHg and a filtration coefficient of 0.05 mL/min/mmHg. With a reflection coefficient of one, the net outward force is 5 mmHg and filtration is 0.25 mL/min. With a reflection coefficient of 0.5 and all specified quantities fixed, the net force is 12.5 mmHg and filtration is 0.625 mL/min. If hydraulic conductivity also increases, the rate could rise further. These calculations are deliberately instantaneous, holding the effective colloid osmotic difference constant; after fluid and protein redistribute, the effective driving terms may change.

The classical and revised Starling descriptions can be reconciled by identifying the physical location of the semipermeable barrier. Osmotic pressure opposes solvent movement across a barrier to the extent that a solute is excluded. If the most effective protein exclusion occurs at the glycocalyx, the protein concentration on the two sides of that structure is the relevant one. Measuring average interstitial protein concentration may not reveal the concentration immediately beneath the glycocalyx. An equation that uses bulk interstitial osmotic pressure can therefore overestimate the tendency for sustained reabsorption in certain microvascular beds.

The revised interpretation does not imply that interstitial protein concentration is irrelevant. Proteins in the interstitium can influence the local protein environment, lymphatic removal and tissue fluid balance. The relationship between bulk interstitial concentration and the subglycocalyx concentration depends on transport and local geometry. Nor does the revised interpretation mean hydrostatic pressure has ceased to matter. Lowering capillary pressure can reduce filtration and may temporarily produce absorption. The key change is that the effective osmotic term can respond to the direction and magnitude of water movement, so steady-state predictions differ from those of a model with fixed osmotic concentrations.

A particularly instructive evidence-based comparison concerns the time course of fluid movement following a sudden pressure decrease. An immediate observation of absorption is compatible with both the classical prediction and the revised interpretation. The difference emerges when absorption is followed over time under controlled conditions. If the rate approaches a much smaller sustained value without the imposed pressure being restored, the assumption of an unchanged effective osmotic difference is challenged. The experimental conclusion must still account for measurement stability, changes in filtration area and possible barrier damage. A single time point cannot establish a complete mechanism.

The student can therefore distinguish three questions: what is the instantaneous net driving pressure, what determines the effective osmotic difference at the actual barrier, and what happens to the tissue fluid volume over time? Each requires a slightly different calculation or experiment. Confusing them leads to apparently contradictory statements about filtration and absorption. Keeping them separate allows the classical equation to remain useful for specified numerical exercises while incorporating the more accurate physiological understanding of the endothelial surface layer.

13. Lymphatic return, oedema and tissue fluid balance

Filtered fluid does not accumulate indefinitely in healthy tissue. Initial lymphatic vessels collect interstitial fluid and macromolecules. Their specialised endothelial overlaps and supporting structures permit fluid entry when local pressure conditions favour it. Collecting lymphatic vessels transport lymph towards the venous circulation. Valves, intrinsic contraction of collecting vessels and external tissue movements help maintain directed transport. The lymphatic network is therefore a return pathway that complements the blood vascular exchange system.

At a simplified tissue scale, the rate of change of interstitial fluid volume equals net vascular filtration minus lymphatic removal, if other fluid inputs and outputs are negligible. The equation is a mass balance. A constant interstitial volume does not require zero filtration; it requires equality of the opposing volume rates. If filtration is 0.4 mL/min and lymphatic removal is also 0.4 mL/min, the volume stays constant. If filtration suddenly rises to 0.7 while removal initially remains 0.4, the initial net accumulation is 0.3 mL/min. This rate cannot necessarily be extrapolated indefinitely because tissue pressure and lymph flow can change.

Oedema is excessive accumulation of fluid in the interstitial space. A rise in capillary hydrostatic pressure, a reduction of effective colloid osmotic opposition, greater hydraulic permeability, a reduction in protein reflection or inadequate lymphatic drainage can contribute. These mechanisms may coexist. The same observed swelling can therefore arise through different physiological routes. A problem that specifies elevated venous pressure suggests increased upstream capillary pressure, while a problem involving impaired lymph drainage suggests inadequate return of filtered fluid and proteins.

The extracellular matrix also influences the pressure-volume relationship of the interstitium. Initially, an increase in tissue fluid can raise interstitial pressure and oppose further filtration. Lymph flow can increase in response to increased fluid availability within limits. These responses provide a buffering capacity against small disturbances, but are not unlimited. When the magnitude or duration of filtration exceeds the combined buffering and drainage capacity, fluid accumulates.

Oedema can increase the diffusion distance between perfused capillaries and cells. The consequences depend on the tissue and on how the fluid is distributed. Excess fluid within a confined structure can also affect local mechanical pressures. These examples show why the circulatory system must regulate not only the movement of blood along vessels but also the movement of water across vessel walls. The preceding blood volume chapter treats whole-body fluid compartments; this chapter provides the vascular mechanism connecting them.

An integrated interpretation begins with the conditions of the tissue rather than with one memorised equation. Determine the pressure difference driving blood through the tissue, identify the sites of resistance, infer the resulting exchange-vessel pressure, examine the barrier properties, and finally compare filtration with removal. In the next node, the same pressure-flow relationships are used to explain how arterial pressure is regulated by neural feedback.

Lymphatic drainage provides a second route for material to return from the interstitium to the blood. Its importance is not restricted to water balance. Plasma proteins that cross vascular barriers cannot necessarily return rapidly by crossing the same barrier in the opposite direction. By collecting interstitial fluid, the lymphatic system also returns macromolecules and contributes to the maintenance of the interstitial protein environment. If lymphatic removal is impaired, protein as well as water can accumulate. This can change effective osmotic forces and make the evolution of swelling more complex than a simple excess of water.

An initial lymphatic vessel differs structurally from a blood capillary. It begins within the tissue rather than connecting an arterial inlet to a venous outlet. Fluid enters when local forces favour opening of the entry structures. Larger collecting lymphatics contain valves and can generate contractions in their walls. Because the lymphatic pressure gradients and surrounding tissue movements vary, lymph flow is not necessarily continuous at every location. Nevertheless, the net return over time is directed towards the venous circulation. This separate circuit is one reason why normal systemic blood volume can be maintained despite persistent filtration across many capillary beds.

Consider a region in which venous pressure rises while arterial pressure and the upstream and downstream resistance values remain fixed. The total pressure difference driving flow declines, while the intermediate capillary pressure rises according to the series-resistance model. Reduced tissue perfusion can therefore occur alongside increased hydrostatic filtration. If lymph flow initially cannot match the increase in filtration, interstitial volume rises. The same disturbance thus affects delivery along vessels and fluid exchange across their walls in different directions. A student who treats low perfusion as evidence of low filtration would miss this mechanism.

In another region, arteriolar constriction reduces flow and tends to lower the downstream capillary pressure when endpoint pressures remain fixed. The hydrostatic filtration drive may decrease rather than increase. If both cases exhibit the same reduction in total flow, measuring flow alone cannot reveal which resistance changed. A simultaneous estimate of pressure at or near the exchange vessels, or an observation of fluid accumulation, helps discriminate between the mechanisms. This comparison is useful because it shows how an experiment can distinguish causes rather than merely document consequences.

The balance of interstitial fluid also has a time dimension. A sudden increase in filtration may occur before lymph flow has adjusted. The initial accumulation rate is the difference of the two fluxes. With increasing tissue volume, interstitial pressure can change, modifying filtration, and lymphatic removal may increase. The system may eventually approach a new steady state with an enlarged interstitial volume but no further net accumulation. Thus the presence of oedema does not prove that its volume is continuing to increase at the moment of observation. Conversely, a normal-looking tissue can be in the early stage of an imbalance that has not yet produced substantial swelling.

This chapter's analytical framework can be used to approach an unfamiliar vascular dataset without memorising a large set of isolated rules. First identify whether the quantity of interest is volume flow, mean velocity, pressure, resistance, compliance, oxygen delivery or transvascular fluid flux. Second identify the part of the network being considered and the experimental variables held constant. Third apply the relevant conservation law, physical model or exchange equation with appropriate units. Fourth consider how biological properties such as active smooth muscle tone, red-cell deformability or endothelial permeability could invalidate a simple assumption. Finally identify which observations would distinguish the possible mechanisms. This sequence connects the physical foundations of haemodynamics with the neural and chemical regulatory mechanisms that follow in the curriculum.
