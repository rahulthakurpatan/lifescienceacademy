The biological problem: building a three-dimensional appendage

A vertebrate limb does not arise by enlarging a miniature hand or foot. Early in embryonic development, a region of the lateral body wall becomes capable of making an appendage. Its initially inconspicuous swelling eventually contains skeletal elements, muscles, connective tissues, blood vessels, nerves and skin. The remarkable feature is not merely the production of these tissues but their arrangement in a coordinated three-dimensional pattern. A hand has a wrist at one end and fingers at the other; the digits occupy different positions across its width, and the skin and tendons on the dorsal and ventral surfaces have different organization. Development must solve these spatial problems while simultaneously increasing the number of cells and changing the shape of the tissue.

The proximodistal (PD) axis extends from the body wall toward the distal tip. Its major skeletal regions are the stylopod, containing a single major long bone such as the humerus or femur; the zeugopod, containing two principal long bones such as the radius and ulna or tibia and fibula; and the autopod, containing the wrist or ankle and the digits. These regional names apply across many tetrapods even when particular bones have become reduced or modified. They describe positions in the appendage, not independent germ layers. A cell that becomes cartilage in the stylopod and one that becomes cartilage in a digit may share a differentiated cell type while occupying very different positional identities.

The anteroposterior (AP) axis runs across the developing limb. In a mammalian forelimb the thumb side is anterior or preaxial, and the little finger side is posterior or postaxial. Embryonic directions must be used consistently, because later limb rotation makes descriptions based on the posture of an adult unreliable. A duplicated series of digits arranged in reversed order suggests a disruption of AP information. It is not automatically evidence for a change in limb length. Dorsoventral (DV) polarity distinguishes the back of the hand from its palm, and analogous surfaces of other limbs. Each axis offers a different question about how position becomes biological identity.

A developmental signal is called inductive when a population changes its developmental behavior in response to another population. The responding cells must also possess competence, meaning that their internal state permits an appropriate response. The limb makes this relationship especially clear: a signal released by mesenchyme alters the neighboring ectoderm, whose subsequent signals influence the mesenchyme. A small signaling territory can therefore organize a much larger region without its own cells becoming the entire organ. This distinction between the source of information and the descendants that receive it will be essential when interpreting graft experiments.

Limb fields, embryonic tissue origins and initiation

During vertebrate embryogenesis, the mesoderm on either side of the trunk becomes partitioned into paraxial, intermediate and lateral plate territories. The lateral plate separates into layers associated with the ectoderm and the endoderm. The somatic lateral plate mesoderm contributes the skeletal and much of the connective tissue framework of a limb. The overlying surface ectoderm contributes epidermis and provides specialized inductive signals. Muscle progenitors instead arrive from the adjacent somites, which are derivatives of paraxial mesoderm. These origins explain why a single limb contains multiple cell lineages even though its shape appears as one unified organ.

The ability to initiate a limb is restricted to particular levels along the trunk. The forelimb and hindlimb fields form in different axial contexts. Hox gene expression helps establish regional identity along the body, while T-box transcription factors contribute to initiating the appendage program. Tbx5 has a particularly important role in forelimb initiation; hindlimb initiation involves Tbx4 and factors including Pitx1. It is useful to avoid a simple statement that Tbx5 alone makes every aspect of a forelimb and Tbx4 alone specifies every aspect of a hindlimb. A factor can be required for outgrowth without independently accounting for all later skeletal and muscular identity.

At a future limb site, cells change organization, accumulate beneath ectoderm and form a protrusion. This mesenchymal population includes progenitors of the future internal framework. Mesenchyme is a description of relatively loosely organized embryonic cells; it is not synonymous with all mesoderm in the embryo. Local cell proliferation and changes in cell shape and movement help generate the bud. As the protrusion grows, the distribution of signaling molecules and the distances over which cells communicate change as well. Thus the spatial map cannot be understood as a static diagram superimposed on an unchanging mass of cells.

The importance of tissue origin can be tested by labeling a group of somitic cells before migration and later determining where their descendants occur. If the label appears in limb muscle but not in most cartilage, this is consistent with distinct embryonic sources for these tissues. Such an experiment must follow a stable label, control for accidental labeling of adjacent mesoderm, and establish the final tissue identity using appropriate markers. A label in a tissue demonstrates contribution of descendants under the experimental conditions; it does not prove that every labeled cell was irreversibly committed at the time of labeling.

The apical ectodermal ridge and reciprocal induction

The distal ectoderm of an early limb bud develops a specialized thickening called the apical ectodermal ridge (AER). Its epithelial organization contrasts with the underlying mesenchymal population. The ridge occupies the distal margin near the boundary between dorsal and ventral ectoderm and is positioned to influence cells near the growing tip. Its location is crucial: signals originating at the tip can maintain a population of cells that will contribute to more distal regions as the limb extends.

Fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) are secreted signaling proteins. FGF10 produced by limb mesenchyme is an important initiating input to the overlying ectoderm, where receptor FGFR2b participates in the response. In the mouse limb, epithelial Wnt signaling contributes to the establishment of AER Fgf8 expression. FGF8 from the ridge in turn acts on mesenchymal populations and helps sustain the signaling environment that supports outgrowth. The interaction is reciprocal because each tissue supplies something required by the other. The ridge is therefore not simply a passive cap that happens to be present on an enlarging limb bud.

An FGF receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase. Ligand recognition promotes receptor activation and downstream intracellular signaling, including pathways that influence survival, proliferation and gene expression. In the limb, the relevant outcome depends on the receptor forms expressed by a particular population and its developmental history. The same extracellular ligand can have distinct effects in tissues with different signaling competence. This helps explain why a broadly distributed protein is not equivalent to a universally identical instruction.

A positive interaction between FGF10 and ridge FGFs is useful during rapid tissue expansion. If an initiating population produces too little signal, the ectodermal response may be insufficient; if the ridge fails to form or its function is lost, the mesenchymal support normally supplied by the ridge is weakened. Fgf10 loss in mice provides a strong genetic test of its importance for appendage formation. The result is fundamentally different from disrupting a late digit-specific differentiation gene, because failure at initiation can prevent the establishment of the substrate on which later patterning acts.

A positive feedback system also raises a question about termination. An appendage cannot remain a growing bud indefinitely. The persistence of ridge activity is coordinated with the stage of mesenchymal development and with other signaling regions, including the posterior mesenchyme. Later changes in the signaling network reduce outgrowth activity and permit differentiation. It is misleading to imagine that the AER mechanically pulls the limb outward, or that FGF itself supplies energy for tissue growth. Cells synthesize proteins, replicate DNA and move using metabolic energy; FGF regulates the cellular processes that determine when and where such activities occur.

Classical ridge excision, rescue and proximodistal identity

The chick wing made early manipulative embryology particularly informative. John Saunders showed in classical work that removal of the distal ectodermal ridge interferes with subsequent development of the wing. A suitably staged untreated wing provides a comparison, while a sham operation controls for the injury involved in manipulation. Removing the ridge early typically has more severe consequences for the distal skeleton than removing it after additional development. This stage dependence indicates that the signaling region is required for normal development over time, rather than contributing a predetermined set of bones by simple descent.

A second kind of experiment asks whether a signal can substitute for the tissue that normally provides it. In experiments reported by Niswander and colleagues in 1993, FGF4 could replace important functions of the removed ridge in chick limbs. An FGF-containing source positioned at the distal tissue provides a biologically active input, whereas an otherwise comparable carrier lacking the active factor helps control for physical effects of the implant. Restoration of outgrowth and pattern provides stronger evidence for a molecular function than the observation of gene expression alone. Nevertheless the capacity of a supplied FGF to rescue ridge functions does not mean that all ridge genes act exclusively through that single molecule.

An influential historical model assigned PD fate according to how long a mesenchymal cell remained near the ridge in a distal region often called the progress zone. Cells that left this region sooner were proposed to acquire more proximal fates, while those that remained longer acquired increasingly distal fates. The model gives a convenient interpretation of why early ridge removal is more destructive to distal development than late removal. It also connects the growth of the bud with a time-dependent change in developmental state.

However, a prediction can agree with more than one mechanistic explanation. Removing the ridge may cause death or reduced expansion of particular progenitors rather than simply stopping a universal identity timer. Evidence for early regional specification and subsequent differential expansion complicates a strictly time-only progress-zone interpretation. Other models consider interactions between proximal and distal signaling environments, with distal FGF activity and changing competence contributing to region-specific gene expression. An exam answer should recognize the classical model without treating its original assumptions as a complete experimentally established explanation of all tetrapod limb patterning.

The distinction between growth and identity is particularly important. Suppose an experimentally treated limb has a shortened distal skeleton. There are at least two possibilities: distal progenitors were lost or failed to expand, or surviving cells adopted inappropriate positional identities. A skeletal preparation alone cannot always distinguish the alternatives. Early expression of regional markers, measurements of cell death and proliferation, and later lineage tracing could provide complementary evidence. If distal molecular identities initially appear but the corresponding tissue disappears later, the loss of the mature structure need not imply that identity was never specified.

The zone of polarizing activity and the logic of transplantation

A normal limb has an asymmetric arrangement across its width. Classical chick experiments identified a region of posterior mesenchyme that can influence this arrangement. The zone of polarizing activity (ZPA) is located near the posterior margin of the early bud, rather than in the epithelial ridge at its distal tip. A posterior tissue graft placed at the anterior margin of a recipient bud can produce extra digits whose identities are organized approximately as a reversed copy of the normal series. A graft from an inappropriate nonpolarizing region is a useful control because it can reveal whether duplication merely results from adding tissue or injuring the host.

The term polarizing activity was initially an operational definition: a region was recognized by the pattern it could induce when moved. The original experiment did not immediately identify a molecular signal. It did, however, constrain the kind of explanation that would be acceptable. A diffusible or otherwise locally transmitted influence from posterior tissue could reorganize nearby host cells. If grafted posterior cells merely differentiated into an extra piece of posterior skeleton, they would not readily explain a spatially organized sequence of multiple distinct identities in the recipient.

In 1993, Riddle, Johnson, Laufer and Tabin linked polarizing activity to the expression and experimental activity of Sonic hedgehog (Shh). This connected tissue-level manipulation with a candidate secreted signaling protein. Ectopic expression of Shh in an appropriate anterior limb context could reproduce a characteristic polarizing effect. The strongest interpretation combines location, perturbation and functional evidence rather than relying on any single observation. Expression in the ZPA identifies a plausible source; an ectopic-expression experiment tests whether the candidate can generate a new organizing influence; loss-of-function experiments address the requirement for normal patterning.

Retinoic acid had previously been found capable of producing polarizing effects in limb experiments. An important lesson from the subsequent molecular work is that a substance capable of inducing a new polarizing region need not be the normal instructive signal emitted by that region. Retinoic acid can induce ectopic Shh expression under experimental conditions. Consequently, a retinoic acid implant and a Shh-expressing graft can produce superficially similar outcomes while operating at different steps in the causal sequence. This is a general example of why phenotypic similarity is insufficient to establish molecular equivalence.

How SHH signaling encodes position and time

SHH is produced as a precursor that undergoes processing to generate a signaling fragment with lipid modifications. These features affect how the protein associates with cells and distributes through tissues. The developmental range of SHH cannot therefore be inferred simply from the free diffusion of a small unmodified solute in water. Production, movement, extracellular interactions and removal influence the signal available to responding cells.

In a simplified description of the pathway, Patched restrains the activity of Smoothened when Hedgehog signal is absent. Binding of SHH relieves that restraint and alters the balance of GLI transcriptional regulators. In limb mesenchyme, GLI3 repressor activity is an especially important component of the AP patterning system. Anterior regions normally experience less SHH-mediated inhibition of repressor formation than posterior regions. A shift in the balance between activation and repression changes target gene expression; the receptor is therefore not itself a digit identity determinant in isolation.

It is tempting to draw five concentration bands and assign one adult digit to each band. This diagram may be useful as a first exercise in threshold interpretation, but it must not be confused with a universally valid map of mammalian digit specification. Duration of exposure, cell proliferation, changing tissue geometry and the lineage of Shh-expressing cells also matter. Posterior digits can receive long exposure to SHH, including exposure experienced by descendants of the source territory, while other cells respond at a distance. The relationship between a measured protein concentration at one instant and final skeletal identity depends on the developmental context.

A striking consequence of this pathway is that reduced SHH activity and inappropriate anterior SHH activity can produce very different classes of abnormalities. Reduced activity can impair posterior digit development and overall pattern; ectopic anterior activity may create additional digit identities. Changes in GLI3 can alter digit number and pattern even when one cannot describe the phenotype as a simple uniform change in SHH concentration. Genetic analysis should therefore distinguish ligand production, receptor-mediated signal transduction, transcriptional repression and the final morphogenetic response. The causal position of a disrupted gene helps determine what kind of rescue would be informative.

A gradient also does not necessarily exist because the source cells possess different concentrations of a molecule. A localized source can produce a signal that decreases with distance as molecules spread and are removed or retained. Cells in an anterior region can thus receive a signal made elsewhere without becoming SHH-producing cells themselves. A reporter for transcriptional response may have a different spatial pattern from a reporter for Shh transcription. A well-designed experiment uses the appropriate kind of measurement for the question being asked.

Coordination of the AER and ZPA

The three axes of a limb are convenient for analysis, but they are not governed by completely independent biological machines. The distal ectoderm and posterior mesenchyme communicate. Signals from the AER help sustain the posterior signaling environment, and SHH-dependent mesenchymal responses help preserve ridge activity. In a widely taught framework, SHH supports expression of the BMP antagonist Gremlin1, which reduces inhibitory BMP effects on ridge FGF maintenance. Ridge FGFs in turn support the SHH-producing domain. The relationship couples the provision of AP patterning information to the expansion of cells along the PD axis.

A BMP antagonist binds or otherwise inhibits the action of bone morphogenetic proteins in an appropriate extracellular context. Gremlin1 is not an FGF receptor and is not a substitute for SHH at Patched. Its importance in this circuit is that it modifies a third signaling system. Inhibition of an inhibitory influence can have a positive net effect. Thus if Gremlin reduces BMP-mediated inhibition of ridge activity, the overall link between Gremlin and ridge FGF maintenance is positive even though the molecular action of Gremlin is antagonistic. This distinction is frequently tested by pathway diagrams in which two negative arrows yield a positive net relationship.

As the bud grows, feedback eventually changes and terminates. Shh-expressing cells and their descendants expand, and the regions in which regulatory signals can be maintained change. Feedback cannot remain a simple self-amplifying cycle indefinitely. The particular contributions of different FGF family members, BMPs and Gremlin change with species, position and stage. A compact exam diagram should capture the relevant net relationships without pretending to specify every molecular interaction or a constant rate of feedback.

Consider an experiment in which loss of a mesenchymal gene decreases both Shh and AER Fgf expression. It would be premature to infer that the gene directly activates both loci. The primary defect might reduce SHH-dependent maintenance of Gremlin, followed by loss of ridge activity and further decline in SHH. A time course that identifies which molecular change occurs first, combined with a rescue using an independently restored signaling input, is more informative than a single late measurement. Feedback makes correlation particularly difficult to interpret because an upstream perturbation can propagate around the circuit and affect its own apparent cause.

Dorsoventral polarity, Wnt7a and Lmx1b

A developing limb requires more than a sequence of bones. Tendon arrangements, joints, surfaces and associated structures must acquire appropriate dorsal or ventral organization. An important instructive signal in the dorsal ectoderm is WNT7A. It influences the expression of a LIM homeodomain transcription factor, known as Lmx1 in classical chick experiments and commonly discussed as Lmx1b in mammalian limb patterning. This factor is expressed in dorsal mesenchyme, showing that the molecular response occurs in tissue beneath the signal source.

The 1995 work of Riddle and colleagues connected WNT7A and Lmx1 expression to dorsoventral pattern. The experiment helps distinguish the act of specifying a dorsal fate from merely moving cells to a dorsal position. If a dorsal-type transcriptional program is induced ectopically in an appropriately competent ventral region, that region can express features inconsistent with its usual positional identity. Conversely, loss of a dorsal-promoting activity may lead to ventralization. The precise extent of a phenotype depends on the perturbation and the developmental stage.

Engrailed-1 (En1) is normally associated with ventral limb ectoderm and helps restrict the dorsal Wnt7a expression program. The resulting boundary contributes to the proper spatial organization of ectodermal and mesenchymal signals. An En1 perturbation is therefore not equivalent to simply deleting Wnt7a: removing a restriction can broaden the domain of a signal, whereas deleting the signal reduces its activity. The ridge is established at a properly organized dorsoventral boundary, making DV patterning relevant to the outgrowth system as well.

WNT7A also participates in maintenance of posterior patterning in some model contexts. An exam statement that describes WNT7A as involved in dorsal identity is accurate; a statement that it can never affect AP outcomes is too restrictive. Network components may influence more than one developmental output. To identify the main direct outcome of a perturbation, compare early marker distributions before the secondary defects have had time to propagate through the network.

Hox programs, skeletal differentiation and digit separation

Hox genes encode transcription factors with DNA-binding homeodomains. The developmental effects of these proteins arise from regulated combinations of target genes rather than from their incorporation into the final skeletal material. HoxA and HoxD genes have important roles in limb patterning. Their expression changes over time and occupies characteristic territories, with posterior Hox genes such as Hoxa13 and Hoxd13 associated with distal appendage development. Overlapping functions and context-dependent regulation make it inappropriate to map each Hox gene to exactly one mature bone.

Regional identity must be translated into morphology. Mesenchymal cells gather into condensations in positions that anticipate many skeletal elements. Cells within these regions activate chondrogenic differentiation programs, including Sox9-dependent regulation, and establish cartilaginous templates for many limb bones. The subsequent replacement of much of this cartilage by bone during endochondral ossification is a later differentiation process, not the initial mechanism by which PD or AP information is established. Failure to make cartilage can obscure a positional pattern that was established earlier at the level of gene expression.

The distal limb initially resembles a paddle rather than a set of fully separate fingers. The appearance of individual digits requires the removal or remodeling of tissue between the developing rays. Programmed cell death is a major mechanism of interdigital tissue elimination in many tetrapods. BMP signaling and the local decline of survival-promoting FGF inputs contribute to this process. A retained web can result from altered interdigital survival even if digit number and identity are normal. Therefore webbing should not automatically be interpreted as a failure of SHH-mediated AP patterning.

The amount of interdigital tissue retained varies among species. A duck's webbed foot illustrates that retaining tissue between digit rays can be a normal developmental outcome rather than a general pathological failure. Evolutionary comparisons should identify the particular cellular process being modified. An altered web, a missing skeletal element and a duplicated digit are different observations and can arise from different changes in the developmental system. The underlying signaling components may be shared while their spatial and temporal regulation differs.

Experimental reasoning: separating necessary, sufficient and permissive effects

Developmental biology often makes causal inferences from comparisons between embryos that differ in one defined manipulation. A removal experiment tests necessity under its conditions: if excising a signaling region disrupts a process, the region contributes something required for normal execution of that process. However, removal also alters physical integrity and cell numbers. A sham manipulation and a suitable tissue removal control help distinguish the consequence of losing a particular signal from a general consequence of tissue damage.

An addition experiment tests whether a region or molecular input can promote an outcome in the tested context. A ZPA graft that generates a second AP pattern provides evidence for instructive polarizing activity. It does not establish that the graft could produce the same pattern at every developmental stage or at any arbitrary site in the embryo. An appropriately responsive host is required. A failure to produce an effect can thus reflect lack of competence rather than lack of biological activity of the transplanted tissue.

A rescue experiment combines perturbation and restoration. If loss of ridge function causes a defect and provision of an FGF source restores much of the outcome, the experiment implicates FGF-responsive processes in that defect. Restoration of length alone is weaker evidence for full pattern rescue than restoration of recognizable skeletal identities, relative positions and appropriate tissue differentiation. Both quantitative and morphological observations should be recorded before making a claim of complete rescue.

Lineage tracing answers a different question. It establishes where descendants of a labeled population appear, whereas a signaling experiment asks what influence that population exerts. Imagine that a labeled posterior graft induces additional digits largely formed by unlabeled host mesenchyme. The result supports the idea that the graft changes host development. If the labeled cells form a portion of the extra skeleton, this does not negate signaling; the tissue could both contribute descendants and instruct neighboring cells. The informative comparison concerns the extent and arrangement of these contributions.

A quantitative model for positional information

An elementary reaction-diffusion model can clarify how a localized signal creates positional differences. Let x represent distance from a posterior source into a one-dimensional strip of mesenchyme. Suppose a signal has effective diffusion coefficient D and is removed at a rate proportional to its local concentration C, with first-order constant k. At steady state, conservation of signal gives D times the second spatial derivative of C minus kC equal to zero. The first term describes net redistribution and the second describes local loss.

The decaying solution for a semi-infinite domain with a maintained concentration C0 at x equal to zero and concentration tending to zero far away is C(x) = C0 exp(-x/λ), where λ = √(D/k). D has units of length squared per time and k has units of inverse time, making λ a length. The characteristic distance λ describes where concentration falls to about 37 percent of the source value. A larger effective diffusion coefficient extends the range in this model, while faster removal shortens it.

If a particular response requires concentration at least T, its boundary under this model is at x = λ ln(C0/T), provided T is positive and no greater than C0. This relationship follows by dividing the concentration equation by C0 and taking natural logarithms. It makes a testable idealized prediction: a twofold increase in source concentration shifts a fixed response boundary by λ ln 2, not by a factor of two in distance. The shift is identical for different fixed thresholds in this simple exponential profile.

This calculation is a model of positional information, not a measured SHH profile in a specific limb. The actual limb is finite, growing, heterogeneous and subject to feedback. SHH is modified and interacts with cellular components, and signal duration and changes in competence are biologically important. Even so, the model makes the concept of a threshold explicit and distinguishes source strength from signal range. An experiment showing a wider response domain could reflect altered transport or removal, a stronger source, or a change in response threshold. Additional measurements are necessary to distinguish these explanations.

Worked quantitative examples

Characteristic length

Hypothetical model: D = 16 μm²/min, k = 0.04 min⁻¹. Then λ = √(16/0.04) μm = 20 μm. At 40 μm from the source, C/C0 = exp(-2) ≈ 0.135. These values illustrate a model and are not empirical SHH measurements.

Threshold shift

For λ = 20 μm and C0/T = 4, the response boundary is 20 ln(4) = 27.73 μm. Doubling C0 gives 20 ln(8) = 41.59 μm, a shift of 13.86 μm. This assumes steady state, fixed threshold and unchanging transport and removal.

Misconceptions

AER is posterior mesenchyme

AER is distal ectoderm; ZPA is posterior mesenchyme.

All limb tissues come from lateral plate

Muscle progenitors arise primarily from somites.

AER cells form all distal bones

Ridge signals regulate mesenchymal development.

FGF provides metabolic energy

FGF regulates growth and survival; it does not fuel them.

Tbx5 alone specifies every forelimb feature

Initiation and final identity are distinct.

AER removal proves a universal timer

Cell survival and progenitor expansion offer additional explanations.

ZPA graft produces only donor-derived digits

Host cells can respond to graft signals.

Retinoic acid is necessarily the normal ZPA signal

Experimental retinoic acid can induce Shh.

Patched activates SMO in the absence of ligand

Patched restrains SMO.

All five digits correspond to five fixed SHH thresholds

Exposure time and lineage also matter.

WNT7A is a posterior signal

Its familiar limb source is dorsal ectoderm.

En1 loss and Wnt7a loss are equivalent

Loss of a restriction differs from loss of its target.

HOX genes encode cartilage

They encode transcription factors.

Webbing means extra digits

Interdigital tissue retention can occur without altered digit number.

All three axes develop independently

Their signaling centers communicate.

Original examination practice

Part B 1. Which structure is the epithelial thickening at the distal bud?

  1. AER
  2. ZPA
  3. Somite
  4. Stylopod
Answer and explanation

A. AER. The AER is distal ectoderm. The ZPA is posterior mesenchyme; a somite supplies muscle progenitors and the stylopod is a skeletal region.

Part B 2. Which embryonic population supplies most limb muscle progenitors?

  1. Surface ectoderm
  2. Somitic mesoderm
  3. Endoderm
  4. AER
Answer and explanation

B. Somitic mesoderm. Somites derive from paraxial mesoderm; ectoderm forms the epidermis and signaling ridge, not the principal muscle population.

Part B 3. Which ligand is characteristic of the ZPA?

  1. FGF8
  2. WNT7A
  3. SHH
  4. BMP antagonist Gremlin1
Answer and explanation

C. SHH. SHH is the classic posterior source. FGF8 is associated with the AER and WNT7A with dorsal ectoderm; Gremlin1 mediates feedback.

Part B 4. Which region contains radius and ulna?

  1. Stylopod
  2. Autopod
  3. AER
  4. Zeugopod
Answer and explanation

D. Zeugopod. The zeugopod is the intermediate paired-bone region. Stylopod is proximal, autopod distal and AER epithelial.

Part B 5. Which activity is restrained by Patched without SHH?

  1. Smoothened
  2. FGFR2b
  3. Lmx1b
  4. Sox9
Answer and explanation

A. Smoothened. Patched restrains Smoothened in the Hedgehog pathway; the other factors belong to separate receptor or transcriptional programs.

Part B 6. What is the principal source of WNT7A in the classic limb model?

  1. Posterior mesenchyme
  2. Dorsal ectoderm
  3. Somites
  4. Interdigital cartilage
Answer and explanation

B. Dorsal ectoderm. Dorsal ectoderm produces WNT7A; the dorsal mesenchyme expresses downstream Lmx1b.

Part B 7. Which factor antagonizes BMP in the feedback network?

  1. GLI3
  2. FGF10
  3. Gremlin1
  4. Tbx5
Answer and explanation

C. Gremlin1. Gremlin1 reduces BMP activity, rather than acting as a Hedgehog transcriptional regulator, FGF ligand or limb initiation factor.

Part B 8. What is a characteristic role of HOX proteins?

  1. ATP synthesis
  2. Cartilage polymerization
  3. Direct receptor phosphorylation
  4. Transcriptional regulation
Answer and explanation

D. Transcriptional regulation. HOX proteins are DNA-binding transcription factors; they are not structural cartilage proteins or metabolic enzymes.

Part C 9. Early ridge removal eliminates distal elements, but early distal markers were present. Which interpretation remains possible?

  1. Distal progenitors failed to survive or expand
  2. Distal identity was necessarily never specified
  3. AER is the sole source of cartilage
  4. ZPA must have moved anteriorly
Answer and explanation

A. Distal progenitors failed to survive or expand. Markers preceding tissue loss suggest initial specification can occur. The final skeleton alone cannot distinguish survival from fate changes.

Part C 10. An anterior graft induces reversed digits, mostly unlabeled host tissue. What does this support?

  1. Only autonomous donor differentiation
  2. A non-cell-autonomous polarizing influence
  3. Complete absence of host competence
  4. Direct conversion of ectoderm to all bones
Answer and explanation

B. A non-cell-autonomous polarizing influence. The host contribution shows neighboring recipient cells were reorganized; the donor need not supply every induced structure.

Part C 11. An FGF source restores length after AER excision but digit identities remain abnormal. What is supported?

  1. Complete AER rescue
  2. FGF cannot influence limb development
  3. Partial rescue of outgrowth, not complete pattern rescue
  4. All digit identities depend only on length
Answer and explanation

C. Partial rescue of outgrowth, not complete pattern rescue. Length and pattern are different outputs. Recovery of one cannot establish restoration of the other.

Part C 12. If D is unchanged and k becomes fourfold larger in the ideal exponential model, what happens to λ?

  1. It quadruples
  2. It doubles
  3. It stays constant
  4. It halves
Answer and explanation

D. It halves. λ = √(D/k). Increasing k by four decreases λ by √4 = 2; the other options confuse the square-root relation.

Part C 13. A mutation expands anterior Shh expression. Which finding most directly supports altered AP organization?

  1. Reversed additional digit identities
  2. Uniform reduction of all bones
  3. Only retained interdigital skin
  4. Only dorsal epidermal changes
Answer and explanation

A. Reversed additional digit identities. Extra anterior polarizing input can reorganize AP digit order. The other findings may concern growth, tissue elimination or DV pattern.

Part C 14. A perturbation reduces both AER FGF and Shh at a late stage. Which inference is justified?

  1. The factor necessarily binds both promoters
  2. Direct regulation of both genes is not established
  3. The AER is the ZPA
  4. Neither gene influences the other
Answer and explanation

B. Direct regulation of both genes is not established. Feedback can propagate one primary defect to multiple domains; a time course and selective rescue can clarify causality.

Part C 15. A ventral ectoderm mutation permits ectopic Wnt7a and Lmx1b. Which result is expected?

  1. All dorsal characteristics must vanish
  2. Only forelimb initiation is affected
  3. Dorsal characteristics may appear ventrally
  4. The radius must become the humerus
Answer and explanation

C. Dorsal characteristics may appear ventrally. Expanding a dorsal signal can expand the dorsal program; the other options confuse loss with ectopic activity or unrelated axes.

Part C 16. A mutant has five distinct skeletal digit rays but persistent tissue between them. Which initial hypothesis best matches the phenotype?

  1. Failure to establish any AP identity
  2. Complete absence of the limb field
  3. Loss of all AER signals before initiation
  4. Altered interdigital tissue elimination
Answer and explanation

D. Altered interdigital tissue elimination. Normal ray count suggests tissue separation should be investigated first. The other hypotheses predict substantially broader defects.

Revision and active recall

Limb development coordinates regional identity, growth and differentiation using reciprocal tissue signals. Distinguish AER, ZPA and dorsal ectoderm, and test your understanding by predicting the effect of each manipulation.

  1. Name the three axes.
  2. Identify the skeletal regions in sequence.
  3. Explain the lateral plate and somite contributions.
  4. Explain how FGF10 and FGF8 cooperate.
  5. Interpret early and late AER removal.
  6. Explain an anterior ZPA graft.
  7. Describe Patched and Smoothened regulation.
  8. Explain why Gremlin supports ridge activity.
  9. Compare WNT7A and EN1 roles.
