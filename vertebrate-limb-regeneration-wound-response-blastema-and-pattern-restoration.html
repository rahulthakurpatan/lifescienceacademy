1. The biological problem: replace a missing limb, not simply close a wound

A limb is an organized system of living tissues. Its skeleton provides support and leverage, its muscles produce force, its nerves transmit information, and its blood vessels supply oxygen and nutrients. Tendons link muscle to skeletal elements, while the skin protects the underlying tissues. These components are useful only when they are arranged in the right relationship to each other. A bone that grows across a joint, for instance, may prevent movement even if the bone itself is healthy. A successful replacement limb must therefore restore both the materials and the spatial organization of the original appendage.

Most injuries in vertebrates are followed by healing, but healing and regeneration do not have identical endpoints. A superficial skin wound can be covered by new epithelial cells. The repair of a deeper injury often involves connective tissue deposition, restoration of a protective barrier and mechanical strengthening. Such repair can be highly beneficial for survival without reproducing the original microscopic architecture. An amputated limb presents a much larger problem: entire joints, groups of muscles, nerve branches, skeletal elements and skin territories may be absent. Rapid closure of the cut surface is only the first requirement.

The Mexican axolotl, Ambystoma mexicanum, and several newt species possess a remarkable ability to reconstruct missing limb parts after amputation. A stump at the wrist can produce the missing hand, whereas one cut nearer the shoulder can produce the forearm and hand as well as missing portions of the upper arm. In a normal outcome, the regenerate joins the retained tissue and does not produce a second copy of the whole limb starting at the shoulder. This is the simplest evidence that regrowth is constrained by the level of injury. A mechanism that merely increased the rate of mitosis would not automatically solve this constraint.

Regeneration of a complex appendage is often described as epimorphic regeneration. The term refers here to the construction of missing structures through a new growth region containing proliferative cells. The mesenchymal growth region in salamander limbs is termed a blastema. Mesenchymal refers to a cellular organization in which cells are embedded in a surrounding extracellular environment rather than arranged as a continuous epithelial sheet. An epithelial covering participates in the process, but it is not synonymous with the mesenchymal blastema underneath it. Both compartments are necessary to understand the developing structure.

In contrast, the classical term morphallaxis describes restoration of body organization largely by rearranging existing tissues, with relatively little new cell proliferation in its idealized definition. Hydra is often used to illustrate this idea, although actual regeneration in many organisms involves both proliferation and rearrangement. Neither term should be treated as an assertion that the entire process uses only one cellular behavior. Salamander appendage regrowth combines cell migration, local tissue remodeling, proliferation and differentiation; it is the prominent formation of a growth region that makes the epimorphic classification useful.

Compensatory growth is another related but distinct concept. When part of the mammalian liver is removed, the remaining liver tissue can increase in mass and restore much of the organ's functional capacity. The original missing lobes are not necessarily recreated with their former anatomy. Such growth demonstrates that adult vertebrate cells can respond vigorously to injury, but it does not prove that they can organize a whole appendage. The comparison reveals why proliferation must be evaluated independently from pattern.

To study a regenerating limb, it is helpful to divide the task into four interacting questions. First, how does the injury become a viable, protected wound? Second, which cells provide the material for the replacement? Third, what permits this cellular population to grow? Finally, what determines which tissues form at particular locations and when growth should change into differentiation? Each question can be addressed experimentally. The answers are not four entirely independent processes, because the wound environment influences both cell recruitment and growth, and pattern formation affects the eventual geometry of the tissue.

A regenerated appendage need not be a molecularly exact copy of every original cell. A muscle fiber can be constructed from new nuclei and new cytoplasm while performing the function of the lost fiber. A branch of a nerve can grow from surviving neuronal cell bodies. Restoring a structure therefore does not require resurrecting cells that were lost at the injury. It requires the recruitment of surviving cells and the production of appropriate descendants. This distinction explains why the study of cell lineage and the study of final anatomy are complementary rather than interchangeable.

The main conceptual task in this chapter is to relate a local response at the stump to the restoration of an entire patterned structure. Throughout the chapter, an intact regenerated limb, a proliferative blastema and a closed wound will be treated as separate outcomes. An experiment may succeed at one and fail at another. Such intermediate outcomes are valuable because they identify the step at which a developmental mechanism has been interrupted.

2. Classical evidence and the development of regenerative biology

In the eighteenth century, observations of regeneration challenged the idea that an animal's organs were fixed once formed. Lazzaro Spallanzani's work on salamanders helped establish that amputated vertebrate appendages could be rebuilt. The striking observation was not simply that tissue grew at the injury. The newly produced appendage had recognizable anatomical subdivisions. Observations of this kind made regeneration an experimental problem in developmental biology rather than merely a curiosity of natural history.

Early experiments necessarily relied on visible anatomy. The investigator could compare the shape of a limb before injury, the changing stump and the final replacement. If one part of a structure was removed and a predictable replacement formed, the result suggested that some surviving tissue contained the information necessary for restoration. However, this evidence alone could not establish whether the information resided in a particular cell population, the nerves, the skin or interactions between them. Anatomical observations generated hypotheses; tissue manipulation and cell labeling were needed to distinguish the alternatives.

A particularly useful series of experiments changed the level at which a limb was amputated. A proximal cut removed more of the original structure than a distal cut. If every wound activated a fixed limb-building program that always produced an entire appendage, both cuts would be expected to yield the same series of newly generated skeletal elements. Instead, regrowth usually corresponded to the missing territory. This established a relationship between the retained tissue and the distal pattern of the regenerate. It did not yet identify the molecular carrier of positional information.

Experiments on nerve dependence asked a different question. In classic work, Marcus Singer and others demonstrated that the regenerative response of salamander limbs depended strongly on adequate innervation. In a denervated limb, the cut surface could still become covered, but formation or continued growth of a blastema was impaired. This separated closure from appendage reconstruction. The results also indicated that peripheral nerves have a function beyond the transmission of sensory impulses and motor commands. Growing tissue can depend on signals provided by nervous tissue.

It is important not to equate the nerve-dependence result with proof that a nerve contains a miniature map of the complete limb. Removing nerves affects multiple aspects of the regenerative environment, including growth support, cellular communication and interactions with the wound covering. If a blastema fails to enlarge, it cannot be used to decide whether the cells retained their positional identities. A failure at an early step conceals the outcome of later steps. This logic is common to experiments on organ induction: removal of a necessary input can abolish an organ without showing that the input encodes every feature of the organ.

Another approach employed grafting. A graft is tissue transferred from one location to another, often so that the origin and destination possess different developmental identities. In regeneration studies, skin and connective tissue grafts helped test whether cells from opposite sides or different levels of a limb behave differently when brought together. An abnormal pattern at the junction between two positional territories can reveal information that is invisible in an undisturbed limb. The experiment is not primarily a test of whether the graft survives; the question concerns how the surviving graft and host cells affect the organization of newly formed tissue.

Modern cell labeling supplied a way to test the origin of the regenerated parts. In a lineage-tracing experiment, a cell population and its descendants are identified by a persistent marker. A marker might be encoded genetically or otherwise restricted to a population whose starting location is known. The important property is that the label allows the investigator to distinguish the descendants of the initial cells from their neighbors after regeneration. The resulting evidence can reveal whether one adult tissue generates many different kinds of regenerate tissue or whether it primarily produces a restricted set.

A lineage marker should not be confused with a marker of a cell's present identity. If a mature muscle cell activates a gene characteristic of proliferating progenitors, it has changed its current expression state. Unless a durable label also identifies its original lineage, an observer cannot know whether that cell was formerly a mature muscle cell or a resident progenitor that had been present nearby. This distinction became particularly important when classical explanations of blastema formation were revisited using genetic fate mapping.

Research on the axolotl showed that the blastema does not represent a universally interchangeable cell population. Kragl and colleagues in 2009 traced labeled tissues and reported restrictions in their contributions to the regenerate. A cell can change shape, migrate, proliferate and alter gene expression while still retaining restrictions inherited from its tissue of origin. This result revised older oversimplified descriptions of the blastema as a mass of completely undifferentiated cells.

Comparative fate mapping subsequently demonstrated that even closely related salamanders may use distinct cellular routes to regenerate skeletal muscle. Sandoval-Guzmán and colleagues reported differences between axolotl and adult newt muscle regeneration in 2014. Consequently, the broad observation that a limb regenerates does not imply that each species recruits the same progenitor populations. Developmental biology increasingly links a particular experimental conclusion to its organism and stage.

These historical steps illustrate how scientific explanations become more precise. Visible anatomy established the phenomenon; level-specific amputation revealed pattern restriction; denervation distinguished growth support from wound closure; grafts tested positional interaction; lineage tracing separated recruitment, dedifferentiation and tissue origin. A strong answer to an examination question should state not only the outcome but also which interpretation the experimental design permits and which alternatives remain untested.

3. Anatomical coordinates and the retained stump

A limb has three major anatomical axes. The proximodistal axis extends from the body toward the tip. The anteroposterior axis distinguishes opposite sides of the appendage, corresponding in a typical tetrapod forelimb to the preaxial and postaxial margins. The dorsoventral axis separates the dorsal and ventral surfaces. The exact visible orientation of a limb depends on its posture and species, so the coordinates should be defined anatomically rather than by the apparent left and right sides of a photograph.

Along the proximodistal axis, a typical tetrapod limb is divided into a stylopod, a zeugopod and an autopod. In the forelimb the stylopod contains the humerus, the zeugopod contains the radius and ulna, and the autopod includes the wrist and digits. The corresponding hindlimb uses the femur, the tibia and fibula, and the ankle and digits. These terms describe segments, not developmental cell types. A nerve branch, for example, extends through more than one segment and cannot be assigned to the skeleton simply because it shares a position with it.

The retained portion after amputation is called the stump. It includes differentiated cells and extracellular matrix that were formed during the animal's original limb development. Its cut surface exposes a cross-section of tissues, and the location of the cut determines which anatomical identities remain nearby. The stump is not an inert platform. Its living tissues are the immediate source of many regenerative progenitors, as well as a source of cues influencing the new appendage.

Imagine a simplified limb that has three segments arranged from proximal to distal. If the cut passes through the distal segment, the proximal and intermediate segments remain. If it passes through the proximal segment, only the proximal stump remains. A replacement limited to the missing territory requires different final products in these two situations. The existence of an injury at both sites is not sufficient to explain the difference. The injury must be interpreted in the context of position.

The concept of positional identity provides one explanation for this context. It describes a cell or tissue's capacity to participate in location-appropriate pattern formation. Positional identity is not equivalent to differentiated identity. Two dermal fibroblasts may share broad morphological and biochemical properties while behaving differently when transplanted between proximal and distal limb territories. Conversely, several types of tissue occupy the same axial level in an intact limb without necessarily storing or using that positional information in the same way.

Positional value is a convenient term in models of pattern formation. It may describe an assigned location along an axis or around the circumference of the limb. In classical diagrams, values are often represented by numbers. Those numbers are conceptual labels, not measured units of a physical substance. Unless a real molecular concentration or other measurement is explicitly given, it is incorrect to treat positional values as concentrations, distances or electrical potentials. The conceptual usefulness of the model lies in predicting which identities are missing when different tissues meet.

Several developmental genes, including Hox genes, participate in specifying regional identities in vertebrate limbs. During salamander regrowth, some gene-expression domains associated with limb development reappear. Yet the presence of an expression domain does not establish that its encoded protein alone constitutes the permanent memory of position. A positional state may be maintained by multiple transcriptional and regulatory features and can be modified by signaling interactions. Expression patterns are useful evidence only when their interpretation is restricted to what was measured.

The three axial coordinates impose distinct requirements on the regenerate. A structure may extend to the appropriate length but have an abnormal arrangement of digits. Another may contain recognizable digits but be incorrectly oriented. Yet another may have normal external shape but defective skeletal joints or nerve supply. These examples show why an overall measurement such as regenerated length cannot establish correct patterning in all dimensions.

In classical graft experiments the investigator must record both the original orientation of a graft and its orientation after transfer. A piece of posterior skin transplanted to an anterior site carries a different positional history than a piece of anterior skin transplanted within the same territory. It also creates a new boundary with host tissue. Failure to distinguish source identity from recipient location makes the interpretation ambiguous. The same principle applies to proximal-distal transplants.

It is reasonable to ask whether an injury signal alone can tell a stump where to stop growing. Injury-associated signals provide information about damage, but similar classes of signals can be produced by many wounds. Local positional cues and interactions between different regenerative populations are required to account for the anatomical specificity. This chapter therefore treats injury detection, growth permission and pattern specification as distinguishable functions, even when one signaling pathway contributes to more than one of them.

4. Early wound response: from damaged tissue to a protective covering

Immediately after a limb is amputated, a cut surface contains disrupted cells, exposed extracellular matrix and damaged vessels. Surviving tissue must respond to local changes in its chemical and physical environment. Released intracellular substances can act as damage signals. Bleeding and fluid loss must be limited, and damaged material must be removed or remodeled. These early events create the environment in which a regenerative response can begin.

Wound closure is an important first transition. Epidermal cells at the margins migrate across the exposed surface and establish a new epithelial covering. This does not mean that a complete differentiated skin with its normal connective tissue and all specialized components is produced immediately. The initial covering has a protective and signaling role and differs from mature skin. Migration permits a relatively rapid redistribution of cells; subsequent proliferation and differentiation can contribute to the restoration of normal epithelial architecture.

Cell migration requires changes in the relationship between a cell and its surroundings. The actin cytoskeleton supports protrusion and contraction, while adhesion proteins transmit forces to adjacent cells and the extracellular matrix. Cells in the moving epidermal sheet can coordinate their movement across a surface. The matrix is both a mechanical substrate and a source of biochemical cues. If adhesion is insufficient, cells cannot generate effective traction; if adhesion is excessive or the matrix cannot be remodeled, movement may also be restricted. The biological response depends on a balance between attachment and release.

The formation of a continuous covering can be assessed separately from deeper regrowth. One can ask whether a wound is completely epithelialized at a particular time and whether a mesenchymal blastema develops subsequently. The existence of a covering in a failed regenerate proves that epithelial continuity alone is insufficient. To go beyond correlation, the investigator would need to manipulate the properties of the covering while controlling for wound size, tissue viability and other inputs.

Inflammation also begins after tissue injury. It includes signals that recruit immune cells and alter the behavior of cells in damaged tissues. An immune response can assist in eliminating microbes and clearing cellular debris. It can also influence matrix deposition and the local growth environment. It would therefore be misleading to describe successful regeneration as a process in which the immune system remains entirely inactive. The timing, composition and resolution of the response matter.

Macrophages are especially important in this respect. They are phagocytic immune cells that can remove debris and produce factors affecting surrounding tissues. In a study published in 2013, Godwin, Pinto and Rosenthal examined macrophage involvement in axolotl limb regeneration and found that disrupting the early macrophage contribution impaired regeneration and favored fibrotic repair. The conclusion is stronger than an observation of macrophages in a normal wound because the experimental perturbation tested their contribution to the outcome.

That finding should not be simplified into the claim that every inflammatory cell promotes regeneration under every condition. Macrophage activity can change across time and environments. Excessive or persistent inflammatory signaling can damage tissue and influence fibrosis in other contexts. A depletion study also does not show that a single macrophage-derived molecule is sufficient to produce a new limb. The role of the early immune response is to establish an environment compatible with the coordinated events that follow.

Connective tissue normally contains structural proteins, notably collagens, together with adhesive proteins, proteoglycans and other matrix components. In a mature limb these materials organize cells and contribute to mechanical strength. After injury some components must be reorganized so that progenitors can move and tissue can change shape. Matrix metalloproteinases are a family of extracellular protein-degrading enzymes that can participate in such remodeling. Their activity can create paths through tissue and change the availability of matrix-associated signals. Controlled remodeling differs fundamentally from indiscriminate destruction of all extracellular material.

A mature scar can form a stable physical barrier, but dense collagen deposition and changes in cell behavior may impede the migration and interactions required for large-scale regrowth. In the regeneration-competent salamander wound, matrix turnover and epithelial-mesenchymal interactions permit a different developmental trajectory. This comparison does not justify the claim that collagen is bad. Collagen is necessary in the mature regenerate, and the same material can have different consequences depending on timing, organization and tissue context.

Injury also creates altered demands for energy and oxygen. Migrating cells must use ATP to reorganize their cytoskeleton, proliferating cells must synthesize new biomolecules, and dying or damaged tissue must be cleared. Blood vessel repair and later vascular growth help sustain the regenerate. Early tissue organization cannot be predicted solely by the available energy, however. ATP is a necessary resource for cellular activity, not an instruction telling a cell which digit or segment to form. In examination questions, metabolic support and positional information must remain conceptually distinct.

The immediate wound response thus creates a necessary but not sufficient starting condition. A covered and viable stump can proceed toward regeneration only when deeper tissue recruitment, appropriate signals and positional interactions occur. The next step is to understand why the newly formed epidermis acquires a special role at the growing tip.

5. Wound epithelium and the apical epithelial cap

The wound epithelium is the epidermal covering established over the amputated surface. In a regeneration-competent salamander limb, it participates in the formation of a specialized distal epithelial region called the apical epithelial cap, abbreviated AEC. The AEC lies at the tip of the developing regenerate and interacts with the underlying mesenchyme. It is not a collection of skeletal progenitors. Its location and epithelial organization distinguish it from the blastema that develops underneath.

The term apical refers to the tip or distal surface in this context. The word cap describes the tissue's position above the growth region rather than a rigid mechanical structure. The AEC is composed of living epithelial cells capable of communicating with underlying tissues and receiving signals from the regenerative environment. Its significance lies in the interactions it establishes. A thick layer of skin at any site is not automatically an AEC.

The distinction between wound epithelium and mature skin is central to classical reasoning. Mature skin is a complex structure containing epidermis, basement membrane, dermis and associated cell populations. A regenerative wound covering initially does not reproduce all of these layers in their final configuration. This permits specialized interactions with the underlying cells. If normal skin architecture were established too early, it could restrict communication between epithelium and mesenchyme. Thus restoration of the protective barrier need not be equivalent to restoration of mature tissue structure.

Signals from nerves help establish a regeneration-supportive wound environment. Nerve fibers approaching the wound can interact with epithelial and mesenchymal cells, influencing the formation and maintenance of the specialized epithelial covering. The resulting relationship is reciprocal in the broad sense that the condition of the local tissue affects the signals encountered by growing nerves and the signals from nervous tissue affect the responding cells. There is no requirement to assume that all nerve effects are mediated by a single cell type or factor.

FGF signaling provides a familiar example of how epithelial and mesenchymal populations can influence cellular growth. Fibroblast growth factors are extracellular signaling proteins that bind appropriate receptors and activate intracellular pathways capable of altering proliferation, migration and survival. In regeneration, members of this broad signaling family participate in interactions at the growing appendage. The precise ligand combinations and cellular sources vary by model and stage. A statement that one FGF is universally sufficient to replace every wound or nerve input would go beyond the evidence.

The AEC is frequently compared with the apical ectodermal ridge, or AER, of the embryonic limb bud. Both are distal epithelial regions associated with mesenchymal outgrowth. The comparison is biologically useful because it suggests that epithelial-mesenchymal interactions employed during development can be redeployed during repair. It is not an identity statement. The embryonic ridge arises in an initial limb field, whereas the regenerative cap develops in response to injury in an already patterned appendage. Their initial tissues, developmental histories and spatial contexts differ.

Consider an experimental situation in which a normal limb wound forms an epithelial covering but the growth-supportive characteristics of the covering are disrupted. If the blastema fails to grow despite survival of underlying tissues and continued nerve input, a reasonable inference is that the epithelial compartment provides necessary support that cannot be replaced merely by nerve presence. If the epithelium is instead removed completely, the result is harder to interpret because physical protection, signaling and possibly injury severity have all changed. The stronger experiment distinguishes the specific function being tested from general tissue damage.

A second experiment might compare wound epithelial cells at early and later stages by measuring a growth-associated gene. If expression increases as the cap forms, the finding is consistent with a role in regeneration, but it does not establish necessity. Selective disruption of the gene, appropriate control tissue and restoration of its function would provide stronger evidence. This example is an application of the distinction between expression and function rather than a claim that a particular unmentioned gene has been tested.

The epithelial covering and the underlying blastema also form a spatial relationship. Cells near the distal tip are exposed to a different combination of epithelial signals, matrix conditions and tissue contacts than cells closer to the stump. As the regenerate grows, those spatial relationships change. The basal region can begin differentiation while a more distal region remains proliferative. The same appendage can therefore contain cells in different developmental states at one time.

The AEC is best understood as one component of a dynamic regenerative niche. A niche is the local cellular and molecular environment that supports particular cell behaviors. It includes signaling molecules, physical contacts, extracellular structures and the neighboring cells that produce them. The specialized epithelium cannot construct a limb alone, and the blastema does not develop independently of its covering. Understanding the next stage requires following the cells recruited from the stump into that niche.

6. Cell sources: recruitment, dedifferentiation and lineage restriction

The cells of a mature limb are specialized for distinct functions. A skeletal muscle fiber contains contractile apparatus; a cartilage cell produces and maintains a specialized matrix; a dermal fibroblast contributes to connective tissue; and a Schwann cell supports peripheral axons. When tissue is removed, these functions cannot all be replaced by a single type of mature cell performing its original activity. New cells must be generated and arranged. The question is where the progenitors come from.

Dedifferentiation is a change from a more specialized state to a less specialized state that can permit new cellular behaviors. It may involve the downregulation of genes associated with mature function, alteration of cell structure, entry into the cell cycle or increased ability to migrate. Dedifferentiation need not erase all previous lineage properties. A cell that becomes less specialized may remain able to form only a particular set of related tissues. This is a key distinction from the much broader potential associated with pluripotent embryonic stem cells.

A second route is the activation of resident progenitors. Many adult tissues contain cells capable of generating replacements under certain conditions. Skeletal muscle satellite cells, for example, are normally associated with muscle fibers and can contribute to muscle repair. If such a cell enters a regenerative growth region and proliferates, it need not have been a fully differentiated contractile fiber beforehand. Its recruitment is not evidence that mature muscle cells reverted to an earlier state.

The two routes can lead to superficially similar cells in a developing blastema. Both a dedifferentiating cell and an activated resident progenitor may lose obvious mature characteristics, express proliferation markers and migrate toward the wound. Under an ordinary microscope they may be difficult to distinguish. Only a method that identifies their history can establish their origin. This is why a lineage study is more informative than a single photograph of the blastema.

Axolotl and newt skeletal muscle illustrate species-specific solutions. In the axolotl, muscle regeneration involves the recruitment of resident muscle progenitors, including satellite cells. In adult newts, mature muscle-derived cells can contribute through dedifferentiation, including the production of proliferative mononuclear progeny from multinucleated muscle structures. The comparison from genetic fate mapping does not imply that all newt tissues dedifferentiate in the same manner or that no resident progenitors exist in newts. It identifies a difference in the contribution of muscle cell populations under the studied conditions.

A mature skeletal muscle fiber has many nuclei within a shared cytoplasm. Its contractile machinery occupies much of the cell. A cell that is to migrate and proliferate independently needs a cellular organization compatible with those behaviors. In the adult newt route, conversion of muscle-derived material into individual progenitor cells requires a substantial change in organization. In the axolotl route, activation and expansion of associated progenitors provides an alternative solution. The different starting conditions explain why simply staining all muscle-derived cells for a proliferation marker cannot distinguish the two mechanisms.

In 2009, Kragl and colleagues provided evidence that axolotl regenerative cells retain restrictions related to their original tissues. The result supports a heterogeneous rather than universally pluripotent blastema. Labeled connective tissue populations can contribute to several related connective tissue derivatives, whereas muscle and Schwann-cell lineages have more restricted contributions. The word restricted must not be taken to mean that a cell can never change its subtype. It indicates that the range of observed descendants is limited relative to the complete collection of regenerated tissues.

The connective tissue populations merit particular attention because they can both contribute material and influence patterning. Fibroblasts are distributed throughout the dermis and internal connective tissue. During regeneration their descendants may participate in rebuilding structural tissue. Positional information associated with such populations provides a potential connection between their prior location and the new pattern. A cell population need not contribute to every tissue in order to influence where other tissues are placed.

By contrast, muscle progenitors can follow a new pattern provided by their environment without necessarily carrying the same kind of position-specific identity as connective tissue. Axons likewise grow into a tissue architecture that has to be organized correctly. This distinction between pattern-forming and pattern-following contributions is a useful model, not a claim that one class of cells is passive or lacks all molecular information. Pattern-following cells still require specific signals and can influence the surrounding tissue.

To test lineage restriction experimentally, a stable label may be introduced specifically into a starting cell population. After regeneration, the investigator identifies the differentiated structures that contain labeled descendants. Label in regenerated cartilage but not muscle would be consistent with a connective tissue-restricted contribution. The absence of label must nevertheless be interpreted cautiously: labeling efficiency, detection sensitivity, survival of the starting cells and completeness of sampling can affect the observed distribution.

The distinction between fate and potency is another important constraint. Fate describes what a cell actually becomes under the circumstances tested. Potency describes the range of outcomes it can produce under suitable conditions. A lineage trace showing that a cell forms cartilage during normal regeneration demonstrates its observed fate. It does not necessarily exhaust every potential outcome under artificial experimental manipulation. Conversely, an unusual outcome in a transplantation experiment does not prove that the same transformation normally occurs during limb regrowth.

It follows that the blastema should be understood as an assemblage of regeneration-competent cells with diverse tissue histories. It is neither a new embryo formed from a fertilized egg nor a homogeneous pool of cells devoid of developmental information. The important question is how this collection of progenitors becomes a growing structure with the capacity to restore the missing limb.

7. Blastema formation and the logic of growth

A blastema becomes visible when cells accumulate beneath the specialized wound epithelium and begin to generate a growing mesenchymal structure. Its early stages are not defined simply by a bump at the stump. The cellular composition, proliferation state and capacity to continue forming a patterned appendage distinguish a regenerative blastema from transient swelling or an ordinary wound-associated cell mass.

The accumulation of cells can arise through more than one process. Local cells may migrate toward the distal region, cells already present may divide, and the rate of cell loss may change. A larger blastema can therefore reflect increased recruitment, faster proliferation, reduced cell death, increased extracellular volume or a combination of these factors. Counting cells within a defined region is more informative about cellular expansion than measuring the diameter of the wound alone.

The early blastema contains cells in relatively immature or altered states. Many cells no longer display the full morphology of their original differentiated tissue. The cellular environment allows them to move, interact and divide. The collection is nevertheless not disorganized in the sense of lacking all regulation. Signals from the stump, the epithelial cap and the local nerve supply contribute to its organization and ability to maintain growth.

As growth proceeds, the blastema becomes elongated and acquires a shape more closely related to a developing appendage. During later stages, recognizable condensations of mesenchyme and differentiating tissue can appear. Condensation refers to the local accumulation and organization of cells that precedes the formation of certain structures, including cartilage. It involves changes in adhesion, matrix interactions and cell behavior rather than simply increased cell number throughout the regenerate.

A growing blastema has to coordinate proliferation with differentiation. A cell engaged in a proliferative progenitor state can generate more descendants; a differentiated cell contributes specialized function and often has reduced proliferative capacity. If every cell differentiated immediately, the population might be exhausted before the necessary structures were produced. If cells continued proliferating without differentiation, an enlarged mass would not restore function. Both processes are necessary and must be deployed in an appropriate spatial and temporal sequence.

The stump-proximal region of the later blastema can enter differentiation while distal cells remain in less differentiated states. A snapshot containing both differentiated and proliferative cells is therefore compatible with a normal regenerative trajectory. To test the direction of the process, a series of specimens collected at comparable stages or longitudinal observations of the same tissue can be more useful than a single time point. A marker of cartilage differentiation and a marker of DNA synthesis answer different questions and can be compared spatially.

DNA synthesis markers are often used to estimate the fraction of cells entering or occupying S phase during a defined labeling interval. Incorporation into DNA indicates that DNA synthesis occurred, but it is not identical to counting completed cytokinesis events. A cell may enter S phase and later arrest. Depending on the system, DNA repair or unusual DNA replication programs can also complicate interpretation. In a normal diploid proliferative population, a pulse-labeling experiment is useful for comparing cell-cycle behavior when the labeling conditions are identical.

A quantitative example illustrates the difference between a proliferation index and population size. If 40 out of 200 sampled mesenchymal nuclei are labeled during a pulse, the observed labeled fraction is 0.20, or twenty percent. If 20 out of 200 are labeled in a second group, the second fraction is ten percent. This difference is compatible with a reduction in cell-cycle activity, but the exact interpretation requires information about S-phase duration, the labeling interval and whether the cells are comparable. A twofold difference in pulse-labeling fraction is not necessarily a twofold difference in net blastema growth.

Cell division, migration and matrix production consume energy and raw materials. A growth-promoting signal cannot sustain cellular expansion without adequate metabolic resources. Vascular supply changes as the regenerate develops, and the physical size of a tissue affects the distance over which substances must travel. Diffusion can support transport across short distances but is constrained by distance. This is one reason why the formation of a fully functional appendage requires integration with blood vessels rather than simply construction of its skeleton.

A normal blastema should ultimately produce a pattern related to the missing territory and should cease behaving as an indefinitely expanding mass. Its transition to tissue organization requires more than growth signals. A well-supported wound environment makes growth possible; interactions among cells with different positional histories make growth productive in the developmental sense. The next sections separate these contributions through classic experiments.

8. Nerve dependence: interpreting classic denervation experiments

Peripheral nerves contain axons extending from neuronal cell bodies as well as associated support cells and connective tissues. In an intact limb they connect target tissues with the nervous system. Following amputation, distal portions of severed axons are lost or degenerate, while surviving axons can grow into the regenerating territory. The nerve supply is therefore a dynamic element of the injury response rather than a fixed cable that merely continues its previous function.

The classical denervation experiment examines whether a limb can regenerate when its normal nerve input is removed. A comparison with an innervated control shows that the presence of adequate nerves is important for blastema formation and growth in salamanders. The cut surface can become covered in the absence of normal nerve supply, yet the deeper regenerative response may arrest. This finding demonstrates that wound closure and regenerative growth have different requirements.

The experimental logic depends on appropriate controls. A manipulation that removes a nerve also causes local injury. A sham-operated control can help distinguish the effect of losing innervation from the effect of additional surgery. Matching the level of amputation, age and general health of the animals reduces alternative explanations. Assessing the integrity of the stump is important because widespread tissue death would prevent regeneration independently of a specific nerve-derived signal.

Denervation can be performed conceptually at different stages, and those stages matter for interpretation. Removal of nerve support before blastema accumulation can prevent a normal growth region from developing. Loss of that support after a blastema has formed can impair further proliferation and outgrowth. A later differentiating regenerate may respond differently to the same change. The conclusion should always be tied to the stage investigated rather than stated as an absolute rule for every tissue and time.

Nerve-derived trophic support refers to signals from nervous tissue that support growth, survival or maintenance of other cells. The term trophic does not necessarily mean nutrient transfer in the ordinary metabolic sense. A ligand released by an axon or associated cell can bind a receptor in the target tissue and change gene expression or cell-cycle activity. Growth factors and related signaling systems can contribute to such interactions, often through cooperation with other tissue compartments.

An important distinction is between a permissive signal and an instructive signal. A permissive signal enables a process that depends on other sources of information; an instructive signal influences the developmental identity or pattern of the responding tissue. Nerve input in salamander limb regeneration is classically interpreted as a major requirement for growth competence. This does not rule out more specific effects of nerve-associated signaling, but the observation that denervation suppresses regrowth is not sufficient to establish that axons specify the identity of every missing digit.

Imagine that a denervated limb forms a small blastema and that a manipulation restores the growth of this population without restoring its spatial pattern. Such an outcome would separate growth rescue from pattern rescue. It would indicate that the supplied input can support cellular expansion but cannot alone reconstruct the anatomical information required. Conversely, if organized regrowth occurs after growth support is restored, that result is compatible with the necessary positional information being available elsewhere in the tissue. This is a hypothetical teaching experiment, not a description of a particular published rescue.

The strength of nerve-associated signals can depend on the amount of functional nerve supply and on the responsiveness of the target cells. A threshold model is often useful for interpreting the general relationship: below an effective level of support, sustained blastema proliferation is insufficient; above it, additional signals may support a more complete response. A threshold is a useful description of an input-output relationship, but it does not imply that every animal has the same numerical requirement or that a single concentration has been measured.

Changes in cell proliferation after denervation must be interpreted alongside changes in tissue composition. A smaller number of proliferating cells might be caused by fewer recruited progenitors, a reduced fraction entering the cell cycle or greater cell loss. A combined analysis of cell number, DNA synthesis, cell death and wound morphology provides a better understanding than any one measurement. The developmental outcome is the integrated consequence of these cellular processes.

Research on the nerve dependence of regeneration also motivates a comparison with the embryonic limb bud. Embryonic limbs initiate development before possessing the mature peripheral nerve arrangement of an adult limb. Regeneration is not therefore a simple replay of the initial developmental sequence. The adult stump uses a wound-associated combination of nerve and epithelial signals to enter a growth-competent state. This dependence is one reason the mechanisms of normal embryonic organogenesis and adult organ restoration must be distinguished.

A nerve can be indispensable without being a sufficient cause of a whole appendage. This conclusion becomes particularly clear in experiments where a new growth region is induced on the side of an existing limb. Such experiments separately examine wound response, nerve support and interactions between cells possessing different positional identities.
