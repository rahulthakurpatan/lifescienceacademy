1. The biological problem: getting a genome across a membrane

An animal virus encounters a cell as a physical particle, not as a freely available genetic message. Its nucleic acid is packaged with viral proteins and, in many families, a membrane. The package protects the genetic material during the journey between cells. Yet the same package becomes an obstacle when the virus reaches a new host cell. Before viral genes can be expressed or copied, the virus must make its information available to the molecular machinery that supports those events. The cell membrane creates a second obstacle: it separates the aqueous contents of the cell from its surroundings. The biological problem is therefore not simply how a virus recognizes a cell, but how a protected genetic package crosses a selective boundary and becomes usable on the other side.

A useful distinction begins with the meaning of attachment. A particle that adheres to an extracellular domain of a membrane receptor has not necessarily crossed the membrane. The receptor may be tightly associated with the particle while the entire viral structure remains outside the cytosol. A signal can travel across the membrane through a conformational change in a receptor, but a signal is not equivalent to the movement of a viral genome. The nucleic acid cannot simply follow the information transmitted by the receptor. A distinct physical process must provide passage across the membrane or make the contents of the particle accessible from an intracellular compartment.

Consider the familiar organization of the animal plasma membrane. Phospholipid molecules contain polar head groups and hydrophobic chains. The chains occupy the interior of the bilayer, while the head groups contact water. This arrangement is maintained by the physical properties of the lipids and is not a rigid wall. Lipids move laterally and the bilayer can bend, form buds and undergo fusion. Nonetheless, the hydrophobic interior is a substantial obstacle to a water-soluble macromolecule bearing many charged groups. Viral nucleic acids contain phosphate groups; they are not molecules that readily dissolve in the hydrocarbon region of a bilayer. A large nucleoprotein complex encounters further steric constraints.

Cells solve ordinary problems of material movement through selective transporters, channels and vesicular trafficking. A conventional ion channel permits a particular class of ions or small solutes to cross the membrane. It does not provide a passage for an entire virion. Vesicular uptake can internalize particles of suitable sizes without exposing them directly to the cytoplasm. Viruses use or redirect some of these host processes, but they must ultimately overcome the membrane that separates the genome from its functional destination. The particle's composition determines which mechanisms are physically available.

The word penetration is used here for the crossing of a limiting cellular membrane by viral genetic material or a genome-containing structure. Internalization denotes movement of material into a cellular compartment, commonly by endocytosis. These two terms are often confused because both can be described casually as entry. If a virus enters a membrane-bounded vesicle, it has moved from the cell exterior to a location inside the geometric outline of the cell. It has not necessarily penetrated the lipid barrier that separates the vesicle lumen from the cytosol. A viral particle can remain trapped in such a compartment and later be degraded.

Uncoating is a third process. The structures that shield the genome must be reorganized, removed or selectively opened sufficiently to permit the next necessary activity. This definition is functional rather than purely visual. The disappearance of every protein associated with the genome is not a general requirement. Some nucleic acids need associated viral enzymes for their first productive reactions; some genomes remain within transcriptionally active core structures. The appropriate state is the one that permits the next biological event, not necessarily a naked strand of nucleic acid.

These distinctions let us partition a complex infection into causal stages. A surface molecule may be needed to hold a particle near the membrane. A second event may make an entry protein competent to interact with the bilayer. Host trafficking may position the particle within a suitable compartment. Penetration then removes the membrane barrier, and additional changes make the viral genome competent for the relevant intracellular function. These events can overlap in time. Their conceptual separation is valuable because evidence for one of them is not automatically evidence for the others.

A cell that supports recognition is sometimes described as susceptible to a virus. In examination contexts the word susceptibility often emphasizes the ability to become infected through the relevant entry pathway, whereas permissiveness emphasizes support for subsequent viral functions. Authors use these terms with some variation. It is safer to identify the event measured than to infer the event from a broad label. A cell may possess a surface receptor yet lack intracellular conditions required after entry. Conversely, a cell may possess machinery that could support viral gene expression but lack the receptor or entry pathway that normally delivers the genome. This explains why receptor detection alone cannot establish the full tissue range of a virus.

The difference between a particle and an infection also matters quantitatively. A count of physical virus particles is a count of structures, whether or not each has a functional genome, intact entry machinery or an appropriate host target. A measure of infectious events is a functional outcome obtained under particular biological conditions. These measurements may differ substantially even when the particles appear similar. A defect limited to the final stage of uncoating can abolish infectivity without preventing particle binding. This chapter therefore follows the fate of the viral package rather than treating all measurements of particle association as interchangeable.

2. Virion architecture and membrane topology

Two broad kinds of virion architecture give rise to different problems of penetration. An enveloped virus has a lipid membrane surrounding an internal assembly of viral proteins and genetic material. The envelope is generally acquired from a cellular membrane during production of progeny virions. Viral proteins are incorporated into this membrane, so the resulting particle is not simply an ordinary piece of host membrane. The viral surface proteins recognize relevant cells and may execute the subsequent fusion reaction. A non-enveloped virus lacks this outer lipid bilayer and presents a protein capsid to the environment. Its entry machinery must interact with the host membrane without merging two pre-existing bilayers.

A capsid is an organized protein shell, built from viral protein subunits. Its repeated interfaces can support a mechanically stable assembly with a limited amount of coding information. The capsid protects the genome and may also carry regions involved in recognition, penetration or later intracellular transport. A nucleocapsid comprises genetic material associated with the relevant structural proteins. The term viral core usually refers to an internal genome-containing assembly, although its precise composition differs between virus families. These definitions prevent a common mistake: loss of a viral lipid envelope does not necessarily mean that the capsid or nucleocapsid has disassembled.

The membrane of an enveloped virus is a useful example of structural adaptation. In a prefusion state, the viral envelope must remain sufficiently intact to protect the contents. Membrane-spanning glycoproteins remain associated with this lipid layer, while exposed domains interact with the environment. Many entry proteins undergo large conformational changes. A hydrophobic region that participates in membrane interaction can be shielded in a resting state, reducing unwanted interactions before the particle encounters an appropriate target. The arrangement reconciles two opposing demands: stability while traveling outside the cell and the ability to rearrange rapidly once a suitable biological condition is encountered.

To understand fusion, imagine two closed compartments, each bounded by a bilayer. Their aqueous interiors are originally separated. Joining the external surfaces of the bilayers does not, on its own, connect the interiors. Only a connection that opens a continuous aqueous route can permit substantial water-soluble cargo to pass. During virus–cell fusion, the viral envelope becomes continuous with a host membrane. The virion's interior becomes connected to the cytosolic side of that membrane. The contents can then reach the cytosol without the genome having to dissolve in the hydrophobic core of intact lipid.

The plasma membrane has an extracellular face and a cytosolic face. During endocytosis, a region of this membrane curves inward and encloses extracellular material. Once the vesicle separates from the cell surface, the former extracellular face lines the lumen of the vesicle. The former cytosolic face remains exposed to the cytoplasm. No reversal of leaflet orientation is required. The lumen is therefore topologically equivalent to the extracellular space, although it is no longer physically continuous with the environment outside the cell. Soluble cargo in the lumen remains separated from the cytosol.

This topology explains why endocytosis is not equivalent to genome delivery. Suppose an enveloped virus is carried into an endosome without altering its own envelope. The viral interior is separated first from the endosomal lumen by the viral membrane and then from the cytosol by the endosomal membrane. Fusion of the two bilayers resolves both separations at the site of fusion. The lumen of the endosome does not become globally continuous with the cytosol. Instead, the virus interior gains an aqueous connection through the locally rearranged membranes. Fusion is thus fundamentally different from rupturing the entire compartment.

For a non-enveloped particle the topological problem is simpler to state but requires another physical solution. There is no viral lipid membrane that can become continuous with a cellular bilayer. Some viruses deliver nucleic acid across a local membrane opening, leaving much of the capsid behind. Others deliver a partially disassembled particle or core across a disrupted limiting membrane. The protein components that participate in these processes often have interactions with lipids that are initially concealed or constrained. Their exposure at the appropriate intracellular stage can turn a protective capsid into a penetration apparatus.

The distinction between envelope and capsid should not be confused with a distinction between RNA and DNA. Enveloped viruses may contain RNA or DNA genomes, and non-enveloped viruses may likewise contain either genome type. The presence of a membrane determines whether ordinary bilayer fusion is physically possible; genome chemistry helps determine what happens after penetration. For example, a negative-sense RNA genome may need an associated RNA-dependent RNA polymerase to make translatable RNA. A DNA virus may require access to the nucleus. These requirements relate to the destination and functional form of the incoming material rather than the mere existence of an envelope.

Genome protection also has a temporal dimension. Exposing a nucleic acid too early may leave it vulnerable to damaging conditions or cause the loss of factors that must travel with it. Keeping a genome enclosed indefinitely can prevent its necessary molecular interactions. Thus a protective structure that is beneficial at one stage can be inhibitory at another. A useful entry model must account for both the stability of the original particle and the controlled change that permits intracellular function. It cannot explain entry merely by saying that the capsid breaks.

Animal cells use membrane remodeling extensively in normal physiology. Secretory vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane, and endocytic vesicles deliver cargo to compartments that exchange material through regulated fusion events. Viral entry makes use of the same broad physical principles of membrane deformation and continuity. The molecules that drive virus–cell fusion, however, need not be the host fusion proteins used in ordinary vesicular transport. The virus brings specialized components that connect its recognition and triggering system to the movement of its own genome. Distinguishing physical similarity from molecular identity is important when transferring knowledge from cell biology to virology.

3. From observations of entry to mechanistic explanations

The experimental study of viral entry required investigators to distinguish events that occur very close together in time and space. A loss of infectious outcome could reflect failure to attach, failure to cross a membrane, damage to the genome or a defect in later gene expression. Studies that measured only the production of progeny therefore gave limited information about the earliest event that failed. The development of biochemical measurements and imaging made it possible to compare physical changes of particles and membranes with the biological outcome of infection.

Influenza hemagglutinin provides an especially instructive historical example. Hemagglutination, the aggregation of red blood cells by particles capable of interacting with their surfaces, reveals a viral binding activity. Such an observation does not show that the cells support the complete viral life cycle. It gives evidence that viral surface components interact with suitable cellular molecules. The separate question of how a bound influenza particle gains access to the cytoplasm required investigation of a different process.

In 1982, White, Kartenbeck and Helenius and other investigators studying membrane fusion helped establish the importance of low pH in influenza entry; the 1982 report on influenza fusion with lipid vesicles documented the ability of the viral particle to merge with suitable target membranes under acidic conditions. In a separate 1982 biochemical study, Skehel and colleagues observed a change in hemagglutinin conformation in the pH region associated with its membrane fusion activity. These are historically documented observations, not numerical data invented for examination exercises. Taken together, such lines of work helped connect an environmental condition encountered during intracellular trafficking with a physical change in a viral surface protein. See the linked historical sources in the references.

The conceptual significance of an artificial lipid vesicle is that it isolates part of the problem. A vesicle does not reproduce all activities of a living cell. It has a membrane and an aqueous interior but lacks the complete cell's gene expression system and ordinary intracellular transport. Observing an appropriate membrane interaction in such a simplified system provides evidence that the physical event can be separated from later viral biosynthesis. Conversely, an artificial vesicle cannot establish that the same event necessarily occurs in every relevant cell type. The inference should remain proportionate to what the model contains and what the measurement detects.

Structural evidence added a further dimension. A protein consists of a chain whose amino acid sequence constrains the possible three-dimensional conformations. Comparing structural states of a viral fusion protein helps identify where a membrane-interacting segment is located before and after activation. If it is buried in one state and positioned to contact another membrane in a different state, a plausible mechanistic connection emerges between protein rearrangement and membrane interaction. Structural snapshots, however, do not alone provide a movie of the entire process. Proposed intermediate states must be supported by additional evidence and should not be treated as directly observed solely because two endpoint structures are known.

HIV entry illustrates the value of revisiting established interpretations. Classical textbook accounts place receptor-triggered fusion at the plasma membrane. Studies of membrane and internal-content mixing showed that some particle–cell combinations can involve uptake and completion of fusion in internal compartments. Miyauchi and colleagues reported endocytosis-dependent entry in their studied models in 2009. This did not make the distinction between receptor and acid triggers meaningless. It demonstrated that the cellular location of fusion and the molecular condition that activates the fusion protein are separate questions. A student should retain the useful textbook contrast while recognizing its experimental boundaries.

The history of uncoating shows a similar progression from broad descriptions to spatially resolved mechanisms. It was convenient to describe capsid removal as something that happens promptly after penetration. Yet an incoming viral core can continue to serve functions after it reaches the cytosol. Evidence from nuclear transport studies of HIV has challenged a universal model of complete cytoplasmic capsid disassembly. The relevant lesson for an examination is not the specialized details of current research, but the fact that membrane penetration and final genome exposure are separable and may occur in different compartments.

Several kinds of evidence can be combined to test an entry model. A measure of surface association establishes an early event. Localization relative to known cellular compartments helps establish where the particle travels. A measure of access to cytosolic contents helps identify membrane crossing. A structural or biochemical measure may reveal changes in capsid association. A later expression readout indicates that some incoming material supported a productive downstream event. Each readout answers a different question. Their relationship is a powerful basis for interpreting the evidence presented in CSIR-NET Part C problems.

4. How a viral fusion protein changes membrane connectivity

Attachment places the viral particle near a possible target, but two membranes that touch do not automatically merge. This is an important physical constraint because animal cells contain many membranes in close proximity. If proximity alone caused fusion, compartmental organization could not be maintained. At molecular scale the exposed head groups of lipid molecules interact with water and neighboring head groups. Bringing the two bilayers into the close apposition needed for fusion involves reorganizing water and deforming lipids away from their usual packing. These rearrangements require overcoming an energy barrier even if the final state is energetically favorable.

The viral fusion protein supplies a means to couple molecular rearrangement to deformation of the two membranes. Its starting structure is often metastable: it can persist in a particular conformation even though another conformation is favored once a constraint is released. Metastability is not the same as chemical instability in the ordinary sense. A metastable protein can remain folded for a considerable time because a barrier prevents access to another state. Activation changes the accessible pathway between states. The movement toward a more stable conformation can provide mechanical work on the membranes with which the protein interacts.

A simple reaction coordinate diagram helps interpret the term activation barrier. The horizontal axis represents the progress of a complex structural rearrangement, not necessarily time or a single physical distance. The vertical axis represents free energy. A prefusion state can occupy a local minimum separated by a higher-energy region from a lower-energy postfusion state. The energy difference between minima indicates the thermodynamic preference under the specified conditions. The barrier between them influences the rate at which the change can occur. A suitable trigger can alter the shape of this energy landscape. It does not follow that the incoming virion must hydrolyze ATP through an intrinsic motor for every membrane fusion event.

A typical viral fusion protein is anchored in the envelope through a membrane-spanning region. Once activated, a membrane-interacting segment becomes available to engage the target membrane. This segment is commonly described as a fusion peptide or fusion loop, depending on its position and structure in the protein. While the viral membrane anchor remains in one bilayer and the newly exposed segment engages the other, the protein spans the gap between them. Subsequent changes in the arrangement of its domains can shorten the effective separation between the two points of attachment. The protein therefore links its own conformational change to movement of membranes.

For many fusion proteins the final rearrangement has a folded-back configuration. Regions associated with the viral and target membranes are brought toward the same end of the rearranged structure. This geometry explains why a structural transition can exert force on two initially separate membranes. The details vary among fusion protein families, and a simple hairpin drawing should not be interpreted as showing identical molecular architecture for all viruses. The shared principle is that a regulated membrane-interacting structure becomes able to bring bilayers together and facilitate their local rearrangement.

It is helpful to distinguish several lipid-level stages. Initially, the two outer or apposed leaflets are separate and their constituent lipids are largely confined to their original membranes. A highly curved local connection may form between the apposed leaflets. This intermediate is often called a stalk. Rearrangement of lipids around such a connection can produce hemifusion, in which the contacting leaflets become continuous but the aqueous interiors are not yet joined. A model of hemifusion helps explain why lipid movement between two membranes can be detected before substantial aqueous cargo has crossed.

A hemifusion diaphragm can be conceptualized as a lipid barrier separating the original aqueous compartments after the apposed leaflets have joined. The distal leaflets must also be rearranged for an aqueous fusion pore to form. The opening of such a pore changes membrane topology: material can now pass from the viral interior to the cytosolic side. The pore may initially be small, and its subsequent enlargement may be necessary for the passage of a larger nucleoprotein assembly. Some intermediate states can reverse or fail to progress. The observation of membrane lipid mixing is therefore not sufficient proof that a functionally adequate fusion pore has opened.

In a familiar vesicular fusion event, aqueous solutes can mix through a fusion pore while the combined membrane remains a barrier between the mixed contents and the environment outside. The same principle applies to viral fusion. The objective is not the indiscriminate destruction of the host membrane. An enveloped particle generally gains access through localized reorganization that makes its interior continuous with the target cytosolic compartment. Membrane disruption used by certain non-enveloped viruses is a different solution and should not be confused with the normal lipid connectivity of a fusion pore.

The physical constraints can be understood by considering surface geometry. Lipid molecules differ in the relative cross-sectional areas of their head groups and hydrophobic tails. A flat bilayer and a curved membrane region place different demands on the packing of these molecules. During a fusion intermediate some lipids must accommodate substantial curvature. The tendency of a membrane to bend, its lateral organization and interactions with proteins can influence the energetics of these configurations. This helps explain why an appropriate receptor and an active viral fusion protein may not fully account for fusion behavior in every membrane context. The lipid environment is a physical participant in the event.

It is useful to distinguish a molecular trigger from the machinery that performs fusion. Binding of a receptor can be the condition that initiates a conformational change. Acidification can provide an alternative trigger by changing protonation states within a viral protein. A preparatory cleavage may make a protein competent to respond to either trigger. These processes are not interchangeable. A protease can generate a functional cleavage product without directly providing an aqueous route between the compartments. A receptor can promote activation without being the molecule that deforms the bilayers. The fusion protein connects these events to the physical rearrangement.

Regulation is particularly important when conformational changes are effectively irreversible on the timescale of entry. If a virion undergoes a transition in the absence of a suitable target membrane, the membrane-interacting region may not engage the membrane it needs to reach. The particle can lose the ability to complete a productive fusion event even though its genome remains physically present. This is a general illustration of why timing matters in molecular mechanisms: a required event can become a cause of failure when it occurs in the wrong context.

The model should not imply that all viral proteins undergo identical transitions or that all fusion intermediates are observed directly in living cells. Structural classes of viral fusion proteins provide examples of distinct protein architectures solving a shared physical problem. Class I includes the well-known influenza hemagglutinin and retroviral envelope fusion subunits. Class II includes proteins such as the flavivirus E protein; these use fusion loops in a largely beta-sheet-rich architecture. Class III includes the vesicular stomatitis virus G protein, which combines structural features of the other broad classes. The classification refers to structural and mechanistic characteristics rather than the type of genome or the compartment in which fusion occurs. The major examination objective is to recognize the common membrane topology and regulated exposure of membrane-interacting regions, without assuming an identical fold for every virus.

5. Receptor recognition, proteolytic priming and environmental triggers

The term trigger is best understood as a condition that permits a previously restrained molecular transition to proceed. A viral particle may experience many changes in its environment as it moves from one cell to another, but a successful entry pathway uses the relevant changes to coordinate activity with location. Receptor contact, exposure to an altered chemical environment, and proteolytic maturation are three major categories of events that can be distinguished. In many cases more than one event is necessary. For example, a fusion protein may first need to be processed into a competent form and later respond to its activating stimulus.

The distinction between receptor binding and receptor-mediated activation can be illustrated by comparing two conceptual viral surface proteins. One may attach to a cellular glycan and allow the whole particle to remain at the membrane long enough for uptake. Its activation for fusion can occur much later in an endosome. Another protein may change shape in response to receptor binding itself, revealing a new interaction site or enabling its partner fusion subunit to rearrange. Both have receptor binding as an early stage, but the role of the receptor differs. It would be incorrect to infer from attachment alone that every viral fusion protein has been triggered.

For a receptor-dependent pathway, the recognition surface of a viral protein interacts with a compatible cellular molecule. This interaction can stabilize one conformation relative to another. A protein is not a rigid object: different regions can shift with respect to each other, and the binding of a ligand in one region may affect accessibility or stability of another. This is an example of allosteric coupling, a principle encountered in enzyme regulation and signaling. In viral entry the output of such coupling can be a change in the protein's readiness to engage a membrane, rather than a conventional intracellular second messenger response.

The host receptor need not be a molecule that evolved for interaction with a virus. Its ordinary physiological role may be cell adhesion, immune recognition, transport, or communication. The pathogen uses properties of the receptor that are already present in the cell. This relationship helps explain why receptor loss can have cellular consequences independent of viral entry. In an experiment comparing receptor-positive and receptor-negative cells, the investigator must consider both altered viral attachment and any accompanying changes in the physiology of the host cell. The outcome of a downstream assay alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

A coreceptor is a second cellular molecule required in addition to a primary receptor in a particular entry mechanism. The first interaction may make a later interaction possible. The second interaction can then contribute to activation of the fusion machinery. However, not all viral pathways have a distinct coreceptor, and a cellular attachment factor is not automatically a coreceptor. The labels should follow functional evidence. If an early interaction concentrates particles without being indispensable for the molecular transition, its role differs from that of a necessary activating partner. Distinguishing the two is especially helpful when different experiments measure binding and entry separately.

Proteolytic priming illustrates a different type of regulation. A protease hydrolyzes particular peptide bonds in a protein precursor. The resulting protein fragments may remain associated through noncovalent interactions or covalent links. Cleavage does not necessarily destroy the original protein's overall organization. Instead, it can create a new chain end that participates in a later event while remaining inaccessible until the protein is activated. The structural consequence of a specific cleavage is therefore different from degradation of the protein into fragments that can no longer perform its biological function.

Influenza hemagglutinin is a classical example in which proteolytic maturation of its precursor is required for fusion competence. The receptor-binding region and the region that participates in membrane fusion are structurally associated in the mature protein. Processing and acid activation are distinct requirements. An attachment assay may indicate that a precursor-containing particle interacts with a cell, yet a productive fusion event may fail if the protein has not undergone the necessary maturation. The result illustrates why a biochemical measure of binding cannot determine whether a particle possesses a functional fusion apparatus.

Protonation-dependent changes illustrate how a chemical condition can act as a switch. The concentration of hydrogen ions affects the balance between protonated and deprotonated forms of ionizable groups. Within a folded protein, the local environment influences the tendency of a group to gain or lose a proton. Changes in protonation can weaken some electrostatic attractions, change repulsions and alter hydrogen-bond networks. The collective effect may change the relative stability of protein conformations. No single universal amino acid is responsible for every pH-dependent viral transition.

In an endosome the external surface of an internalized virion is exposed to the luminal solution. If the endosome becomes more acidic, protonation changes can occur in portions of viral proteins exposed to that environment. The interior of the virion may not have the same pH as the lumen immediately, because the viral envelope is itself a selective membrane. Processes that alter the internal environment must therefore be distinguished from processes that act on a surface protein. Influenza provides a useful case where the cell-derived acidic environment has both an effect on the outside of the virion and, through a viral ion channel, an effect on its interior.

It is tempting to assign a single pH value as the activation threshold for all enveloped viruses. Such a number would be misleading. Some entry pathways depend on acidification, some depend on receptor interaction at approximately neutral pH, and the relevant pH responses of different acid-dependent proteins vary. Even for a given viral system, an observed transition can depend on the assay and membrane context. For general reasoning, a low-pH-dependent event should be defined by an experimentally demonstrated dependence on an acidic environment, not by the memorization of a universal numerical threshold.

The sequence of triggering events also imposes a direction on the pathway. A receptor interaction outside the cell can bring a particle into an endosome, where the chemical conditions differ from those at the surface. If the protein responds to those conditions only after it is appropriately primed, the pathway contains two checkpoints. In contrast, a receptor-triggered fusion protein can receive its activating information at or near the cell surface, although completion of fusion may take place elsewhere. The location of the stimulus and the location of the completed event do not have to be identical.

A careful examination answer should specify the viral example and the step affected by a perturbation. A perturbation that changes endosomal pH has an obvious connection with acid-dependent mechanisms, but its actual effect can also include disruption of host trafficking. A perturbation that alters receptor binding may reduce delivery to endosomes, even if the fusion trigger itself is acidity rather than receptor contact. A defect in proteolytic maturation can prevent fusion even when receptor recognition and the correct chemical environment are intact. These causal distinctions provide more information than labeling all three outcomes as a failure of entry.

6. Endocytosis, intracellular trafficking and the fate of incoming particles

Endocytosis is a cellular process by which material from the cell surface becomes enclosed within a membrane-bounded compartment. It occurs as part of normal nutrient acquisition, receptor regulation and membrane turnover. An incoming virus can become associated with this machinery because its binding partner is a component of the cell surface, because the interaction influences cellular uptake, or because the particle is captured by a suitable pathway. Entry through endocytosis therefore depends on a sequence of interactions involving both viral structures and host processes.

In clathrin-associated endocytosis, proteins on the cytosolic side of the plasma membrane help organize a region into a curved coated pit. Cargo is associated with the membrane on the opposite side, either directly through membrane components or indirectly through receptor interactions. A virion captured at the cell surface does not bind clathrin in the same way it binds an exposed extracellular receptor: these components occupy different membrane faces. Curvature and scission eventually produce a vesicle that carries extracellular cargo into the cell. Removal and reorganization of coat components allow further trafficking.

Not all viruses use clathrin-coated uptake. The cell possesses several routes of internalization, including pathways that do not depend on the conventional clathrin coat. Larger areas of the plasma membrane can also form ruffles that enclose extracellular fluid in macropinocytosis. A single virus species can use different uptake pathways under different cellular conditions. For this chapter the important principle is the conservation of membrane topology: the particle is initially enclosed within a lumen regardless of which membrane-bending and scission machinery produced the compartment.

A newly formed vesicle may deliver its contents to an early endosomal system, where cargo is sorted. Some components return to the cell surface; some progress to compartments with different membrane identities, chemical environments and destinations. The early and late endosomes are not identical bags of solution located at different distances from the plasma membrane. They differ in composition and interactions with other parts of the trafficking network. The sorting and maturation of these compartments provide temporal and spatial organization to normal cellular processes. A virus that relies on a particular environment must reach it before being diverted to an unproductive fate.

Acidification is a normal feature of many endocytic compartments. The vacuolar-type ATPase uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to move protons in a direction that contributes to acidification of the lumen. The actual luminal pH also depends on buffering, ion movement and other processes that influence the electrochemical conditions of the compartment. The pump is a host protein and is not part of the viral genome delivery machinery. Viruses that respond to the acidic lumen exploit a condition already established by the host.

The difference between a proton pump and a proton channel is central to influenza entry reasoning. A pump couples transport to an energy source, whereas a channel provides a selective path for ions to move according to their electrochemical driving force. The host vacuolar ATPase builds and maintains a proton gradient. A viral proton channel can allow protons to move into the virion when the channel is active and conditions favor that movement. The two proteins act at different membranes and perform different physical functions. A drawing of an endosome containing a virus should place the cellular pump in the endosomal membrane and the viral channel in the viral membrane.

Trafficking can produce an unproductive outcome even when internalization succeeds. For example, if a particle remains within a compartment whose conditions never support its required structural transition, it can remain membrane-separated from its intracellular target. If it is delivered to a degradative compartment, the original virion may cease to be functional before its genome becomes available. Recycling of the relevant receptor can also change the movement of receptor-associated material. A successful entry pathway requires more than movement into the cell: it requires delivery of the cargo to an appropriate place in an appropriate state.

The distinction between endosomal residence and escape can be tested conceptually by comparing a label attached to a viral structural component with a marker of endosomal membranes. Overlap in their optical positions is consistent with an association at the resolution of the imaging technique, but it does not by itself identify which side of the membrane the viral component occupies. Even a punctate signal apparently inside the cell may correspond to a membrane-enclosed object. Conversely, diffuse signal might represent release, degradation of labeled material or altered distribution of the label rather than an intact genome-containing assembly. Independent evidence of cytosolic access strengthens the interpretation.

The cytoskeleton is another part of the host trafficking system. Actin participates in membrane movement and remodeling, and microtubules support directed movement of many vesicles and other cargoes. An incoming virus that requires transport toward particular intracellular destinations can consequently depend on a normal cellular organization. However, a change in viral localization after disruption of a cytoskeletal component does not automatically prove a direct interaction between the viral particle and that component. Changes in host compartment distribution can produce indirect effects. A scientifically careful model distinguishes a demonstrated physical association from dependence on the broader transport system.

Endocytosis has different consequences for enveloped and non-enveloped viruses. For the former, the virion carries a bilayer that can merge with the surrounding endosomal membrane when the appropriate fusion machinery is activated. For the latter, membrane traversal requires a different kind of interaction. The common dependence on trafficking does not imply that the particles cross the final membrane in the same way. This distinction will become particularly important when the same experimental manipulation affects viruses with different structural architectures.

A general principle emerges from these observations. The location of a particle, the state of its structural proteins, the availability of its genome and the eventual production of viral gene products are related but distinct variables. Entry is a sequence of changes in those variables, constrained by the topology and physiology of the host cell. The explanatory task is to identify the transition being observed rather than to equate any internal signal with successful infection.

7. Influenza A: two membranes, two acid responses and coordinated genome delivery

Influenza A is an instructive model because its early infection events involve several separable functions organized within the same particle. The virus has an envelope containing viral membrane proteins. Hemagglutinin, abbreviated HA, contributes both to recognition and to fusion. Neuraminidase is another surface protein, but its major classical role concerns cleavage of sialic acid from glycoconjugates, especially in the context of movement through sialylated environments and release of progeny. M2 is a viral membrane protein that forms a proton channel. Under the envelope lies a matrix containing M1, associated with the internal organization of the virion. The genome comprises eight RNA segments in the form of viral ribonucleoprotein assemblies, abbreviated vRNPs.

Each vRNP includes viral RNA associated with nucleoprotein and a viral polymerase complex. This association is not merely a protective shell to be discarded at the first opportunity. Influenza A has negative-sense RNA, meaning that the genomic RNA is complementary in orientation to the corresponding translatable message. A host ribosome cannot simply treat that RNA as a normal messenger RNA to produce viral proteins. The packaged polymerase is required to initiate the appropriate RNA synthesis after delivery. The vRNP is consequently a functional unit that must reach an intracellular location in an appropriate state.

The receptor-binding activity of HA recognizes sialic acid-containing structures on the host surface. These structures occur on glycoconjugates rather than representing one universal protein receptor. The presentation of glycans and the compatibility of the viral binding surface are relevant to attachment. The previous node treated the relationship between recognition and tropism. Here the question is what follows attachment. Binding of HA to a suitable host surface establishes proximity and supports uptake, but it does not itself normally provide the acid trigger for the later fusion rearrangement.

The HA molecule is synthesized initially as a precursor often called HA0. Functional maturation produces the associated HA1 and HA2 portions. HA1 contributes the receptor-binding region; HA2 contains the membrane fusion machinery, including the fusion peptide near its newly generated amino terminus. The parts remain associated in the mature trimeric assembly. In the prefusion structure the membrane-interacting segment is shielded from unrestricted contact with lipid membranes. Thus proteolysis establishes structural readiness but does not mean that the particle will fuse immediately after cleavage.

The distinction between HA cleavage and acid-triggered HA rearrangement is essential. The cleavage event and the conformational switch serve different functions and need not occur in the same compartment. Cleavage generates a competent fusion apparatus; the subsequent acidic environment favors the major rearrangements that execute fusion. An uncleaved precursor can retain receptor-binding properties but fail to complete productive fusion. A mature HA protein can remain in its prefusion state until it encounters the activating condition. Therefore the terms maturation, attachment and activation should not be used as synonyms.

Once internalized, the influenza particle is situated in the lumen of an endocytic compartment. As the compartment matures, the acidic luminal environment acts on the exposed surfaces of HA. This is possible without protons first passing through the viral membrane, because the relevant protein regions are accessible from outside the particle. The structural changes of the activated HA assembly expose the fusion peptide and permit interaction with the endosomal membrane. The viral membrane anchor remains associated with the envelope. Subsequent rearrangement couples the protein's changing geometry to movement of the viral and host membranes.

The fusion process establishes a localized connection between the two lipid bilayers. In an intermediate state the membranes may exchange lipids without opening a route suitable for a large internal assembly. The formation and enlargement of an aqueous pore then provide a route toward the cytosol. This is fusion of the viral envelope with the host endosomal membrane, not fusion between two endosomes, nor a requirement for complete destruction of the endosomal boundary. The key change is the connection of the viral interior with the cytosolic compartment.

A second acid-dependent event takes place in the virion itself. M2 permits proton movement into the viral particle when activated under suitable conditions. The acidification of the viral interior alters interactions associated with the matrix and vRNP organization, supporting their separation during uncoating. The process illustrates why penetration and disassembly must be coordinated. HA changes the connectivity of the two membranes, whereas M2 contributes to a change in the physical state of the material that must pass through the connection. Neither function should be substituted for the other in an examination answer.

Consider what the different membrane proteins do in a diagram. The host proton pump is located in the endosomal boundary. It contributes to the concentration difference between cytosol and lumen. HA is anchored in the virion envelope, presenting its external domains toward the lumen before fusion. M2 is embedded in the virion envelope and provides a path between the lumen and the particle interior. A schematic that places all three proteins in the same bilayer obscures the physical organization that makes these events possible. The same acidification phenomenon influences two different viral targets because the virus is enclosed within an acidic compartment.

The release of vRNPs is not equivalent to release of RNA without its binding proteins. Once the assemblies are accessible to the cytosol, their nuclear localization information can be recognized by host nuclear import machinery. Importins mediate transport across the nuclear pore complex. The nuclear pore is a regulated channel through the nuclear envelope, and the transport of appropriate macromolecular assemblies through it is conceptually different from viral fusion with a lipid bilayer. Nuclear delivery therefore involves another transition after endosomal escape, one that uses a normal pathway for macromolecular transport.

This final intracellular destination is a notable feature of influenza biology. Students often learn that many RNA viruses carry out their major RNA synthesis activities in the cytoplasm. That is a useful broad pattern but not a universal rule. Influenza is a classic counterexample in which the relevant incoming RNA–protein assemblies must reach the nucleus. The presence of an RNA genome alone is insufficient to predict where its early productive events occur. Conversely, a nuclear destination does not imply that the incoming virus must directly fuse with the nuclear envelope.

Historical biochemical and structural studies of HA support this mechanistic interpretation. The work of Wilson, Skehel and Wiley in 1981 provided a structural basis for understanding the organization of HA. The 1982 conformational studies by Skehel and colleagues connected protein rearrangement to acidic conditions. Such observations are mutually informative: structure explains the spatial constraints on activation, while biochemical observations relate the change of state to the environment encountered by the incoming particle. The mechanistic conclusion is strongest when supported by more than one kind of evidence.

The model predicts that an intervention affecting the acidification-dependent stage may allow surface association and uptake while preventing a later step. The precise affected step cannot be inferred from the loss of viral expression alone because acidification contributes both to fusion activation and to virion interior changes. If a measurement detects lipid mixing but shows a failure of delivery of the functional internal assemblies, the result indicates that at least some membrane rearrangement occurred. Additional evidence would be needed to distinguish failure of pore enlargement from insufficient disassembly or another problem affecting cargo release.

Similarly, normal appearance of HA on a particle is not proof that HA is functional. A surface protein can be present in an unsuitable conformational or maturation state. Measurements of recognition, membrane mixing and content delivery report different properties of a particle. If a study finds normal attachment with impaired fusion, the result is consistent with a defect after recognition, but does not alone identify the structural cause. A strong interpretation includes a direct readout of the affected transition, rather than assigning a particular molecular defect solely from an endpoint.

Influenza therefore offers a connected example of biological organization. Recognition, regulated protein maturation, endocytic transport, host-dependent acidification, viral membrane fusion, ion movement into the virion, rearrangement of internal structures and nuclear import together account for the early route of the incoming genome. The central lesson is the partition of these events into mechanistically distinct stages. Later synthesis and assembly are different subjects; our concern ends at the point where the incoming material has reached a location and functional state compatible with the next stage.

8. HIV: receptor-triggered fusion and the distinction between fusion and capsid uncoating

HIV offers a contrast to influenza because the classical trigger for its membrane fusion machinery is interaction with cellular receptors rather than a requirement for acid activation. The viral envelope carries an organized glycoprotein complex commonly termed Env. Its surface component, gp120, and transmembrane component, gp41, have different but coupled functions. The complex is produced from a precursor, and appropriate processing contributes to formation of a functional entry protein. As with influenza, the arrangement of receptor-binding and membrane-interacting regions allows recognition to be coupled to physical membrane rearrangement.

A suitable cellular interaction involves CD4 and an appropriate chemokine coreceptor, commonly CCR5 or CXCR4 in the standard textbook account of HIV-1. These cellular proteins have normal physiological functions in immune cells. Their interaction with Env should not be understood as an ordinary physiological signal simply commanding the cell to take up a foreign genome. Rather, their binding to the viral complex changes the molecular interactions and accessibility of regions within Env, allowing the entry protein to proceed toward its fusogenic state.

Binding to CD4 changes the presentation of gp120 regions involved in coreceptor recognition. Association with a compatible coreceptor helps permit subsequent rearrangements of gp41. The fusion-active part of gp41 can interact with the host bilayer, while another region remains associated with the viral envelope. The protein rearrangement then draws membranes together. The final folded-back organization is a class I fusion protein example. The fundamental physical problem is the same as for influenza: two lipid bilayers must locally rearrange to establish an aqueous connection between the virion's interior and the cytosol.

CD4 and the coreceptor are not components of a membrane channel through which the viral core is transported. Nor does their ordinary activity as cell signaling proteins explain the entire membrane fusion reaction. The viral fusion machinery performs the structural work at the membrane interface. This is why an entry-blocking intervention can interfere with a specific recognition or conformational stage without requiring generalized destruction of the cellular membrane. The conceptual difference between an attachment receptor and a fusion protein should remain clear even when both proteins participate in the same molecular event.

The traditional comparison between HIV and influenza is informative if stated carefully. Influenza is a classical example of endocytic uptake followed by fusion promoted by acidic conditions. HIV is classically illustrated by fusion at the plasma membrane after receptor and coreceptor engagement at approximately neutral pH. These illustrations show different ways of coupling recognition to fusion. They should not be treated as mutually exclusive categories that classify every entry event according to whether the host cell has used endocytosis.

Studies have demonstrated that HIV can complete fusion after endocytic uptake in some virus–cell systems. A receptor-triggered entry mechanism need not lose its defining molecular characteristics just because the particle moves into an intracellular compartment before the fusion pore completes. The relevant questions are what condition activates the viral entry machinery and where the viral contents actually cross a host membrane. Evidence for one does not by itself establish the other. Consequently, a pH-independent fusion reaction is not a synonym for fusion exclusively at the cell surface.

The location issue also helps explain how different assays may yield apparently different conclusions. An assay of lipid mixing can show that a viral membrane has interacted with a membrane at the surface. Yet an aqueous connection sufficient for content passage may not have formed there. If subsequent imaging or a content-transfer readout identifies passage from an internal compartment, the observations need not contradict the earlier detection of lipid mixing. They can be reporting different stages of a multistep mechanism. Conclusions should specify which stage was measured and in which cellular context it occurred.

Following productive membrane fusion, the incoming HIV material includes a capsid-containing core. The capsid is a protein assembly distinct from the viral envelope. Loss of the outer lipid boundary is thus not synonymous with disappearance of the capsid. The core contains the genetic material and viral factors involved in the earliest stages of the subsequent intracellular program. The genome's functional destination differs from influenza's, even though both viruses have RNA genomes and utilize envelope fusion.

In a retrovirus the incoming genetic information is ultimately represented in DNA through reverse transcription. Full mechanistic treatment of reverse transcription belongs to another part of the curriculum. For the present topic, it is sufficient to recognize that genome exposure and capsid rearrangement must be compatible with this process and with transport toward the nuclear compartment. The capsid may support functions beyond initial physical protection. Premature or inappropriate loss of its organization can therefore interfere with successful completion of downstream events.

Modern evidence supports the presence of substantial HIV capsid assemblies in or near the nucleus, with completion of uncoating in the nuclear compartment in studied systems. The details of the spatial and temporal relationship among nuclear entry, reverse transcription and final disassembly have been refined over time. The examination-level distinction is that uncoating is not universally an instantaneous event that follows membrane fusion at the cell surface. Evidence from HIV challenges that simplified picture, without requiring students to memorize specialized contemporary models of capsid–nuclear pore interactions.

Comparing HIV and influenza also clarifies what is meant by an entry-specific measurement. For HIV, detecting core delivery to the cytosol provides evidence of penetration even if full capsid disassembly is incomplete. For influenza, detecting released vRNP assemblies may establish delivery without demonstrating their arrival in the nucleus. In neither case does detecting an early intracellular structure establish that all later steps are successful. If a question describes reduced final gene expression but unchanged early delivery, it is reasonable to investigate a step beyond penetration rather than automatically attributing the phenotype to a failure of envelope fusion.

A further distinction concerns virus–cell fusion and cell–cell fusion. In the first process, the envelope of an individual virion merges with the membrane of a target cell, giving the viral interior access to the cell. In the second process, membranes of two cells merge so that their cytoplasmic contents become connected. If viral fusion proteins are expressed at the surface of an infected cell, they can in some systems promote fusion with another cell possessing compatible recognition molecules. Such events can produce multinucleated structures called syncytia. The cells themselves, rather than only a small virus particle and one cell, provide the two membrane-bound compartments.

Syncytium formation can be an important biological consequence of expression of viral fusion machinery, but it is not a mandatory consequence of successful virion entry. A cell may support entry without forming large fused cell structures. A cell–cell fusion assay may reveal the ability of a particular protein system to merge membranes without reproducing the geometry, matrix organization or internal cargo release of an intact virion. The study of physiological and pathological cell–cell fusion is developed separately later in this subunit. Here the contrast prevents a change in cell morphology from being mistaken for direct proof of viral genome penetration.

The comparison between influenza and HIV should therefore be built around a matrix of distinct properties. Both are enveloped viruses whose surface proteins can mediate fusion. The triggering logic differs, as does the fate of the released genome-containing structure. The textbook locations of fusion differ, with experimentally demonstrated context dependence. The two pathways illustrate the general principle that the physical mechanism of membrane crossing and the biochemical mechanism of making the genetic material functional are linked but not identical.

9. Non-enveloped viruses: membrane penetration without envelope fusion

A non-enveloped virion has no external lipid bilayer to merge with the host plasma membrane or an endosomal membrane. Nevertheless, it faces the same requirement of delivering genetic information to an intracellular location. Its capsid may recognize the cell, and the particle may be internalized, but ordinary virus-envelope fusion cannot occur because one of the required lipid bilayers is absent. The penetration mechanism must therefore involve a controlled change in a host membrane mediated by protein components of the particle.

One representative solution is the creation of a local opening through which nucleic acid can cross. A capsid protein can participate in membrane interaction after an appropriate change of state. The opening need not be large enough to accommodate the entire intact particle. A genome may be transferred while much of the original protein shell remains on the opposite side of the membrane. This provides an important distinction from the picture of an intact virion passing through a hole of the same diameter as the whole particle. The cargo of interest is often smaller or more flexible than the original virion structure.

Picornaviruses provide classical examples of small non-enveloped RNA viruses whose capsid rearrangements are associated with membrane penetration. Poliovirus is widely used in textbooks to illustrate receptor-associated capsid changes and delivery of the RNA genome. The important point for this curriculum is that the membrane-associated intermediates facilitate genome transfer without requiring a viral lipid envelope. A pore-mediated model is supported by evidence from this family, but the precise structural details of the passage cannot be assumed identical for all non-enveloped particles or every experimental system.

A second representative solution is disruption of the limiting membrane sufficiently to permit a genome-containing particle or subviral structure to leave a compartment. Adenovirus provides a useful contrast with the genome-transfer model. Its protein shell encloses a DNA genome. Following uptake, structural changes of the particle expose internal components involved in interactions with the membrane. Work identifying adenovirus protein VI as a mediator of membrane disruption helped explain how an internalized non-enveloped particle can escape a membrane-bounded compartment. After escape, further trafficking and genome release are still required.

The distinction between a fusion protein and a membrane-disrupting capsid protein follows directly from topology. A fusion protein brings together two lipid bilayers and creates a new connection between enclosed spaces. A membrane-disrupting protein acts on an existing cellular bilayer without supplying another bilayer to merge with it. Both may use exposed regions that interact with lipids, but the final connectivity and the biological meaning of the membrane event differ. The discovery that a viral protein associates with membranes does not, by itself, establish that it is a fusogen.

A non-enveloped particle may also use endosomal acidification or other features of the intracellular environment to promote a transition of its capsid. This does not mean that low pH universally causes viral envelope fusion. The same environmental cue can influence different physical structures in different organisms. In an enveloped virus it may act on a fusion glycoprotein; in a non-enveloped virus it may influence the capsid or the activity of a cellular processing system. A loss of infection following alteration of endosomal conditions is therefore consistent with several distinct mechanisms.

The endosomal lumen can also contain host enzymes whose activity depends on compartment identity and chemical conditions. For some viruses, processing of capsid components contributes to the generation of a penetration-competent form. Reovirus is a representative example of a virus in which changes of the outer capsid allow an internal particle to gain access to the cytosol. The internal core can remain organized around the genome and perform its initial RNA synthesis activities. Such an arrangement is particularly important for understanding why complete removal of genome-associated proteins is not the universal definition of successful uncoating.

The comparison of poliovirus, adenovirus and reovirus reveals that there is more than one possible relationship between the location of the genome and the fate of the shell. In one model, RNA is transferred out of much of its original capsid. In another, a partially reorganized DNA-containing particle escapes before genome release near its nuclear destination. In another, a functionally active core remains intact enough to support transcription. These examples should be learned as solutions to the same biological problem, not as an indiscriminate list of viral families.

Membrane integrity measurements must be interpreted carefully in this context. Evidence that material normally retained in the endosomal lumen has become accessible to the cytosol supports a change in membrane permeability or integrity. It does not automatically establish the shape, size or lifetime of the opening, nor does it show that the viral genome has reached a functional intracellular state. A small change of permeability and large-scale membrane disruption are different phenomena, and assays designed to measure them may have different sensitivities. The scope of an experimental conclusion should match the actual readout.

A cell can respond to membrane damage or the appearance of material in an unusual compartment through repair and defensive mechanisms. However, the details of these pathways are not the focus of the current node. What matters mechanistically is that a virus interacting with a host membrane encounters a living, regulated system rather than an inert film. The eventual fate of a membrane-penetrating particle reflects both its structural properties and the cellular context. These considerations further explain why a membrane interaction observed in an artificial system is not sufficient, by itself, to establish a productive biological outcome.
