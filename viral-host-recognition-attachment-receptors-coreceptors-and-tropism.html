The problem of selective infection

A virus encountered by an animal is surrounded by many possible cells, but it cannot use all of them equally. A particle in the airway may pass several epithelial cell types, extracellular mucus and soluble molecules before it contacts a membrane. A particle in the blood may encounter erythrocytes, leukocytes, endothelial cells and proteins in plasma. These surfaces are chemically different even though they belong to the same organism. The first question is therefore not simply whether the virus is present near a cell, but whether the two surfaces can make a biologically consequential molecular contact. Selective attachment is one reason that an infection affecting the respiratory tract does not necessarily involve every tissue in the body.

A virion is a complete extracellular viral particle containing a genome protected by viral structural proteins and, in many viruses, a lipid envelope. Unlike a metabolically autonomous bacterium, a virion does not possess the machinery needed to make a full new generation of particles on its own. Its genetic information must reach a compatible intracellular environment. Viral attachment is the initial stable association between a particle and a component of the host-cell surface. This interaction need not involve immediate membrane crossing, and it may be reversible. Although attachment is often described as the first stage of the infectious cycle, physical collision with a cell is not itself attachment: the contacting surfaces must make interactions of sufficient duration and specificity to be detected or to allow subsequent events.

The distinction between exposure and infection becomes clearer when we think about a cell whose exterior lacks the molecular structure recognized by a particular virus. Such a cell can be surrounded by particles and yet remain resistant to the normal route of infection. Conversely, a cell bearing the recognized molecule can retain particles on its surface without ever producing viral progeny. The second case illustrates that successful recognition is only a gateway. Passage through that gateway must be followed by penetration, appropriate uncoating and compatible intracellular events. A definition of infection based solely on the presence of viral material at a cell surface therefore confuses an early necessary event with an entire biological process.

Tropism describes the preference or restriction of a virus for certain host species, tissues or cell types. At the cellular level, a tropism statement should ideally identify the biological endpoint: preferential binding, entry, viral gene expression or productive infection. These endpoints are not interchangeable. In an intact animal the distribution of susceptible cells is combined with anatomical exposure, local conditions, extracellular barriers and host responses. It is consequently possible for cells to be susceptible in isolation but rarely encountered by a virus in vivo. This principle connects the current chapter with the recognition and colonization framework established in the earlier nodes without assuming that bacteria and viruses have identical entry strategies.

Throughout this chapter, the term recognition refers to molecular recognition of a host feature by a viral component. It should not be confused with host immunological recognition of viral antigens by an antibody, B-cell receptor or pattern-recognition receptor. In one relationship the virus interacts with a cellular surface; in the other, the immune system detects the virus or its products. Some molecules may participate in more than one type of interaction, but their classification depends on the event being explained. This distinction will prevent a common examination error in which any molecule binding a virus is automatically called its entry receptor.

How the receptor concept emerged

George Hirst's 1941 observation of influenza-associated red-cell agglutination and his 1942 investigations of adsorption and elution provided an important distinction between a physical association and intracellular multiplication. Erythrocytes participate in the aggregation phenomenon without being the ordinary site of productive influenza infection. The observation was useful because it isolated one property of extracellular particles from a much longer biological sequence. If disappearance of particles from a fluid were taken as proof of multiplication, this distinction would be impossible to explain.

The aggregate arises from multiple interactions linking neighboring cellular surfaces. Its macroscopic appearance is an indirect indicator of recognition and does not give the dissociation constant of an individual molecular contact. The later identification of the importance of terminal sialic acids and of neuraminidase as a receptor-cleaving enzyme helped account for the different events of particle association and release. The molecular explanation was developed over time; the earliest physical observations did not immediately establish every structural detail of the modern model.

An important result in a different recognition system came in 1989, when Mendelsohn, Wimmer and Racaniello described the cellular poliovirus receptor, now known as CD155, as an immunoglobulin-superfamily protein. Their work highlighted a distinction between the distribution of receptor transcripts and actual detectable virus-binding sites. This is an illustration of a general principle: molecular identity and accessible functional display are related but not identical. The same principle helps students understand why a tissue expression chart alone cannot establish the complete distribution of infection.

The HIV receptor history illustrates another form of scientific revision. The identification of CD4 was not invalidated by the later recognition of the importance of chemokine coreceptors. Rather, the earlier model was expanded to explain observations that CD4 alone could not account for. A scientific model can be incomplete while some of its individual components remain strongly supported. In examination questions, evidence that one component is necessary does not establish that no other component is necessary.

The surfaces that meet

An animal plasma membrane is a lipid bilayer that contains many proteins and membrane-associated carbohydrates. The extracellular domain of a transmembrane protein can project beyond the bilayer, while its cytoplasmic domain may interact with intracellular molecules. Glycoproteins contain carbohydrate chains covalently attached to protein; glycolipids bear carbohydrate headgroups attached to membrane lipids. These molecules contribute to the glycocalyx, a carbohydrate-rich layer that can extend outward from the membrane. A virus contacting the cell is thus more likely to encounter a complex hydrated molecular landscape than a bare sheet of phospholipid.

An enveloped virion has a lipid bilayer acquired from a cellular membrane during its formation. The envelope incorporates viral proteins that provide molecular identity. Their outward-facing domains may contain receptor-binding surfaces, and some also have a structural role in membrane fusion. The presence of a lipid envelope does not mean that the particle simply dissolves into any other membrane. Biological bilayers are stable structures, and membrane merger requires a controlled rearrangement of their constituent lipids. The detailed physical transition belongs to the next chapter. At this stage, what matters is that exposed viral glycoproteins establish contact before such a transition can occur.

In a non-enveloped virus the outermost particle structure is a protein capsid. Capsid subunits assemble into a protective shell around the genome or a genome-associated complex. Surface loops, depressions, protrusions or other protein domains can provide the cellular recognition interface. The capsid is therefore not merely packaging: the same structural assembly that protects the genome may also contribute to receptor recognition. A non-enveloped virus does not require a lipid envelope to bind specifically. Its exposed proteins can perform molecular recognition just as a viral envelope protein can. Conversely, it cannot merge a viral lipid bilayer with the cell membrane because it has no such bilayer.

For both types of particles, the recognition surface must be physically accessible. A molecular site may be structurally present but obscured by another protein domain or a carbohydrate chain. Protein conformation determines which side chains face the solvent, and the orientation of a glycan determines which parts of it can be contacted. A change that does not remove a protein altogether can therefore change what is exposed on the outside of the particle. Similarly, a cellular protein detected in a whole-cell extract may be retained in an intracellular compartment rather than presented on the plasma membrane. The relevant quantity for initial attachment is accessible surface recognition material, not total cellular protein abundance.

The geometry of both partners matters because viral surfaces often present several copies of the same structural protein. Several weak contacts can hold a particle more effectively than a single weak contact if the corresponding cellular sites are appropriately spaced. In contrast, a high-affinity contact demonstrated with isolated proteins may not be possible when those proteins are oriented differently in their native membranes. This is why structural descriptions should be interpreted alongside evidence from intact cells rather than treated as complete explanations of host range.

Non-enveloped recognition: two representative systems

A common mistake in comparing viral structures is to imagine that an envelope is required for any meaningful interaction with a host membrane. The outer shell of a non-enveloped particle can itself carry the recognition machinery. Its protein subunits are arranged into a stable structure that protects the viral genome while exposing particular portions of the protein surface. Some portions may be mainly structural, whereas others interact with cellular molecules. These roles need not be distributed evenly across all parts of the capsid.

Poliovirus provides a classical example of this distinction. Its compact capsid presents a receptor recognition region in a depression often described as a canyon. The word canyon is a structural description, not a literal hollow channel extending to the viral genome. The extracellular region of CD155 recognizes a surface of the assembled capsid. This association does not involve an envelope glycoprotein, because the particle is non-enveloped. The example demonstrates that a folded protein surface can act as a receptor-binding interface without protruding from a lipid bilayer.

A structure of a molecular complex provides evidence about the relative positions of its components. If a receptor domain is seen in a particular location relative to a capsid, the observation constrains plausible explanations of contact. However, the picture is not necessarily a complete description of all motions in solution or at the cell surface. Flexible regions may adopt different conformations, and a structure may represent one state of a dynamic complex. Structural biology therefore answers the question of how components can be arranged, while complementary functional evidence is required to connect that arrangement to a biological outcome.

CD155 illustrates why naming a host protein is not the end of a scientific explanation of tropism. The cell protein has a molecular identity and a distribution that can be studied independently of its interaction with poliovirus. Its immunoglobulin-like structural domains are not evidence that it functions as an antibody. The immunoglobulin superfamily includes diverse cellular proteins with related folded domains and different physiological roles. Learning to distinguish a shared fold from a shared function is important because terms derived from structural biology can otherwise be misinterpreted as complete functional descriptions.

Adenoviruses provide a different non-enveloped architecture. Their capsids bear outward-projecting fiber structures at vertices. The distal portion of a fiber, often called the knob, participates in receptor recognition in many adenovirus types. In the classical example involving certain human adenoviruses, the coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor, abbreviated CAR, participates in initial association. This name reflects its recognition by different viruses, illustrating that a host molecule need not be exclusive to one virus family. A host receptor is named for a biological relationship, not for ownership by a particular pathogen.

In the classical adenovirus model, other capsid components and cellular integrins contribute to subsequent interactions. The distinction between initial attachment and a later cellular interaction is particularly instructive because it shows that sequential recognition is not confined to enveloped viruses. It would therefore be incorrect to equate the term coreceptor with the presence of a viral lipid bilayer. Some non-enveloped viruses employ more than one cellular interaction during entry, while some enveloped viruses are described using a single principal receptor.

The poliovirus and adenovirus examples should not be collapsed into one mechanism merely because both particles lack envelopes. One presents a recognition interface in a relatively compact capsid surface, while the other has prominent projecting fibers in the classical structural model. Different architectures solve the same general biological problem: establishing a meaningful interaction with a cellular surface. The arrangement of the recognition component is determined by the structure of the particle, rather than by a universal requirement that the binding domain have a particular shape.

The diversity of adenovirus receptors is another reason to state the scope of a textbook example. Different adenovirus types can recognize different cellular molecules. CAR is a useful representative receptor, but it is not appropriate to treat it as the only possible receptor for the entire family. This caution parallels the earlier distinction between common influenza glycan preferences and absolute claims about every influenza lineage. In an examination, a statement about the classical model may be correct even when a universal statement about the whole family is not.

These examples also make the boundary with the next chapter clear. Once a non-enveloped particle is associated with the cell, it cannot carry out fusion of its own lipid envelope with the plasma membrane. It must use a different way of making its genome accessible across the membrane barrier. The details of membrane penetration and uncoating are not needed to understand why the exposed capsid can recognize the cell, but they are required to understand how the particle proceeds from recognition to infection.

Membrane organization and accessible receptor display

Cells of different lineages have different combinations of surface molecules because their physiological tasks differ. A lymphocyte and an epithelial cell may contain much of the same genomic information while expressing different proteins and displaying different carbohydrates. A virus encounters the molecular results of differentiation, not a uniform animal cell surface. The same host organism can therefore contain cell populations with markedly different recognition properties.

A transmembrane protein must be synthesized, folded and transported before its extracellular domain can be accessible at the cell surface. A protein detected in a whole-cell extract may include immature material in intracellular compartments. Its messenger RNA is even further removed from the functional surface measurement. Thus gene expression, total protein and surface availability are separate quantities. A comparison of these levels can reveal that an apparent discrepancy is not a contradiction but a consequence of cellular organization.

For glycans, cellular biosynthesis introduces another level of variation. A glycoprotein's amino-acid sequence is specified by a gene, but the carbohydrate structures attached to it depend on cellular processing. Two cells can contain the same polypeptide and nevertheless display different glycan patterns. Because the influenza example involves recognition of a carbohydrate feature, a list of membrane protein names alone cannot provide a complete description of the surface being recognized.

The glycocalyx has spatial depth. A small molecular probe may reach a domain that is relatively inaccessible to a large particle. Furthermore, epithelial cells have polarized membranes: apical and basolateral surfaces can differ in the molecules they display. A molecule on one surface is not necessarily exposed to material in another anatomical compartment. This provides a biological explanation for why total abundance is not equivalent to accessibility.

Membrane proteins also differ in their lateral mobility and distribution. A measurement of average surface abundance does not describe every local arrangement of molecular sites. This distinction is important when interpreting multivalent association, because the spacing of contacts contributes to the behavior of a complex. These principles follow from general membrane biophysics rather than being specific to a single virus.

The material outside a cell is chemically complex. Soluble glycoproteins and extracellular matrix can present interacting structures without being entry receptors. The association of a particle with extracellular material may alter its observed distribution without establishing that any cell membrane has been crossed. Tissue-level observations should therefore be connected carefully to the location and functional role of the molecules being considered.

Attachment factors, receptors and coreceptors

A host receptor in ordinary physiology may be a signaling molecule, an adhesion protein, a transporter or a cell-surface carbohydrate recognized by another molecule. In virology, the term receptor is used functionally for a cellular molecule exploited during recognition or entry. Its native physiological role does not have to involve viruses. The host molecule may have existed long before the viral recognition mechanism evolved. The viral component that contacts it is often called a receptor-binding protein or attachment protein. Calling both partners receptors creates ambiguity, so it is useful to state explicitly which molecule is viral and which belongs to the host.

An attachment factor increases the association or retention of a particle at the surface without necessarily completing the recognition events that permit entry. A functional entry receptor contributes directly to the transition toward entry. Some molecules do both jobs. Others promote surface contact while a different molecule participates in a subsequent recognition step. The distinction is established by evidence about function, not by the chemical class of the molecule. A carbohydrate can be a physiologically meaningful receptor, and a protein can act only as an attachment factor in a particular system. It is incorrect to define the former as invariably nonspecific or the latter as invariably sufficient.

A coreceptor is an additional cellular component used together with a primary receptor in a particular entry mechanism. The word does not imply that it is always present in a stable complex with the primary receptor before a viral particle arrives. In a sequential mechanism, the first contact can alter the shape or accessibility of a viral binding surface, making another interaction possible. The additional interaction may distinguish two cells that express the same primary receptor. Coreceptors are not universal requirements of all viruses. In some systems a single identified cellular molecule accounts for much of the recognition step; in others the division among initial docking, entry-receptor engagement and accessory factors is complex.

Three conceptual claims must be kept separate. First, a viral protein can bind a cellular molecule. Second, the cellular molecule can be necessary for a defined stage of infection in a particular cellular context. Third, the molecule can be sufficient for that stage under stated conditions. Binding alone establishes neither necessity nor sufficiency. Necessity means that the specified outcome fails in the absence of that factor under the conditions considered. Sufficiency means that the factor accounts for the outcome when the relevant background conditions are already satisfied. This is conditional biological reasoning, not a statement that one molecule can replace every cellular function.

An examination question may provide several observations about a hypothetical virus, such as surface association, internal signal and later viral expression. These observations should be read in their temporal order. If surface association is present but the later signal is absent, it is possible that the factor being considered has an attachment role but not a complete entry role. If the earliest observable event is absent, subsequent events may disappear merely because the earlier step failed. The later negative result by itself does not prove that the same factor has a direct biochemical role in every downstream stage.

Molecular recognition is best viewed as a conditional transition between states rather than as a permanent identity tag. A cellular molecule that is abundant at one developmental stage may be less accessible at another. The local membrane can also contain structures that screen an otherwise compatible surface. These facts explain why statements such as receptor positive and receptor negative are often useful experimental classifications but insufficient descriptions of all cell biology.

Structural complementarity, conformational states and specificity

A binding interface is rarely a perfect rigid mold. Proteins are dynamic molecules whose atoms fluctuate around conformations favored by the chemical environment. The familiar lock-and-key analogy is useful for explaining why two surfaces may be compatible, but it becomes misleading when interpreted as a literal picture of immovable structures. A receptor and a ligand can undergo changes in local shape or arrangement while forming a complex. In some systems a pre-existing conformation is preferentially recognized; in others the formation of the interaction is accompanied by further rearrangement. These descriptions are not mutually exclusive in all molecular systems.

The structural specificity of recognition is also distinct from its selectivity at the cellular level. A protein might associate with a particular chemical group in isolation, yet only a subset of cells displays that group in a suitable arrangement. Conversely, two distinct cellular molecules may contain related structural features that are recognized by the same viral protein. A recognition specificity is therefore a statement about molecular compatibility, while the distribution of compatible structures across cells is a separate biological question.

To understand the importance of conformation, consider a noninfectious model protein composed of two folded domains connected by a flexible region. One domain contains a ligand-binding pocket. A second domain may partially obscure the pocket in one conformation and leave it exposed in another. A structural illustration of the two states can explain why the total amount of protein is not equivalent to the number of available binding pockets. No change in the number of molecules is needed for their measured accessibility to differ. The same general concept is used in enzyme regulation and receptor signaling.

Allostery extends this principle by linking changes at different locations of a molecule. A ligand associating with one part of a protein can alter the distribution of conformations elsewhere. The relevant effect may be a change in shape, flexibility or exposure of another interface. The sequential HIV recognition model is an example in which understanding such coupling is more useful than memorizing an isolated list of four proteins. The initial interaction with CD4 and the subsequent interaction with an appropriate cellular coreceptor belong to one connected process rather than two unrelated binding events.

The chemical environment also influences how molecular groups are presented. Charged groups are surrounded by water and ions; their interactions occur within this environment rather than in a vacuum. A conceptual account of recognition based only on attractive charges would overlook solvent effects, geometric constraints and the presence of neutral interacting groups. Similarly, the hydrophobic effect is not a simple direct attraction between every pair of nonpolar atoms. It involves changes in the organization of surrounding water when nonpolar surfaces are buried or exposed. These general principles connect viral recognition with physical chemistry without requiring a specialist structural analysis of a pathogen.

A distinction between complementarity and complement is useful here. Complementarity describes compatibility of interacting molecular surfaces. Complement is the name of a host immune system of proteins. Although both concepts can appear in the biology of infection, the word complementarity in the molecular-recognition discussion does not refer to activation of the complement cascade. The latter is part of the immunology subunit. Clear terminology prevents a student from confusing a physical property of binding with a separate host-defense process.

A molecular complex may be stabilized by several types of noncovalent interaction simultaneously. Hydrogen bonds are directional, whereas electrostatic interactions depend on the distribution of charges and the surrounding medium. Close atomic contacts can contribute van der Waals interactions. The organization of water around an interface also affects the free-energy change. A binding pocket shown as a colored region in a molecular diagram therefore represents the net consequence of many physical interactions rather than one universal kind of chemical bond. A student should not assign a specific chemical force to a diagram unless the legend or evidence identifies it.

Molecular specificity does not imply absolute exclusivity. The word specific often means that an interaction favors some partners over others under defined conditions. Biological interactions can exhibit a spectrum of association strengths, and a measurement may have a threshold below which an interaction is not detected. A receptor specificity statement should therefore identify the molecular partner and context rather than claim that every other conceivable molecular interaction is physically impossible. This is the same reasoning used when distinguishing antibody cross-reactivity from a lack of antigen specificity.

Molecular recognition by proteins and carbohydrates

A protein-binding interface is shaped by the three-dimensional arrangement of amino-acid side chains and other chemical groups. Residues brought close together by protein folding need not be adjacent in the primary sequence. An interface can include hydrogen-bonding groups, regions of complementary charge and surfaces whose association reorganizes surrounding water. The number of individual contacts is not, by itself, a measure of total binding free energy because solvent effects and changes in molecular motion also matter.

Carbohydrate structure varies through stereochemistry, glycosidic linkage and branching. The same terminal sugar may be presented in different spatial contexts when linked to other residues in different ways. A glycan's biological recognition properties are not captured simply by listing its constituent monosaccharides. This is the general glycobiological foundation of the influenza HA example.

The meaning of receptor depends on biological context. Hormone receptors often participate in signaling, but a recognition structure used by a virus need not have an intracellular signaling domain. A carbohydrate can be a functional component of a recognition mechanism. Equally, a host protein used in viral recognition can have a normal role in adhesion or immune communication. The term viral receptor describes the role of a cellular feature in a pathogen interaction, not a unique structural class.

CD4 and chemokine receptors illustrate that ordinary host-cell molecules can have distinct roles depending on their molecular partners. The function of CD4 in helper T-cell biology is not the same as its interaction with the HIV envelope. CCR5 and CXCR4 belong to a shared structural family but are not interchangeable in all biological contexts. A structural classification does not establish that all members of the family recognize identical partners.

The distinction between binding and catalysis is also important. A recognition event can occur without changing the chemical structure of the bound ligand. An enzyme recognizes a substrate and participates in chemical conversion. HA-mediated recognition and NA-mediated sialic acid cleavage represent these different functional categories. A student must identify the consequence of an interaction before assigning the molecule its biological role.

Molecular forces and quantitative interpretation

Recognition does not generally require the formation of a permanent covalent bond between the virus and the host. Complementary molecular surfaces can be held together by hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, van der Waals interactions and the effects of excluding or reorganizing water around contacting surfaces. Each interaction depends on the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms. A negatively charged group on a host molecule may interact favorably with a positively charged region of a protein, but charge alone cannot establish specificity. Shape, orientation, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, and the energetic cost of rearranging solvent all contribute to the overall result.

The association of two molecules can be represented using a simple reversible equilibrium. Consider an isolated noninfectious model ligand L and binding site R that form a complex LR. At equilibrium, the dissociation constant Kd equals the product of free ligand and free receptor concentrations divided by the complex concentration. If concentrations are expressed in moles per litre, Kd has the same unit. Let the total number of receptor sites be represented by total receptor concentration, which is the sum of free and bound sites. Substituting the equilibrium relationship into this conservation equation gives the bound fraction as free ligand concentration divided by the sum of free ligand concentration and Kd. This relationship applies to one class of independent sites and assumes an equilibrium description.

The bound fraction equals one half when the free ligand concentration equals Kd. It approaches zero when ligand concentration is very small relative to Kd and approaches one when ligand concentration is much greater than Kd. The approach to one reflects finite occupancy: once all sites are occupied, more ligand cannot produce more occupied sites in the same population. A binding curve can therefore plateau even in the complete absence of any biological entry process. Saturation is evidence compatible with a limited set of binding sites, but it is not proof of a functional infection receptor.

The equilibrium description also separates affinity from capacity. Affinity is the strength of an individual ligand-site interaction expressed in terms of an equilibrium constant, whereas capacity refers to the amount of ligand that can be associated with a given sample at saturation. Two samples can have identical fractional binding curves but different total capacities. A comparison of raw signal alone can be misleading if the samples contain different amounts of accessible binding material. Normalizing to site abundance, or interpreting the expected maximal signal separately from the concentration giving half-maximal occupancy, helps clarify the difference.

Avidity refers to the combined stability of multiple interacting contacts. A single particle may make several contacts with a surface, and a partially detached particle may remain nearby because another contact persists. Reassociation can prolong the overall association even when an individual bond is short-lived. As a result, an apparent binding constant for an intact multivalent particle need not equal the dissociation constant measured for one isolated protein and one isolated ligand. This is a limitation of simple molecular models, not a contradiction of them. The one-site equation is useful as a conceptual baseline, but its assumptions must be stated whenever it is used to interpret a graph.

Association and dissociation can also occur at different rates. An equilibrium measurement cannot by itself distinguish a fast association and fast dissociation from a slower pair of rates that gives the same equilibrium ratio. In a simple elementary reversible model, the dissociation constant is the dissociation rate constant divided by the association rate constant. The latter has concentration-inverse time-inverse units and the former has time-inverse units. This algebra explains why their ratio has concentration units. A real cellular interface can involve multiple steps and need not follow the elementary model. Thus a numerical value is meaningful only with its defined system and assumptions.

Quantitative model assumptions and thermodynamics

For a simple isolated reversible molecular interaction, the total binding site concentration is equal to the sum of free and occupied sites. The definition of the equilibrium dissociation constant permits free site concentration to be expressed as the concentration of occupied sites multiplied by the ratio of Kd to free ligand. Substitution into the conservation equation gives the fraction occupied as free ligand divided by free ligand plus Kd. This derivation shows why the equation describes occupancy rather than viral entry or infectious progeny.

The standard free-energy change of association can be written as RT multiplied by the natural logarithm of Kd divided by the standard concentration of one mole per litre. R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. The concentration ratio is dimensionless, as required for the logarithm. The sign of the standard free-energy change refers to defined standard states. The actual distribution of free and bound molecules in a mixture also depends on their concentrations.

Equilibrium does not mean molecular movement stops. Individual complexes can form and dissociate while the overall fraction remains stable. In a simple reversible interaction, the rates of association and dissociation balance at equilibrium. A binding curve on a linear concentration scale can approach saturation gradually, while the same data plotted on a logarithmic concentration axis can appear sigmoidal. That visual difference alone is not proof of cooperativity.

A fitted dissociation constant is interpretable only in relation to the model used. Independent equivalent sites, a known free ligand concentration and a stable equilibrium are among the assumptions of the simplest equation. In a heterogeneous or multivalent system, an apparent binding parameter may combine multiple interactions and structural states. A real cellular interface is more complex than an isolated noninfectious model ligand and its site. The numerical exercises here use the simplified inert system to teach graph interpretation and are not estimates of virus infectivity.

Influenza recognition of sialylated glycans

Sialic acids are a family of acidic sugars commonly found at the outer ends of animal cell-surface carbohydrate chains. N-acetylneuraminic acid is a prominent member of this family. It can be attached to another sugar, often galactose, through different glycosidic linkages. The notation alpha-2,3 and alpha-2,6 specifies how the participating sugar atoms are connected. These are different chemical arrangements, not two names for the same isolated sugar. They can present the terminal residue in distinct orientations within a larger carbohydrate chain.

Conventional influenza A virions display hemagglutinin, abbreviated HA, on their lipid envelopes. Hemagglutinin is an oligomeric viral glycoprotein whose extracellular region contains a recognition site for suitable sialylated glycans. The name originated in the observation that influenza particles can agglutinate red blood cells. Agglutination means that cells form a visible association because a multivalent agent links them together. This historical observation revealed that influenza particles recognized a feature on erythrocyte surfaces. It did not establish that erythrocytes were normal cells supporting the complete influenza replication cycle.

The carbohydrate recognized by HA need not be an independent membrane protein. It may be displayed as part of a glycoprotein or a glycolipid. This provides a clear counterexample to the belief that every viral receptor is a transmembrane protein with a cytoplasmic signaling domain. In a glycan-mediated recognition system, the sugar presentation and the molecular environment are relevant. Detecting free sialic acid in a sample is not equivalent to demonstrating that a particular cell displays an accessible glycan architecture appropriate for viral recognition.

A common textbook generalization is that many human-adapted seasonal influenza A viruses preferentially recognize alpha-2,6-linked sialylated glycans, whereas many avian influenza A viruses preferentially recognize alpha-2,3-linked forms. The word preferentially is essential. It describes a broad tendency, not an absolute division among all viruses or all tissues. Both linkage classes can occur within a host species, and the larger structure of the glycan also influences recognition. Linkage preference alone cannot establish that a virus will spread efficiently or cause disease in a particular species. These whole-organism outcomes depend on additional biological conditions beyond attachment.

Influenza particles also display neuraminidase, abbreviated NA, which can cleave terminal sialic acids from suitable substrates. The distinction between HA and NA is functional: HA participates in recognition of sialylated surfaces, whereas NA possesses receptor-cleaving enzymatic activity. A student may wonder how a virus can carry both a binding protein and an enzyme that removes related binding structures. There is no logical contradiction because the two proteins perform different functions within the viral life cycle. The recognition of a suitable surface and the later separation of particles from sialylated material are distinct biological problems. Detailed entry and release mechanisms are beyond the present chapter, but the distinction prevents the false claim that HA and NA are interchangeable receptor-binding proteins.

Not every influenza A lineage follows the conventional sialic-acid model. Certain bat influenza A viruses use MHC class II molecules rather than conventional sialylated glycans as entry receptors. This is a useful narrowly framed exception, not an alternative general rule for human seasonal influenza. It illustrates why a family-level description should not replace the identification of the actual virus being discussed. The examination-level principle is to distinguish a typical example from a universal claim and to avoid extending one receptor preference to all members of a diverse viral group.

Receptors in their ordinary cellular roles

The normal function of a host molecule is often the best starting point for understanding where it is displayed. Cells maintain surface molecules because those molecules participate in physiology, development and communication. A cell that expresses a particular adhesion protein or signaling receptor has not acquired it for the purpose of viral entry. Viral recognition uses features of existing biological systems. This relationship explains why learning receptor biology in the earlier signaling and communication subunits is valuable for understanding host–pathogen interactions.

CD4 is associated particularly with helper T-cell function. In ordinary immune recognition, CD4 acts in conjunction with the T-cell receptor system and interacts with MHC class II molecules. This physiological interaction is different from the interaction of CD4 with HIV gp120. The two contexts should be kept separate even though the same cellular protein is involved. The location of CD4 on particular immune cells is related to their normal immune identity and function, while the use of CD4 by HIV helps explain the association of the virus with those cell populations.

CD4 expression is not limited to a single perfectly uniform population of lymphocytes. Some other immune cells can express the molecule, and immune cell populations differ in developmental and activation states. Even within a population classified using a surface marker, the cells may have different intracellular properties. This is why a category defined by one marker does not necessarily define all biological behavior of that category. The lesson is particularly important when a question moves from naming a receptor to predicting the cellular distribution of infection.

Chemokines are small signaling proteins involved in the organization and movement of cells, particularly in immune contexts. Chemokine receptors belong to the broader family of cellular membrane receptors that communicate extracellular information. CCR5 and CXCR4 illustrate how members of that family can also be used as recognition components in a pathogen interaction. Their normal identities as chemokine receptors remain valid; describing them as HIV coreceptors does not replace their physiological classification.

The physiological state of a receptor can be affected by the ordinary processes that regulate membrane proteins. Cells synthesize and degrade receptors, deliver them to the surface and internalize some of them. These processes help regulate cellular responsiveness to normal signals. The result is that surface presentation can change over time even within a single cell lineage. An observed distribution of receptor molecules is therefore a description of cells at a particular state and time, not necessarily a permanent feature of every cell of that type.

This regulation should not be confused with a change in the host species or the identity of a virus. A tissue can contain multiple cellular states within one organism. Likewise, a receptor-bearing population can change in size as cells differentiate or move. When reasoning about tropism, these differences explain why a biological distribution can be dynamic without requiring the claim that a viral recognition protein has changed. The host contributes to the observed pattern through its own physiology.

An analogy with hormone signaling helps clarify the distinction between molecular binding and its physiological outcome. A hormone can bind a receptor, but the eventual response depends on cellular signaling machinery and the cell's state. Two cell types that bind the same hormone may respond differently because they contain different downstream components. In viral biology, the downstream events are not the same as hormone signaling, but the general logic is analogous: recognition establishes an interaction, whereas the outcome depends on additional properties of the system.

The use of an adhesion protein as a viral receptor also should not lead to the conclusion that every adhesion molecule can serve in that role. Adhesion molecules are structurally and functionally diverse, and their normal partners differ. The classical adenovirus recognition example refers to particular cellular molecules, not to adhesion as an undifferentiated property. Identifying a receptor requires evidence about the relevant molecular interaction rather than a general observation that a cell has an adhesive surface.

HIV recognition of CD4 and chemokine coreceptors

The HIV envelope provides a textbook example of a recognition mechanism requiring more than one cellular molecule. Its envelope glycoprotein complex contains an outward-facing component, gp120, associated with the transmembrane component gp41. The precursor glycoprotein gp160 is processed into these components during viral protein maturation. On a mature envelope, the glycoprotein complexes are arranged as oligomeric spikes. Their architecture brings receptor recognition and the later membrane-merging function into a coordinated structural assembly, although the two functions are not identical.

CD4 is a host cell-surface glycoprotein important in immune biology. It participates in the function of helper T lymphocytes and is also expressed by some other immune cell populations. In the context of HIV recognition, CD4 provides the principal initial receptor interaction for typical HIV-1 entry. Binding of gp120 to CD4 changes the conformational state of the envelope complex, exposing or reorganizing a site that can interact with a suitable chemokine coreceptor. This illustrates a general principle of allostery: contact at one location of a protein can affect the structural properties of another region.

CCR5 and CXCR4 are chemokine receptors that normally participate in cellular communication. Both belong to the seven-transmembrane receptor family. In HIV biology, they can function as coreceptors for appropriate viral envelope variants. R5 and X4 terminology describes use of CCR5 and CXCR4, respectively; some variants can use both. These terms refer to a coreceptor usage phenotype and should not automatically be translated into an exclusive list of infected cell types. Cell differentiation state, receptor abundance and additional biological restrictions complicate the relationship between coreceptor usage and the actual cell populations infected.

A chemokine receptor is not a viral protein. It is a host molecule recruited into a viral recognition process. Similarly, CD4 is not a viral antigen. This simple distinction becomes important when answering a question that lists gp120, gp41, CD4 and CCR5 together. The first pair belongs to the viral envelope complex, whereas the latter pair is cellular. The initial gp120–CD4 interaction is distinct from recognition of the appropriate coreceptor. A cell may support the first interaction but fail to support the next. In that case, the presence of the primary receptor has not guaranteed successful entry.

The early discoveries of CD4 involvement in HIV infection were reported by independent groups in 1984. Subsequent evidence demonstrated that human CD4 could account for susceptibility in some cellular settings. Nevertheless, observations in other settings showed that CD4 alone did not explain every restriction. In 1996, work identifying CXCR4, originally called fusin in this context, and CCR5 clarified why the initial CD4 model was incomplete. These historical findings support a principle about how scientific explanations develop: a strongly supported component of a mechanism need not be the whole mechanism. Adding a second required component can reconcile observations that appeared inconsistent under a one-receptor explanation.

The role of gp41 is considered here only to distinguish it from gp120. The gp41 component participates in the downstream membrane-fusion function of the envelope complex; gp120 is centrally involved in the recognition of CD4 and the appropriate coreceptor. It is misleading to attribute all recognition and membrane merger to gp120 alone. The physical rearrangements that allow bilayers to fuse, and the distinction between fusion and delivery of the genome, will be developed in the next node rather than repeated here.

Susceptibility, permissiveness and levels of tropism

Susceptibility and permissiveness are useful but differently defined properties in the classical virology literature. A susceptible cell can be entered by a virus through the relevant natural or experimental exposure route. A permissive cell has the intracellular conditions necessary for the particular virus to complete its replication cycle. A cell can possess the second property while lacking the first, or possess the first while lacking the second. Because authors sometimes use these terms with different operational endpoints, it is important to identify how a particular study defines them before comparing results.

A classical textbook illustration is the difference between exposure of certain chick cells to intact poliovirus particles and the response of those cells to viral RNA delivered independently of natural particle recognition. The two observations were used to distinguish cell-surface restrictions from intracellular compatibility. The point is logical: failure at the initial gate cannot establish that every later stage is impossible. Nor does evidence that a genome can function in a particular cellular environment demonstrate that intact particles naturally reach that environment. This distinction is relevant to exam questions about susceptibility and permissiveness without requiring experimental implementation details.

Productive infection means that the cell yields newly produced infectious viral particles. Other outcomes can occur. In an abortive infection, the infectious process begins but fails to produce a complete productive outcome. Detection of an early viral marker can therefore coexist with absence of infectious progeny. A nonproductive outcome can arise for reasons other than receptor absence, because the receptor has already served its early role in a cell that has proceeded to later stages. Conversely, absence of a viral marker does not identify a receptor defect unless the earlier recognition stages were assessed.

Host range describes the spectrum of organisms in which a virus can establish the specified infection outcome. Tissue tropism concerns the organs or tissues preferentially involved within a host, and cell tropism concerns the kinds of cells that participate. These are related scales, but one cannot be inferred completely from another. A receptor detected in a tissue may be confined to a small subpopulation of cells. A tissue with compatible cells may be protected from initial exposure by anatomy. A cell in an exposed tissue may lack an intracellular requirement. A meaningful tropism explanation therefore follows the chain from organism-level accessibility to the molecular surface and then to the intracellular response.

Receptor gene expression is also distinct from receptor protein localization. Messenger RNA abundance indicates that a transcript is present, not necessarily that correctly folded protein is displayed at the relevant cellular surface. A whole-cell protein measurement includes intracellular material. An extracellular recognition event depends on the abundance, accessibility and structure of the surface-associated form. This is a general connection with protein trafficking and membrane organization studied earlier in the Academy. In a polarized epithelial cell, molecules can be distributed differently on surfaces facing different compartments. A tissue-level map that neglects orientation can misrepresent which molecular features are accessible.

An infection can cause disease when it disrupts cell or tissue function, but productive infection and clinical disease are also separate concepts. Tissue injury may reflect loss of infected cells, altered physiological function or the response of the host. Some infections produce little detectable illness. A receptor cannot be described as a disease severity determinant without additional evidence linking the relevant molecular interaction to tissue-level consequences. The present chapter stops at that conceptual connection; detailed disease cases belong to a later node.

Connecting cellular targeting to anatomy and ecology

Host range is sometimes described as a list of species, but a list alone hides the criteria by which those species were included. An organism may have been exposed to a virus, may show evidence of an immune response, may support a localized infection or may support extensive replication. These are different observations. A scientifically meaningful host-range claim identifies the outcome under consideration. The importance of this distinction is not restricted to virology; ecological descriptions of host–parasite interactions generally require clarity about whether an association is transient or biologically sustained.

At the level of anatomy, a virus reaches only a portion of the body's potential target surfaces during a particular exposure. Epithelial barriers separate compartments, and cells in different tissues are not equally accessible from every route. A host-range or tropism description that includes only receptor identity overlooks this organization. This is why an observation made with isolated cells cannot automatically be interpreted as the distribution of infection within an intact animal.

Tissue tropism and cellular tropism are also distinct even when a particular organ is named. A tissue includes cells that perform different physiological roles and may include immune cells, vascular cells and structural cells. If a viral marker is detected somewhere in the organ, the result does not necessarily identify which type of cell contains the marker. Microscopic localization and cellular identity are separate kinds of information. This is a general lesson about translating a tissue-level observation into a cell-level explanation.

The concept of an anatomical compartment is useful in understanding why extracellular association cannot always be assigned to a particular cellular receptor. Material located in mucus or other extracellular regions can be detected in a sample from a tissue without being inside the tissue's cells. Likewise, a sample that contains blood may include material associated with cells or proteins circulating through that tissue. A biological sample has a physical origin, but that origin does not by itself reveal the precise cellular localization of everything detected in it.

The distinction between presence and biological outcome also matters in comparative biology. A molecule may be conserved across species while differing in distribution or sequence context. Even when an extracellular recognition event is similar in two organisms, the subsequent intracellular environment may not be identical. Conversely, failure of a whole-organism outcome does not identify which molecular stage failed. A species comparison should therefore be interpreted with the same stage-specific reasoning that is used for a comparison between two cell types.

Plant cells present a particularly different physical context. Their plasma membranes lie inside cell walls, and the organization of plant tissues creates barriers that are not equivalent to the exposed plasma membranes used in many animal-virus textbook models. Many plant virus infections are associated with wounds or vector-mediated delivery, rather than an ordinary animal-style initial receptor interaction on an exposed cell membrane. The later movement of viral material through plant tissues raises additional cellular questions. The detailed routes and plant-specific mechanisms are assigned to later nodes, but the contrast is useful for preventing a misleading generalization from animal virus recognition to all organisms.

The relation between host and virus is an evolutionary relationship rather than a static correspondence between a species name and a molecule. Host surfaces and viral populations have histories, and the observed associations reflect multiple biological constraints. At the examination level, it is sufficient to recognize that related viruses can differ in their recognition properties and that related host species can differ in cellular and physiological properties. A receptor match is one component of the explanation, not a standalone prediction of transmission or disease.

When a question asks why a virus is associated with one tissue rather than another, the most complete answer often begins with a distinction among anatomical access, surface recognition and intracellular compatibility. The question may provide enough information to identify one of these as the relevant difference. If not, an answer claiming that a particular receptor is absent is more specific than the evidence warrants. This disciplined interpretation is a useful bridge from textbook mechanisms to the reasoning expected in unfamiliar Part C problems.

Reading evidence without overinterpreting it

Evidence for a molecular recognition relationship may come from different kinds of observations. A structural model can identify a contact interface between two molecular partners. A biochemical measurement can indicate whether isolated molecules interact. A cellular observation can reveal whether particles associate with a surface. Each type of evidence answers a different question. A structure alone does not show that the interaction is essential in an organism. An association measurement alone cannot show that the associated material crossed a membrane. A later viral marker alone cannot establish which surface molecule mediated its arrival.

A useful examination strategy is to label the stage represented by every observation before reading the answer options. A sample that shows strong surface signal and weak intracellular signal is not equivalent to a sample showing weak surface signal and weak intracellular signal. The first pattern suggests that the earliest observable association occurred and that a later event may be restricted. The second pattern leaves open the possibility of a restriction at the earliest stage. These are interpretations of a hypothetical teaching dataset, not a claim about the actual molecular mechanism of any named virus.

Negative observations require care. An absent signal can reflect a truly absent biological event, a detection limit or an unsuitable marker. A receptor stain can fail because the relevant epitope is inaccessible even if the protein is present. A binding signal can represent material that is attached nonspecifically, retained by extracellular structures or located on the cell itself. A strong biological conclusion requires an appropriate relationship between the measurement and the proposed mechanism. The most important question is whether the data distinguish the proposed explanation from reasonable alternative interpretations.

A correlation between receptor abundance and infection-associated signal is suggestive, but several properties of a cell may change together. For example, a differentiating cell may change membrane composition, intracellular metabolism and gene expression at the same time. If two observations vary together, the association does not automatically assign causation to one of them. A causal explanation should be consistent with independent evidence concerning the same proposed role. In an examination setting, a statement that a receptor is the sole determinant is generally stronger than what a simple correlation can support.

When evaluating claims about loss of a cellular component, students must also consider whether the change affects general cell viability or the integrity of the cell surface. A reduction in an endpoint can arise indirectly if the cell is no longer functioning normally. Conversely, persistence of a signal despite absence of one candidate does not rule out all receptor involvement: the endpoint could reflect another interaction, residual material or an alternative mechanism. The correct conclusion should match the specificity of the given evidence rather than a preferred textbook label.

Recognition at the cell surface is a selective biological process, not a sufficient explanation of every downstream outcome. This is the central connection among influenza glycan recognition, the HIV primary receptor and coreceptor example, and the general distinction between susceptibility and permissiveness. Once a virus has completed its relevant attachment events, it still faces the physical problem of crossing a membrane and making its genome accessible. That is the starting question of the following chapter.

Reading experimental endpoints at the appropriate scale

A fluorescent signal associated with a cell can represent labeled material rather than its biological activity. A host response can occur without productive infection, and a viral structural protein can be present as part of an incoming particle. The biological meaning of a marker must therefore be identified before drawing conclusions from its presence or absence. The earliest measured event, the next measured event and the final biological outcome should not be assigned the same name.

In a hypothetical figure with surface association, intracellular signal and a later outcome, differences restricted to early panels can propagate to later panels simply because the events are dependent. Differences appearing only in later panels cannot automatically be explained by a failure of attachment. This is a principle of causal interpretation, applicable to any sequential biological process. The data may establish the approximate stage of a restriction without identifying its precise molecular cause.

A negative observation can reflect the absence of an event or the limits of the measurement. If a signal is reported as undetectable, the statement is relative to the assay's ability to detect it. If a surface stain is absent, the relevant epitope may be inaccessible rather than the molecule entirely absent. Careful examination answers distinguish these possibilities rather than replacing an operational observation with an absolute biological claim.

Normalization determines the question answered by a measurement. A total signal from one hundred cells is not directly comparable with a total signal from two hundred cells when the objective is to compare average cellular signal. A conditional proportion among cells showing a particular event is likewise different from a proportion among all cells. For example, fifteen events among sixty selected observations represent one quarter of that selected group but fifteen percent of an original group of one hundred. Both fractions can be correct.

Correlation between receptor expression and an infection-associated marker does not establish that the receptor is the sole cause. Differentiation can change several cellular properties together, including protein distribution and metabolic activity. Results at the molecular, cellular, tissue and organism levels therefore require different supporting evidence. A strong answer identifies exactly the level at which a claim is justified and avoids transferring that conclusion to another level without additional evidence.

Worked examples: equilibrium and evidence

Example 1 — Binding fraction of an inert model ligand

A one-site equilibrium has Kd = 4 nM and free ligand concentration 12 nM. Fraction occupied = 12/(12+4) = 0.75, or 75%. This calculation concerns equilibrium occupancy of an abstract noninfectious ligand, not an infection probability.

Example 2 — Capacity versus fraction

At the same fractional occupancy of 0.5, sample A has 80 arbitrary site units and sample B has 160. Occupied site units are 40 and 80, respectively. The twofold signal difference need not imply a different affinity.

Example 3 — Conditional biological outcomes

Of 100 hypothetical observations, 60 show surface association and 15 of those show a later marker. The conditional fraction among associated observations is 15/60 = 25%; the fraction among all observations is 15/100 = 15%. The denominators describe different questions. Neither fraction alone demonstrates infectious progeny.

Original examination practice

Part B — Conceptual questions

1. Which structure performs surface recognition in a non-enveloped virus?
  1. Capsid protein
  2. Lipid envelope
  3. Host ribosome
  4. Viral genome alone

Answer: A. An exposed capsid protein can contact cellular molecules. A non-enveloped virion lacks a viral lipid bilayer.

2. Which is the primary receptor in the standard HIV-1 recognition model?
  1. gp41
  2. CD4
  3. HA
  4. NA

Answer: B. CD4 is cellular. gp41 belongs to HIV; HA and NA are influenza proteins.

3. Which distinction is most accurate?
  1. Attachment always yields progeny
  2. All receptors are proteins
  3. Productive infection yields infectious progeny
  4. Every bound particle has entered

Answer: C. Attachment and entry are earlier outcomes. Productive infection specifically includes new infectious particles.

4. Avidity most directly describes what?
  1. Genome length
  2. Number of host species
  3. Cellular ATP concentration
  4. Combined stability of multiple contacts

Answer: D. Avidity concerns multivalent association, not the strength of a single isolated contact alone.

5. Which statement about HA is appropriate?
  1. It recognizes suitable sialylated glycans in conventional influenza A
  2. It is a human chemokine receptor
  3. It is the same enzyme as NA
  4. It is an HIV capsid protein

Answer: A. HA mediates glycan recognition. NA cleaves sialic acid, and HIV uses different proteins.

6. Which name identifies an HIV coreceptor?
  1. gp120
  2. CCR5
  3. Hemagglutinin
  4. Sialic acid

Answer: B. CCR5 is a cellular chemokine receptor exploited as a coreceptor by appropriate HIV variants.

7. The term virion refers to:
  1. A complete extracellular viral particle
  2. A host-cell nucleus
  3. A ribosome carrying viral mRNA
  4. A soluble host receptor

Answer: A. A virion is the particulate infectious form of a virus. Viral nucleic acid or a host receptor alone is not a complete virion.

8. Which is a host component in the conventional influenza A recognition example?
  1. HA
  2. NA
  3. Sialylated glycan
  4. Viral envelope matrix protein

Answer: C. Sialylated glycans are presented by host surfaces. HA and NA are virus-encoded glycoproteins.

9. Which viral component participates in the downstream fusion function of the HIV envelope complex?
  1. CD4
  2. gp41
  3. CCR5
  4. CXCR4

Answer: B. gp41 is part of the viral envelope complex. CD4, CCR5 and CXCR4 are cellular proteins.

10. A coreceptor is best defined as:
  1. Any antibody that binds a virion
  2. The lipid envelope of a virus
  3. An intracellular ribosome
  4. An additional cellular recognition component used with a primary receptor

Answer: D. Coreceptor is a functional role in a particular recognition or entry mechanism, not a general name for a viral protein or immune antibody.

11. Which normal property of CCR5 and CXCR4 is relevant to their classification?
  1. Seven-transmembrane chemokine receptor architecture
  2. Catalytic cleavage of sialic acid
  3. Protection of a viral genome
  4. Viral genome replication

Answer: A. These cellular chemokine receptors are members of a seven-transmembrane receptor family. Their use by HIV is an additional biological context.

12. Which description of glycosidic linkage is correct?
  1. It specifies a viral genome segment
  2. It determines the number of chromosomes
  3. It describes the chemical connection between sugar residues
  4. It is the name of a cell-surface lipid bilayer

Answer: C. A linkage identifies how residues are connected. Alpha-2,3 and alpha-2,6 describe distinct glycan arrangements.

13. Which quantity measures the fraction of equivalent binding sites occupied at equilibrium?
  1. Total tissue mass
  2. Occupancy
  3. Virion diameter
  4. Number of viral genome segments

Answer: B. Occupancy is a proportion of available sites that are occupied, not the physical dimensions or genomic properties of a particle.

14. A finite-site binding system reaches a plateau principally because:
  1. All cell types become identical
  2. The genome must have entered a nucleus
  3. The host becomes a different species
  4. The finite available sites approach full occupancy

Answer: D. Saturation follows from a limited number of available sites. It does not establish any downstream infection event.

15. Which description best fits the glycocalyx?
  1. A carbohydrate-rich layer associated with the cell surface
  2. The lipid core of a virion
  3. The inner mitochondrial membrane
  4. The protein core of a ribosome

Answer: A. The glycocalyx includes extracellular carbohydrate-bearing structures. These can participate in molecular recognition.

16. Which observation is associated with Hirst's historical influenza work?
  1. Discovery of CD4
  2. Identification of MHC restriction
  3. Red-cell agglutination
  4. Discovery of the DNA double helix

Answer: C. Influenza-associated erythrocyte agglutination was an early physical indicator of surface interaction. CD4 discovery was a separate later development.

17. What does permissiveness describe in the classical terminology?
  1. Ability to adhere to an extracellular matrix only
  2. Intracellular compatibility for the relevant viral multiplication cycle
  3. The number of species exposed to a pathogen
  4. The abundance of circulating antibodies

Answer: B. Permissiveness refers to the intracellular conditions for multiplication; susceptibility concerns the ability to undergo entry in the relevant exposure setting.

18. Which molecule is known as the cellular poliovirus receptor CD155?
  1. A viral glycan
  2. A ribosomal RNA
  3. A viral enzyme
  4. An immunoglobulin-superfamily cellular protein

Answer: D. CD155 is a host protein with immunoglobulin-like domains, not a secreted antibody or viral structural component.

19. Which statement about epithelial cells is correct?
  1. Apical and basolateral surfaces can display different molecular compositions
  2. All membrane proteins are always equally exposed
  3. All epithelia lack carbohydrates
  4. Surface localization can be inferred perfectly from total RNA

Answer: A. Polarized membrane sorting permits different compositions on opposite surfaces. Transcript abundance cannot establish accessibility.

20. Which phrase best describes tissue tropism?
  1. The total number of virions per litre
  2. The chemical type of the viral genome
  3. Preferential involvement of particular tissues
  4. The mass of an individual receptor

Answer: C. Tissue tropism describes a biological distribution within an organism, rather than a molecular mass or genome classification.

Part C — Analytical questions

1. Two hypothetical cell samples show equal surface association. Only one shows an intracellular marker. What is justified?
  1. Both show productive infection
  2. The primary receptor is absent in both
  3. The samples differ at or after the measured association stage
  4. The first marker proves membrane fusion

Answer: C. Equal association does not show equal passage to the next stage. The exact molecular cause is not established.

2. An abstract ligand has Kd = 5 nM and free ligand 5 nM. Under the one-site model, occupancy is:
  1. 20%
  2. 100%
  3. 75%
  4. 50%

Answer: D. 5/(5+5) = 0.5. Equal concentrations of free ligand and Kd give half occupancy in this model.

3. A tissue has high receptor mRNA but little surface protein signal. Which conclusion is best supported?
  1. Transcript abundance is not a direct measure of accessible surface protein
  2. The tissue must be productively infected
  3. All transcripts are translated immediately
  4. The viral genome is absent from all cells

Answer: A. Protein maturation and localization intervene between transcript and surface accessibility. The observations do not establish infection.

4. A model particle remains associated with a surface but never yields a downstream marker. Which claim is unwarranted?
  1. Surface association occurred
  2. Productive infection necessarily occurred
  3. A downstream limitation is possible
  4. The endpoint used matters

Answer: B. Association cannot establish productive infection. Additional stage-specific evidence is needed.

5. Two samples have the same fractional occupancy but different maximum binding capacities. What can explain this?
  1. Different viral genome lengths must be responsible
  2. The samples are necessarily equally abundant in receptor
  3. Different amounts of accessible sites
  4. One sample has no sites

Answer: C. Capacity measures the available total number of sites. Fractional occupancy describes the proportion occupied.

6. A binding observation correlates with a later biological marker. Which interpretation is most rigorous?
  1. Correlation proves a sole receptor
  2. Every cellular feature is identical
  3. The marker proves the exact binding structure
  4. The association supports a hypothesis but does not establish sole causation

Answer: D. Multiple cellular properties can vary together. The evidence should not be made stronger than its design allows.

7. A hypothetical binding graph shows saturation at 90 arbitrary units. A second sample saturates at 180 units but has the same normalized binding curve. Which interpretation is best?
  1. The second sample must have a different genome
  2. The two samples differ in total capacity, not necessarily affinity
  3. The second sample necessarily produces progeny
  4. The first sample has no binding sites

Answer: B. The normalized curve describes fractional occupancy. A higher maximum can reflect more accessible sites without a changed single-site equilibrium constant.

8. An inert model ligand has a bound fraction of 0.8. Of 50 equivalent sites, how many are occupied?
  1. 10
  2. 25
  3. 50
  4. 40

Answer: D. Multiply the fraction by the site count: 0.8 × 50 = 40. The remaining 10 sites are unoccupied; this is not an infection count.

9. A report records equal receptor transcript abundance in two cell types but different surface staining. Which statement best fits?
  1. The two measures concern different stages of protein expression and localization
  2. The transcript measurement is necessarily fraudulent
  3. The receptor cannot be a protein
  4. The cell types must belong to different species

Answer: A. RNA abundance and surface-localized protein are distinct biological measurements. Neither alone establishes infection.

10. A cell-associated marker is detected immediately after contact, but the marker is known to be present in incoming particles. What is the most careful interpretation?
  1. New viral gene expression is demonstrated
  2. The full replication cycle is complete
  3. Particle-associated material is present; new synthesis has not been established
  4. The cell has necessarily undergone lysis

Answer: C. A marker carried by incoming material cannot by itself distinguish association from newly synthesized products.

11. An abstract reversible binding model has Kd = 2 nM. Free ligand is 6 nM. What is the occupied fraction?
  1. 0.25
  2. 0.75
  3. 0.50
  4. 1.00

Answer: B. 6/(6+2) = 0.75. The formula assumes equilibrium and a single class of independent sites.

12. A hypothetical dataset shows 40 associated observations among 200 total. Ten of those 40 show a later marker. What is the overall frequency of the later marker?
  1. 25%
  2. 20%
  3. 10%
  4. 5%

Answer: D. The question asks for the overall fraction, so divide 10 by 200. The conditional frequency among associated observations would be 25%.

13. A binding curve appears hyperbolic on a linear concentration axis and sigmoidal on a logarithmic axis. Which statement is warranted?
  1. The change in appearance can be caused by the axis transformation
  2. The molecule necessarily changed its structure between plots
  3. Cooperativity has been conclusively demonstrated
  4. The number of cells doubled

Answer: A. Changing the coordinate scale can change the curve's appearance without changing the underlying model. Cooperativity requires independent support.

14. A study detects a receptor in total tissue extract but does not identify which cells express it. Which conclusion is justified?
  1. Every cell in the tissue displays the receptor
  2. All cells are equally permissive
  3. The tissue contains detectable receptor material, but cell-level distribution remains unresolved
  4. The receptor is necessarily absent from the cell surface

Answer: C. A tissue homogenate loses spatial and cell-type information. It cannot establish the distribution of accessible receptor.

15. A schematic identifies gp120 and gp41 on a particle and CD4 on a cell. Which relationship is correct in the standard model?
  1. gp41 is the cellular receptor
  2. gp120 is a viral recognition component and CD4 is cellular
  3. CD4 is a viral fusion protein
  4. Both gp120 and CD4 are lipid molecules

Answer: B. The labels distinguish the two interacting partners. gp41 is a viral component involved in the downstream fusion transition.

16. Two data series show total signals of 200 and 300 units from 100 and 150 cells, respectively. What is the mean signal per cell?
  1. 2 and 3 units
  2. 1 and 2 units
  3. 3 and 2 units
  4. 2 and 2 units

Answer: D. 200/100 = 2 and 300/150 = 2. Total signal differs because the sample sizes differ.

17. A figure shows high surface association in a cell population but no evidence of new infectious progeny. Which conclusion is defensible?
  1. Association occurred, but productive infection has not been established
  2. The cells must have completed a full viral cycle
  3. The primary receptor necessarily failed to bind
  4. The data prove a particular membrane-fusion mechanism

Answer: A. Only the observed early association is supported. The exact location of a later restriction is not demonstrated by these two observations alone.

18. Two isolated inert ligands have the same Kd but different rates of reaching equilibrium. Which explanation is compatible with the simple model?
  1. Equilibrium and kinetics are identical concepts
  2. One ligand must be a virion
  3. Different association and dissociation rates can give the same equilibrium ratio
  4. The equilibrium equation is necessarily invalid

Answer: C. The ratio of the rate constants determines Kd in the elementary model; the individual rates determine time-dependent behavior.

19. An antibody stain shows no detectable surface signal. Total protein analysis detects the same protein. Which inference is most defensible?
  1. The total protein result proves complete surface exposure
  2. The surface assay and total-protein assay measure different properties
  3. Both measurements necessarily detect viral replication
  4. The gene must be deleted

Answer: B. Intracellular protein or inaccessible epitopes can contribute to a discrepancy. The surface staining result has a defined detection context.

20. In a report, an inert ligand binds an isolated cellular molecule but no cellular entry endpoint was measured. Which claim exceeds the data?
  1. The molecules associate under the conditions studied
  2. A physical interaction may be relevant
  3. More evidence is needed for a functional role
  4. The molecule is sufficient to mediate complete viral infection

Answer: D. The binding measurement supports association only. Functional entry and productive infection require separate evidence.

Additional advanced practice

1. Assertion: Binding of a virion does not by itself establish productive infection. Reason: Intracellular multiplication and production of infectious progeny are later requirements.
  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true

Answer: A. The later requirements are precisely why binding is insufficient evidence.

2. Assertion: Every viral recognition structure must be a transmembrane protein. Reason: Sialylated glycans can participate in viral recognition.
  1. Both true and related
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Assertion true, reason false

Answer: C. The carbohydrate example disproves the universal protein-only claim.

3. Assertion: Two cells may have the same receptor transcript abundance but different surface protein abundance. Reason: Protein trafficking and processing intervene after transcription.
  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Both true and the reason explains the assertion
  3. Both true but unrelated
  4. Both false

Answer: B. The cellular steps after transcription can explain the observed divergence.

  2. 1d 2a 3b 4c
  3. 1c 2d 3a 4b
  4. 1b 2c 3d 4a

Answer: D. HA and gp120 are viral recognition proteins in different systems, while CD4 and CD155 are cellular receptors.

5. Which two statements are correct? I. Glycan recognition can depend on linkage. II. All influenza A viruses have identical receptor use. III. Attachment is distinct from productive infection. IV. Coreceptors are required by every virus.
  1. I and III
  2. I and IV
  3. II and III
  4. II and IV

Answer: A. I and III are valid. II and IV make unsupported universal claims.

6. Which statements about binding models are correct? I. Kd has concentration units. II. Fractional occupancy is dimensionless. III. A plateau proves new infectious particles. IV. Equilibrium means no molecular exchange.
  1. I and III
  2. II and IV
  3. I and II
  4. III and IV

Answer: C. Kd carries concentration units in this simple formulation; occupancy is a ratio. Equilibrium is dynamic and saturation is not an infection measurement.

7. A drawing shows a particle associated with a cell exterior; its genome remains enclosed in the particle. Which stage is depicted?
  1. Productive infection
  2. Attachment
  3. Genome replication
  4. Release of progeny

Answer: B. The genome is still within the extracellular particle, so the depicted stage is association rather than genome delivery.

8. Match 1. host range; 2. tissue tropism; 3. permissiveness; 4. avidity. a. intracellular compatibility; b. combined stability of multiple contacts; c. spectrum of host organisms; d. preferential tissue involvement.
  1. 1a 2c 3d 4b
  2. 1b 2a 3c 4d
  3. 1d 2b 3a 4c
  4. 1c 2d 3a 4b

Answer: D. Each term addresses a different biological or biophysical property.

9. An inert model ligand occupies 30% of 90 equivalent sites. How many remain unoccupied?
  1. 63
  2. 27
  3. 30
  4. 90

Answer: A. Occupied = 0.30 × 90 = 27; unoccupied = 90 − 27 = 63.

10. Which is an example of a correctly limited conclusion from red-cell agglutination?
  1. All erythrocytes are productively infected
  2. The viral genome has integrated
  3. A multivalent physical association has occurred
  4. The complete molecular structure is determined

Answer: C. Agglutination is a collective physical outcome and does not establish intracellular replication or full structural identity.

11. Which observation concerns the earliest stage among the four choices?
  1. Detection of newly formed progeny
  2. Surface association
  3. Genome expression
  4. Cell injury following replication

Answer: B. Surface association precedes the intracellular outcomes listed in the conventional sequence.

12. Which conclusion best integrates recognition and disease?
  1. A receptor alone determines all symptoms
  2. Any bound particle necessarily causes disease
  3. Tissue injury is identical to receptor abundance
  4. Recognition contributes to cell targeting, while disease depends on additional biological events

Answer: D. The cellular recognition stage contributes to the distribution of infection but does not establish the later physiological consequences.

Revision and active recall

Recognition depends on complementary accessible surfaces. The presence of a receptor can explain a defined early restriction without establishing complete intracellular compatibility. Glycans and proteins may both participate in recognition. Influenza HA and HIV gp120 illustrate different molecular arrangements; neither example establishes a universal mechanism for all viruses.

  1. Define a virion and distinguish collision from attachment.
  2. Explain why capsid proteins can recognize cellular molecules.
  3. Distinguish an attachment factor from a functional entry receptor.
  4. Explain what conditional necessity means.
  5. Define affinity and avidity.
  6. Derive the simple one-site occupancy relationship.
  7. Explain the significance of glycosidic linkage in the influenza example.
  8. Distinguish HA from NA.
  9. Identify the viral and cellular components of HIV recognition.
  10. Explain why CD4 alone cannot account for all HIV recognition.
  11. Distinguish susceptibility, permissiveness and productive infection.
  12. Explain why total receptor transcript abundance is insufficient to infer tropism.
