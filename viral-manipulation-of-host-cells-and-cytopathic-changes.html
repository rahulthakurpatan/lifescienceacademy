1. From a functioning cell to an infected cell

A cell is not simply a bag containing the substances needed to make a virus. It is a continuously operating system in which energy supply, protein synthesis, membrane traffic, gene regulation and degradation of damaged components are coordinated. A healthy epithelial cell, for example, maintains a physical barrier while transporting selected molecules and communicating with neighboring cells. These activities require thousands of proteins whose concentrations and locations are regulated. Infection introduces an additional source of genetic information into this coordinated system. The information can redirect existing activities and establish new molecular relationships without building an entirely new cell. Understanding the consequences of this redirection is the central problem of viral host-cell biology.

The extracellular infectious viral particle is called a virion. It carries a genome protected by protein, and in some viruses by a lipid envelope as well. The particle has no autonomous ribosomal machinery and cannot continuously synthesize proteins or regenerate energy independently. Inside a suitable living cell, however, its genetic information becomes accessible. Viral gene products may recruit cellular proteins, alter their activity, bind cellular membranes or change which messenger RNAs are used. The host supplies a biochemical environment within which these changes are possible. In contrast to a bacterium that contains its own ribosomes and many of its metabolic enzymes, a virus necessarily depends on the host for translation. This dependence explains why the alteration of cellular behavior is closely connected with viral reproduction rather than an incidental accompaniment of infection.

Attachment and internalization were the subjects of the preceding chapter. A particle can bind to a cell without establishing infection, and a cell can internalize a particle without releasing a functional genome. Once genome access has been established, the question changes from how a physical barrier is crossed to how intracellular processes are altered. These stages should not be collapsed into a single event. The presence of viral antigen inside a cell could reflect incoming material rather than new synthesis; detection of newly made viral RNA supports a later stage but does not by itself demonstrate production of infectious particles. This distinction becomes important whenever an examination asks which step of infection is affected by an experimental perturbation.

A cell that allows a virus to enter is described as susceptible in the relevant context. A permissive cell supports the intracellular events necessary for productive infection. Susceptibility and permissiveness can coexist but need not do so. A cell might display the appropriate surface receptor while lacking an essential intracellular function or maintaining a restrictive physiological state. In an abortive infection, some viral activities begin but infectious progeny are not produced. Nevertheless, abortive infection may perturb the host, for example through expression of an early viral protein or activation of a cellular stress response. Therefore failure to recover infectious progeny does not prove that the host remained unchanged.

Productive infection means that the sequence of intracellular events culminates in production of infectious progeny. It does not specify the fate of the host cell. Some cells are destroyed during or after productive infection, whereas others continue releasing progeny for appreciable periods. The term cytopathic effect, often abbreviated CPE, refers to recognizable structural or functional injury associated with infection; in cell culture it is especially used for observable changes in cell morphology. A cell can produce particles before developing an obvious morphological abnormality. Conversely a cell can die without completing production. These are different axes of observation: viral reproductive outcome, cellular function and visible morphology.

A useful causal sequence is genome accessibility, viral gene expression, interaction between viral and cellular components, altered cell physiology and, in suitable conditions, production and release of progeny. This sequence is not a rigid clock. Some changes originate from events at entry, others develop during genome synthesis, and still others occur as a consequence of host stress. Different host cells can respond differently to the same virus because their basal physiology, differentiation state, regulatory machinery and defense capacities differ. A complete explanation identifies the relevant stage, the altered cellular process, its consequence for the cell and the evidence linking the change to infection.

2. How cellular alterations became scientific evidence

Early studies of viruses were constrained by the inability to propagate many of them in ordinary nutrient media. Unlike many bacteria, viruses required suitable living material. Cultured cells eventually provided a setting in which investigators could observe the appearance of infected cells and compare them with uninfected cells maintained in the same environment. This was an important conceptual change: cellular injury could now be separated experimentally from many of the complex interactions that occur in an entire infected organism. Although culture systems do not reproduce every aspect of tissue physiology, they made it possible to ask directly whether infectious material could alter the structure or function of a particular cell type.

The work of John Enders, Thomas Weller and Frederick Robbins on poliovirus propagation in tissue culture was a landmark in this development. Their demonstration that poliovirus could be propagated using cultured human tissues broadened the available experimental systems. The achievement was not simply an improved way to detect virus; it established the usefulness of living-cell systems for studying viral growth outside a whole organism. Visible cellular injury became one important observation in such systems, but the ability to demonstrate propagation was the decisive feature. An abnormal-looking cell alone is insufficient evidence that a particular virus has multiplied.

The development of animal-virus plaque assays provided another important connection between local cellular damage and infectious propagation. A plaque is a localized area of altered or destroyed cells in a layer of susceptible cells under appropriate assay conditions. Its appearance is the cumulative consequence of infection and subsequent local spread. A count of plaques measures infectious units capable of initiating detectable foci under those conditions; it is not a direct enumeration of all physical particles. The distinction explains why molecular measurements of genome abundance can disagree with a biological measure of infectivity. A sample may contain genomes or particles that are unable to complete the events necessary to produce detectable infection.

Microscopy established that different infections produce different patterns of cellular alteration. Electron microscopy added information about changes in internal membranes, the accumulation of particles and the location of cellular structures. However, structural information must be interpreted in relation to time and biochemical function. A cluster of membranes may be associated with replication, assembly or cellular degradation. Its shape does not identify which of these processes occurs there. Localization of appropriate molecular markers and evidence that a process occurs at that site strengthen the interpretation. The same caution applies to conspicuous inclusions within cells: some are related to viral factories or accumulated viral products, while others include altered host components.

Studies of cellular protein synthesis added a different type of evidence. Investigators could distinguish a general decrease in protein production from a redistribution of synthesis toward viral proteins. Such results established that host shutoff need not mean that every ribosome has stopped functioning. The cellular machinery can continue to operate while becoming less available to ordinary cellular transcripts. Later work on initiation factors, RNA turnover and viral messenger RNA features helped explain why host and viral products could be affected unequally. This is a good example of how a physiological observation becomes a mechanistic question: first identify what changes, then identify the molecular interaction responsible.

No single experimental observation describes the entire infected cell. A microscope image reveals structure, a biochemical measurement reveals a selected molecular activity, and an infectivity measurement reveals the ability of material to initiate further productive infection under specified conditions. Their combination is more informative than any individual result. A comparison must also include appropriate untreated and uninfected controls, because changes in nutrition, culture density, chemical exposure and sample handling may alter cells independently of infection. Scientific reasoning therefore proceeds from converging observations rather than from naming a characteristic appearance and assuming its mechanism.

3. Redirection of host gene expression and protein synthesis

Gene expression involves several distinct stages. A cellular gene may be transcribed into RNA, the RNA may be processed and transported, and a mature messenger RNA may be translated on ribosomes. The resulting protein can be folded, modified, transported and eventually degraded. Changing any one of these stages can alter the quantity of functional protein in the cell. Consequently a reduction in the abundance of a host protein cannot automatically be attributed to inhibition of transcription. The messenger RNA may have become unstable, translation initiation may be impaired, or protein turnover may have increased. A mechanistic explanation requires a measurement that distinguishes these possibilities.

Most cellular messenger RNAs use a modified guanosine cap near the five-prime end to help recruit the eukaryotic translation initiation machinery. The cap is recognized by eIF4E, which participates in the eIF4F complex together with the scaffold eIF4G and the RNA helicase eIF4A. Through further interactions, an initiation complex containing the small ribosomal subunit is recruited and proceeds toward an appropriate initiation codon. The large ribosomal subunit subsequently joins. Elongation and termination occur after initiation. The important point here is that cap recognition and recruitment are regulatory steps upstream of peptide bond formation. A change in these steps can restrict translation without directly destroying the catalytic function of the ribosome.

Poliovirus supplies a textbook example of unequal effects on cellular and viral messenger RNA translation. A viral protease cleaves the cellular scaffold protein eIF4G, disrupting important interactions used for ordinary cap-dependent initiation. Poliovirus RNA contains an internal ribosome entry site, or IRES, through which translation initiation can occur without conventional cap recognition. The IRES is a functional RNA region that recruits components of the translation machinery through a different set of interactions. The viral RNA still requires host ribosomes and relevant initiation factors. The result is not independence from the cell, but a different dependency on its components.

It is tempting to imagine a viral protease cutting every initiation factor so that only viral proteins can be synthesized. This picture would be misleading. Translation of viral RNA remains a cellularly supported process, and the extent and timing of host shutoff depend on the virus and the physiological condition of the infected cell. Some cellular RNAs can continue to be translated under circumstances that suppress many other messages. Furthermore, additional changes in RNA metabolism and signaling can contribute to the net suppression of host protein synthesis. The cap-dependent versus internal-initiation comparison is a useful model of differential access to the machinery, not a claim that one molecular event explains every effect of every picornavirus.

Another mechanism acts at the level of messenger RNA abundance. Herpes simplex virus expresses a virion host shutoff protein, commonly called vhs, which promotes degradation of messenger RNA. Reduced RNA stability can decrease the amount of template available to translating ribosomes. A fall in cellular protein synthesis can therefore occur even when the host is still able to initiate transcription and its ribosomes remain intact. Because transcript degradation also affects the balance of viral messages in infection, it should not be described as a perfectly selective mechanism that recognizes every host message and spares every viral one. The molecular target and timing differ from proteolytic disruption of translation initiation.

Influenza virus provides a further illustration of distinct mechanisms operating at different stages of RNA expression. Its messenger RNAs obtain short capped leaders derived from host transcripts during viral transcription, a process known as cap snatching. This uses capped cellular RNA material to support viral gene expression. Separately, viral proteins including PA-X can contribute to host RNA shutoff. Cap acquisition and host RNA degradation should not be treated as identical events: one concerns the initiation of viral messenger RNA production, while the other changes the abundance of cellular RNA. Neither gives influenza an independent ribosome. These examples are useful because similar reductions in cellular protein synthesis arise through different molecular routes.

A cell may also reduce its own translation in response to infection. One well-known regulatory route involves phosphorylation of the initiation factor eIF2 alpha, which reduces the efficiency with which the machinery delivers the initiator transfer RNA to the small ribosomal subunit. Stress-responsive kinases can promote this phosphorylation. A consequence is decreased initiation on many messenger RNAs, although selected stress-associated transcripts can respond differently. This cellular response can reduce the availability of translation machinery to the virus, but it also changes normal cellular protein production. A decrease in translation can thus represent a direct viral effect, a host response or a combination of both. Observing the decrease does not establish which participant initiated it.

A reduction in ongoing synthesis does not instantaneously erase pre-existing proteins. Suppose a membrane transporter has a long cellular lifetime. Even if new synthesis is suppressed, its existing molecules may remain active for some time. In contrast, a rapidly degraded regulatory protein may decline soon after synthesis is reduced. This difference follows from the balance between production and removal. If the protein concentration is P, its synthesis rate is s and its first-order removal constant is k, the simplest model is dP/dt = s − kP. The model assumes a homogeneous pool and constant rates during the interval of interest. At steady state the production rate equals the removal rate, so P = s/k. After complete shutoff of synthesis, the concentration declines exponentially, with a half-life equal to ln(2)/k. Real proteins can depart from this model when trafficking, dilution or regulated degradation changes, but the model explains why loss of synthesis and loss of physiological function need not occur simultaneously.

Suppression of host gene expression can affect signaling receptors, cytoskeletal regulators, secreted factors and components of defense responses. A cell that previously maintained a stable membrane or secretory output may become unable to replenish proteins removed by normal turnover. Nevertheless, translation is only one determinant of cellular injury. A protein can remain abundant but become inactive if it is mislocalized or chemically modified. A change in RNA abundance can also occur without an immediate measurable change in protein abundance. Students should therefore separate transcriptional abundance, translational activity, protein amount and functional output. These are related measurements, not interchangeable ones.

A simplified experiment may compare a cellular cap-dependent reporter with a reporter whose initiation follows an alternative route. Reporter output is interpreted as a proxy for gene expression only if the relevant controls are considered. If the cap-dependent signal decreases while the alternative signal remains relatively stable, selective impairment of conventional initiation is consistent with the observation. However, unequal messenger RNA stability, reporter protein degradation and differences in baseline expression can also influence the signals. Measuring RNA amounts and verifying comparable cellular viability help narrow the interpretation. The experiment illustrates how an investigator distinguishes competing explanations; the numbers in later worked problems are hypothetical and are not reported measurements from historical studies.

4. Membranes, organelles and cellular architecture

The membranes within a eukaryotic cell separate chemical environments and organize biochemical reactions in space. The endoplasmic reticulum, or ER, provides extensive surfaces for synthesis of membrane lipids and the production and folding of many secreted and membrane proteins. The Golgi apparatus modifies and sorts materials moving through the secretory pathway. Endosomes participate in uptake and sorting, and lysosomes degrade a wide variety of cellular materials. Vesicles carry cargo between compartments through regulated budding, movement and fusion. These systems continually consume energy and depend on accurate recognition of destination membranes. They are especially important to viral biology because a virus must accomplish its intracellular activities within a pre-existing architecture.

Viral infection can change this architecture without simply destroying every organelle. An altered membrane may acquire new proteins, a different shape or a new function. A normal vesicle may fail to reach its destination because traffic has been redirected. A surface that usually contributes to secretion may instead be associated with production of viral components. These changes can be selective and temporally regulated. A cell may therefore lose its ability to carry out some specialized tasks while remaining capable of synthesizing other molecules. To understand the alteration, ask first which ordinary cellular process the relevant structure performs, and then what happens when that function is redirected.

Many positive-sense RNA viruses associate genome synthesis with modified intracellular membranes. Such modified structures are often called replication organelles. They are not autonomous organelles inherited by virions; they arise through interactions of viral and host components in infected cells. A positive-sense viral RNA can act as messenger RNA once it becomes accessible, allowing synthesis of viral proteins that participate in subsequent stages. Some of these proteins associate with cellular membranes and alter the location of other components. Consequently genome replication may occur within or closely associated with membrane compartments whose shape and molecular composition differ from those of the original organelle. This contrasts with a model in which every relevant molecule simply diffuses freely throughout the entire cytoplasm.

A physical advantage of compartmentalization is that interacting molecules can be concentrated in a small region. If a fixed number of molecules is confined to a smaller accessible volume, the average concentration rises. Since the likelihood of a bimolecular encounter depends in part on local concentration, this can influence the rate at which associated molecules meet. However, concentration alone does not prove enhanced catalytic activity. Substrates and products must be able to reach or leave the relevant environment; excessive confinement could also restrict access. Membrane association can influence the orientation of molecules and the local lipid environment, both of which may matter to the organization of protein complexes. These principles explain why the geometry of a cellular compartment can be biologically significant rather than a mere visual curiosity.

Membranes are deformable because their phospholipids are arranged in fluid bilayers. Bending a bilayer away from its preferred geometry has an energetic cost that depends on composition and curvature. Proteins that bind to one surface or insert into a leaflet can favor particular shapes, as can alterations in lipid distribution. Formation of an unusual curved membrane therefore requires an explanation in terms of the cellular or viral components that influence its physical organization. The membrane does not become curved because the cell recognizes the purpose of a virus. Rather, binding interactions, protein accumulation and membrane dynamics change the energetic balance between possible shapes.

Dengue virus is a representative example of a virus whose intracellular replication is associated with rearranged ER membranes. In conceptual diagrams, the modified ER can be drawn as a membrane system with local invaginations that organize viral components. Such a picture should distinguish the cytoplasm from the ER lumen; an inward membrane curvature need not imply that all relevant molecules have crossed an intact membrane into a sealed lumen. Connections to the cytoplasm can remain important for the exchange of material. The geometry is different from a conventional secretory vesicle transporting cellular cargo to the Golgi. The appropriate conclusion is that membrane organization changes in ways associated with viral processes, not that the ER has been replaced by an entirely foreign organelle.

Other positive-sense RNA viruses show different membrane arrangements. Enterovirus infection can produce conspicuous altered cytoplasmic membrane systems; hepatitis C virus is associated with a characteristic modified membrane environment. Similarities in dependence on cellular lipids do not establish that every virus produces the same type of compartment. Even during a single infection, the appearance of modified membranes can change as infection progresses. A static micrograph therefore provides a snapshot of one state. Identifying a membrane structure by shape alone is less reliable than combining its shape with information about cellular origin, associated molecules and the time at which it appears.

Not all visible viral factories are bounded by a lipid bilayer. Some viruses organize viral proteins and nucleic acids into distinct cytoplasmic regions that lack a conventional surrounding membrane. Rabies virus-associated Negri bodies are an example of cytoplasmic viral inclusions associated with viral RNA synthesis. A compartment in this broader sense is a spatially organized region with a distinctive composition; it need not be a membrane-enclosed organelle. This distinction prevents a common overgeneralization from positive-sense RNA virus examples to all viral infections. A concentration of viral proteins could also be a site of storage or assembly rather than an active site of genome synthesis. Function must be established independently of appearance.

Viral proteins entering the secretory pathway pose an additional problem for host cells. Many viral envelope glycoproteins are synthesized at the ER and then folded and modified as they move through cellular membrane systems. Their correct orientation in a membrane and their distribution within the cell can be relevant to the eventual structure of a viral envelope or to interactions with other cells. The ER provides an environment that differs from the cytosol in its folding machinery and chemical properties. A defect in the trafficking of a membrane protein may alter its distribution without necessarily changing the total amount synthesized. A whole-cell measurement of that protein could therefore appear normal even when its surface-dependent function has been lost.

Accumulation of proteins within the ER may exceed its capacity to fold and process them, producing ER stress. Cells respond to this stress through signaling systems collectively called the unfolded protein response. Its general purposes are to adjust the load entering the ER, increase folding capacity and deal with misfolded material. One branch reduces much of new protein synthesis, whereas other branches can alter gene expression in ways that support recovery. If the disturbance is severe or prolonged, stress signaling can contribute to cell death. Infection-associated ER stress may consequently be both a consequence of altered cellular activity and a cause of additional physiological changes. It should not automatically be interpreted as a direct destruction of ER membranes.

Autophagy is a cellular system that helps deliver selected cytoplasmic material for degradation and recycling. Autophagic vesicles and viral membrane rearrangements can sometimes have superficially similar features, including double-membrane profiles. Similarity of appearance does not establish equivalence of origin or function. An increase in autophagy-associated structures may arise because more structures are formed, because their removal is impaired, or both. For that reason a single measurement of their number cannot demonstrate an increase in the total rate of autophagic degradation. Some viral infections alter autophagy-related processes, but the direction and consequence vary. The useful syllabus principle is that membrane traffic and cellular quality control are dynamic, regulated systems whose observed intermediate abundance depends on both formation and clearance.

The cytoskeleton also contributes to the organization of intracellular activity. Microtubules provide tracks for directed transport, while actin filaments support cell shape, membrane remodeling and aspects of short-range movement. Infection-related changes in either system can influence the position of organelles or the shape of the cell. A change in microtubule organization can alter the distribution of vesicles even if their membrane composition remains unchanged. Conversely an alteration in membrane-associated proteins can change the organization of actin through signaling. These mechanisms connect internal architecture to the visible cellular morphology discussed later. A rounded cell, for example, has lost much of its previously spread shape, but whether it has lost adhesion, changed cytoskeletal tension or undergone a death-associated contraction cannot be inferred from outline alone.

A normal organelle contributes to both a local process and whole-cell homeostasis. A change in secretory traffic may reduce the amount of a cellular protein delivered to the surface, even when its transcript and total cellular protein abundance are unchanged. A redistribution of intracellular membranes can affect the availability of particular membrane surfaces for ordinary cellular reactions. A change in mitochondrial function can affect energy supply and death signaling. These are distinct mechanisms with potentially overlapping consequences. Examination problems often exploit this distinction by providing one molecular measurement and a cellular phenotype. The strongest explanation is the one that accounts for both observations without adding unsupported steps.

5. Signaling and cell-cycle changes during infection

Cells continuously interpret signals from their surroundings and from their own internal condition. Receptors and intracellular signaling proteins help translate changes in nutrients, growth factors, cell contacts, DNA integrity and stress into changes in enzyme activity or gene expression. Viral proteins can intersect with these pathways, and cellular responses to viral material can also alter them. To describe such a change, it is useful to identify the initial stimulus, the signaling intermediate and the measurable downstream effect. Merely stating that a pathway is activated or inhibited does not explain the biological consequence.

Phosphorylation is a common regulatory mechanism in signaling. A kinase transfers a phosphate group to an appropriate target, while phosphatases remove phosphate groups. The modification can change protein activity, localization or binding interactions. Its consequence depends on the target and the specific residue; phosphorylation does not universally mean activation. An increase in the phosphorylated form of a regulatory protein can arise from increased kinase activity, decreased phosphatase activity or a change in the accessibility of its phosphorylation site. An infected-cell experiment that detects altered phosphorylation therefore needs further evidence before assigning a direct viral target. Measuring total protein alongside its modified form is especially useful because the total amount may also change during infection.

Cellular stress signaling can change rapidly in response to altered metabolism and macromolecular synthesis. This is particularly important in cells that cannot maintain normal protein folding or ion balance. A response that initially helps the cell tolerate stress may become damaging if it persists or is inappropriate to the tissue's physiological role. Infection may interrupt normal feedback control, for instance by changing the abundance of a signaling intermediate or by interfering with the production of new regulatory proteins. The temporal order of these changes matters. An early change in a kinase could be upstream of a decline in protein synthesis; the same change detected only after extensive cell damage might instead be a secondary response.

The cell cycle comprises the ordered events by which a proliferating cell duplicates its DNA and divides. Cells in G1 have not yet replicated their chromosomes in the current cycle. During S phase DNA content increases, and G2 precedes mitosis, in which duplicated chromosomes are distributed to daughter cells. Quiescent cells may remain outside active cycling in a state commonly termed G0. Progress through the cycle depends on the coordinated activity of cyclins, cyclin-dependent kinases and regulatory systems that respond to DNA integrity and other conditions. A checkpoint is not a physical barrier: it is a regulatory process that can delay progression when certain requirements are unmet.

Viral infection may interfere with these controls. Some viruses need cellular conditions that resemble those found during particular stages of the cycle, while others are able to carry out their intracellular activities in cells that are not dividing. A viral protein can modify cellular control of cycle progression even when the virus does not depend directly on cellular chromosomal DNA synthesis. For example, the HIV-1 accessory protein Vpr is associated with accumulation of cells at the G2 stage of the cell cycle. The example illustrates a viral alteration of an existing regulatory system, not the transformation of the infected cell into a permanently proliferating cancer cell. Cell-cycle arrest and uncontrolled cell proliferation are very different phenotypes.

A population accumulating in a particular phase does not necessarily mean that more cells are entering that phase than usual. Consider a sequence of stages through which cells normally move. The number found in a stage depends on both the rate of entry and the time spent there. If cells remain in G2 for longer, the fraction with duplicated DNA may increase even when the rate of entry has not increased. An observation of increased G2 DNA content is thus compatible with slowed exit. It may also be affected by selective loss of cells in other stages. A time-resolved analysis or an independent marker of progression helps distinguish these possibilities.

DNA-content measurements provide a useful introductory tool for reasoning about the cell cycle. A diploid G1 cell has a 2C DNA content, an S-phase cell has an intermediate amount, and a cell in G2 or before separation of chromosomes in mitosis generally has a 4C DNA content. The letter C describes the amount of DNA relative to a haploid genome, not the number of distinct chromosome sets. A G2 cell remains diploid despite its doubled DNA content because the replicated sister chromatids do not constitute an additional set of homologous chromosomes. A DNA-content histogram alone cannot distinguish G2 from mitosis, and a cell doublet can also produce an apparent high-DNA signal if the analysis does not adequately separate single cells.

A viral infection can alter the cell cycle indirectly by activating a cellular response to DNA damage or other stress. Therefore an arrest is not sufficient evidence that a viral molecule directly binds a particular cyclin or kinase. A comparison of infected and uninfected cells establishes an association, while additional molecular observations may identify a more direct mechanism. A change in checkpoint signaling may have different consequences in a dividing cell than in a terminally differentiated cell. For instance, a mature nondividing cell can suffer severe physiological dysfunction without an experimentally meaningful G2 accumulation. The stage-specific effects of infection are thus inseparable from the normal biological state of the host.

Stable cellular transformation is treated separately in the next node. Here the relevant distinction is between an infection-associated change in ongoing regulation and a lasting alteration of growth behavior. An acute arrest might reverse if the initiating disturbance disappears, or it might precede irreversible cell death. Persistent viral expression can produce a different pattern of altered regulation, and further evidence is needed to establish stable transformed behavior. A short-term observation of cell rounding, altered kinase activity or increased DNA content does not establish that the cell is transformed. This boundary is essential because the terms alteration and transformation have different meanings in the official syllabus.

6. Survival decisions, apoptosis and membrane damage

The cell's ability to remain alive during infection is closely related to its ability to maintain chemical gradients, regenerate energy, replace damaged macromolecules and regulate stress. Death may occur when these systems fail beyond recovery, but many forms of cell death are actively regulated. Apoptosis is a well-characterized form of regulated death in which proteases called caspases help dismantle cellular structures. It can be initiated through signals originating within the cell or through certain extracellular receptor pathways. The purpose of studying this mechanism here is to understand why death during viral infection is not simply a matter of physical damage by accumulating particles.

The intrinsic apoptotic pathway responds to several forms of internal stress. Members of the Bcl-2 protein family regulate whether the outer mitochondrial membrane becomes permeable to certain proteins. When the balance of signals favors permeabilization, cytochrome c can enter the cytosol and participate in assembly of an activation complex with Apaf-1. Initiator caspase activation then contributes to the activation of executioner caspases. These proteases cleave selected cellular substrates, producing coordinated changes in nuclear structure, cytoskeletal organization and other cellular processes. The mitochondrion in this context is not merely an energy-producing organelle; it participates directly in a regulatory transition associated with death.

The extrinsic pathway begins with signaling through particular cell-surface death receptors. Recruitment of intracellular adaptor proteins can lead to activation of an initiator caspase, commonly caspase-8 in canonical examples. Executioner caspases can subsequently be activated, and in some cell types signals from the receptor pathway also engage mitochondrial amplification. The two pathways therefore need not operate as completely independent routes. They describe different initiating conditions that can converge on parts of the same dismantling machinery. For examination purposes it is important to distinguish an initiating signal from an execution event: blocking an executioner can prevent some downstream changes even when the initial stress signal remains present.

Apoptotic morphology often includes cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation and membrane blebbing. Early in the process, the plasma membrane can retain its barrier function even though the internal organization is changing extensively. Phosphatidylserine, a phospholipid normally concentrated in the cytoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane, can become exposed on the outer surface. This change contributes to recognition by cells that remove dying material. If apoptotic cells are not cleared, they may eventually lose membrane integrity. Consequently a late loss of membrane integrity does not exclude an earlier apoptotic process. The sequence is especially relevant in cell culture, where removal of dying cells may differ greatly from that occurring in a tissue.

Lysis describes physical loss of the integrity of a cell, often with release of cellular contents. It is an outcome and does not, by itself, specify the molecular pathway leading to it. A lytic death can involve severe damage or regulated processes that culminate in membrane disruption. Conversely early apoptotic morphology does not imply immediate lysis. Membrane integrity and caspase activity therefore measure different features of a dying cell. A membrane-impermeant dye can report failure of the barrier, while a biochemical marker of caspase cleavage can provide evidence of an apoptotic process. Neither marker alone describes every possible death state.

The timing of apoptosis is particularly important during infection. An early death response can interrupt the activities of a cell before a productive viral cycle is completed. In that context death may help restrict infection even though the individual cell is lost. Viral interference with cell-death signaling has been documented for diverse viruses, and it may permit cellular functions to persist during stages in which the virus depends on them. Other infections are accompanied by activation of cell-death pathways; the outcome can contribute to tissue injury and is not always beneficial to the virus. These alternatives should be understood as different relationships between infection, host regulation and cell fate rather than as a universal strategy that every virus follows.

A molecular change that prevents execution of apoptosis does not guarantee that a cell will recover. Upstream stress may continue, cellular metabolism may remain impaired, and alternative forms of death may eventually occur. Likewise increased survival over a short observation interval does not demonstrate that a cell can resume its specialized function or proliferate normally. A useful comparison includes cellular viability, selected physiological output and morphological observations. If survival is inferred only from the absence of one apoptotic marker, the conclusion is too strong: cells can be damaged or die through mechanisms that do not produce that particular marker.

When many cells die within a tissue, the consequence depends on the functions of those cells and the capacity of the tissue to replace them. Loss of epithelial cells can impair a barrier; injury to long-lived, poorly replaced cells may have a lasting effect even when few cells are involved. A rapidly renewing tissue may compensate for some cellular losses, although replacement and inflammation can themselves alter tissue function. The same percentage of dying cells therefore has different biological meanings in different organs. This provides a link from cellular cytopathology to the disease-level reasoning of a later node without requiring a catalogue of clinical conditions.

7. Recognizing and interpreting cytopathic morphology

An adherent animal cell normally maintains a characteristic relationship with its surroundings. Its plasma membrane and cortical cytoskeleton define its outline, adhesion proteins connect it to extracellular material or neighboring cells, and its nucleus occupies a particular position and shape. In a layer of cultured cells, disruption of these relationships may be readily visible. A change that affects only a small number of molecular targets can give rise to a conspicuous change in appearance because cellular shape depends on coordinated mechanical forces. Conversely a serious functional disturbance may initially leave the outline of the cell almost unchanged. Cytopathic morphology is an entry point into the study of cellular injury, not a complete description of it.

Cell rounding is the loss of a spread or elongated outline and the adoption of a more nearly spherical shape. In a normally adherent cell, actin filament organization and attachment to extracellular matrix help distribute tension over a wide area. If attachment weakens or the organization of the cortex changes, the area of contact can decrease. The cell may retract, round up or become detached. These changes do not necessarily imply that the plasma membrane is ruptured. A rounded cell can remain alive, and an uninfected cell can also round normally during mitosis. A student should therefore avoid defining rounding as cell death. The interpretation depends on the original cell type, the time course, its capacity to remain attached, and other indicators of its state.

Adenovirus infection of suitable cultured cells can produce rounded cells aggregated into clusters. This is a recognizable pattern in some cell systems, but the existence of a cluster does not establish the identity of the infecting virus. A rounded cell in contact with another rounded cell still has its own plasma membrane unless the membranes have actually fused. Thus a cluster is not automatically a syncytium. Under a microscope, cell boundaries and nuclear arrangement help distinguish the possibilities. A cell-cell adhesion change and a cell fusion event are fundamentally different mechanisms, even if they both result in large irregular structures at the scale of a low-magnification image.

The term lysis refers to destruction of cellular integrity with the escape of material normally retained in the cell. In a layer of cells this can appear as a decrease in the number of intact cells or the emergence of gaps in regions that were previously occupied. However, gaps can also be produced if cells detach while retaining intact membranes. In an experiment in which an adherent layer becomes sparse, measuring only the cells that remain on the surface can therefore overestimate the number that have lysed. Detached cells should be considered conceptually as part of the population. An extracellular marker normally retained within the cytoplasm provides evidence complementary to microscopy, although the marker may also be released from uninfected cells exposed to other forms of injury.

Enteroviruses are classic examples associated with cytolytic infection in suitable cell types. The visible injury in these systems can develop after a period of molecular reorganization that initially leaves some aspects of cellular integrity intact. It would be incorrect to suppose that a virion has to physically rupture a membrane at the instant of entry in order for a cytolytic phenotype to occur later. Entry permits the intracellular stages to begin; lysis is a possible later cellular outcome. The timing of loss of membrane integrity relative to other observations helps identify which stage is being studied.

Inclusion bodies are regions of conspicuous material within an infected cell, often visible because their staining properties or optical density differ from those of the surrounding cytoplasm or nucleoplasm. A nuclear inclusion is located within the nucleus, whereas a cytoplasmic inclusion lies outside it. The location can help relate a structure to possible viral or cellular processes, but the appearance of an inclusion does not alone establish its composition. Some inclusions contain viral proteins, nucleic acids or assembled structures; some contain a mixture of virus-associated and cellular material. The term is descriptive and should not be confused with a specific molecular structure such as a capsid.

Adenovirus may produce intranuclear inclusions, consistent with important events in its infection occurring in the nucleus. Some herpesvirus infections can also be associated with prominent nuclear inclusions. Rabies virus-associated Negri bodies are cytoplasmic inclusions found in infected neurons, and they are a classical example in virology. Their presence illustrates that distinctive infection-associated structures can be visible even in highly differentiated cells whose function is not adequately described by proliferation or death alone. It would be wrong to infer that every cytoplasmic inclusion represents rabies virus or that all nuclear inclusions are caused by adenoviruses. Morphological patterns become more meaningful when combined with specific molecular or immunological evidence.

A viral inclusion need not be a passive pile of completed virions. A spatially organized concentration of viral RNA and proteins can serve as an active region associated with viral gene expression or genome synthesis, while a different inclusion may contain accumulated structural material. This distinction can be studied using conceptual colocalization reasoning. If a stain for newly synthesized viral RNA is concentrated within an inclusion, the observation supports a relationship between the region and RNA synthesis, although it does not alone demonstrate which molecular interactions are responsible. If an inclusion contains abundant structural proteins but little detectable newly made RNA, it may reflect a different stage or role. Both interpretations require controls for the specificity and sensitivity of the measurements.

A syncytium is a multinucleated cellular structure in which the cytoplasm of previously separate cells has become continuous through cell-cell fusion. In certain infections, virus-associated membrane proteins present on an infected cell can interact with a neighboring cell and cause their plasma membranes to merge. Nuclei that formerly belonged to separate cells then share a cytoplasmic environment. This changes the scale over which cytoplasmic molecules can move and the organization of the tissue. Measles virus is a classical example associated with syncytium formation, and respiratory syncytial virus is another familiar example. The detailed requirements for cell-cell fusion and its comparison with normal developmental fusion belong to a later chapter.

Multinucleation alone does not prove fusion. A cell may undergo nuclear division without completing cytokinesis, producing multiple nuclei while retaining a single continuous cytoplasm that was never shared between independent cells. In contrast, a fusion-derived syncytium results from loss of the boundary between previously separate cells. A useful conceptual distinction comes from following cellular markers associated with two original populations: mixing of cytoplasmic markers from previously distinct cells supports fusion, whereas repeated nuclear division within one labeled cell would not necessarily produce such mixing. These are different histories that may converge on a similar endpoint appearance.

Fusion of a virion envelope with the plasma membrane or an endosomal membrane is also distinct from cell-cell fusion. A virion is not a second cell containing its own nucleus. Fusion during entry can deliver viral material into a host cell without generating a multinucleated cellular structure. Cell-cell fusion involves two cellular plasma membranes and can produce a shared cytoplasm containing nuclei from both participants. A virus that enters by membrane fusion does not necessarily cause prominent syncytia in every infected tissue. Surface expression, activation of relevant proteins and the properties of neighboring cells influence whether cell fusion is observed.

Other cytopathic features include cell enlargement, abnormal vacuoles, alterations in nuclear appearance and changes in the spatial arrangement of cellular components. Cytomegalovirus is a classical example associated with enlarged infected cells and conspicuous inclusions. Enlargement may reflect alterations in cellular volume and internal organization; it is not evidence of a greater number of cells. Vacuolization means that conspicuous spaces or membrane-associated compartments have become visible within the cell. Without additional markers, one cannot assume that all vacuoles are lysosomes, replication compartments or autophagic structures. The number of vacuoles at one moment also reflects the balance of their formation and disappearance.

Morphological descriptors can occur together. A cell may round, detach and later lyse; another may enlarge and develop inclusions without immediately losing its plasma membrane. A syncytium may show degenerative changes at a later time. Listing these descriptors as mutually exclusive types of infection would therefore create a false classification. It is better to describe each observation independently, establish its temporal order and ask what mechanisms could explain the combination. A histological section and a live-cell image may emphasize different features because they sample different spatial and temporal aspects of the process.

The absence of striking cytopathic effects does not exclude infection. Some viruses can produce progeny in cells that retain a recognizable morphology for a substantial interval. A cell may maintain its membrane integrity while losing a specialized physiological function, and a viral genome may persist without continuous production of infectious particles. Conversely cytopathic changes can be seen in an abortive infection. This means that both the presence and the absence of CPE must be interpreted with independent evidence of viral activity. For a NET or SET problem, a description of infected cells that look normal should never be taken by itself as proof that entry or replication has failed.

Tissue-level injury differs from morphology in an isolated culture. The cells that form a tissue exchange signals, adhere to one another and contribute to collective functions that cannot always be inferred from individual viability. Disruption of a small region of epithelial contacts, for instance, can alter the continuity of a barrier even if most cells in the tissue remain intact. A large fused structure may disturb the spatial organization of surrounding cells. In contrast, conspicuous morphology in a culture does not automatically predict the severity of disease in an organism. Tissue architecture, repair, immune responses and the distribution of affected cells influence the eventual outcome. These relationships explain why cytopathology is one component of pathogenesis rather than a synonym for it.

8. Release, local spread and changes in cellular function

The production of viral components does not complete the definition of productive infection. New particles must be assembled into structures capable of initiating an appropriate infection, and they must become accessible for transmission to other susceptible cells. The relationship between assembly and release differs among viruses. A cell may contain viral nucleic acid and abundant structural proteins without producing infectious particles if assembly or maturation is defective. Similarly infectious particles may be retained within a cell or released into its surroundings at different times. These distinctions matter when measurements of intracellular viral material and extracellular infectivity are interpreted.

An enveloped virion contains a lipid membrane surrounding other viral components. The envelope is associated with membrane material derived from the host, with viral membrane proteins contributing to its properties. Budding is a process in which the developing particle becomes surrounded by a region of membrane that curves around it and eventually separates. The membrane used for this process may be at the plasma membrane or within an intracellular compartment, depending on the virus. Cellular trafficking can consequently be important even after the viral genome has been synthesized. A membrane protein present in the wrong cellular location may not participate normally in assembly or release, despite being present in the cell.

The acquisition of an envelope is not simply random trapping of cellular membrane. The structural organization of the particle and the molecular properties of the membrane-associated components influence which region of membrane participates. The energetic requirements of curvature and separation differ from those of maintaining a planar bilayer. Viral and cellular components can contribute to these changes. At the level of this chapter, it is sufficient to recognize that budding represents a coordinated interaction with pre-existing cellular membrane systems rather than the spontaneous appearance of a new membrane around every particle. It also differs topologically from cell lysis: the separating particle has acquired a membrane while the cell need not undergo immediate catastrophic rupture.

Influenza virus illustrates release associated with budding at the cell surface. Its surface glycoproteins include hemagglutinin, which recognizes sialic acid-containing structures, and neuraminidase, which can remove terminal sialic acid residues. The roles of attachment and release must be distinguished. A molecule involved in binding a host surface at one stage of infection is not necessarily performing that same function during release. Neuraminidase activity helps prevent newly produced virions from remaining attached to sialic acid-containing structures. The example connects a property of host-cell surface chemistry with an event occurring after particle formation. It does not mean that all enveloped viruses require a neuraminidase or that budding always occurs at the same cellular site.

Non-enveloped viruses do not acquire a lipid envelope as a defining component of the mature virion. Lysis is a familiar route of release for many non-enveloped viruses, but the association should not be stated as an absolute rule. Non-lytic release mechanisms have also been described for some non-enveloped viruses, and enveloped-virus infection can be accompanied by eventual cell death. The structural class of the particle and the fate of the cell are different properties. A viral envelope indicates a component of particle structure; it does not guarantee that the host cell survives indefinitely.

Release into extracellular space is one route by which infection can reach other cells. Local spread can also involve close contacts between cells, and certain viral infections exhibit cell-cell fusion. The infected and uninfected members of a tissue need not be exposed uniformly because physical distance, tissue organization and cellular susceptibility vary. A cluster of affected cells may therefore develop as an infection extends outward from an initial site. In a conceptual diagram, movement of infectious material and movement of an injury signal should be drawn differently. Nearby cells can be affected by secreted signaling molecules even if they do not themselves contain viral genomes.

Local spread must also be distinguished from increasing amounts of viral material within the original infected cell. If the intensity of a viral protein signal rises within a cell while the fraction of cells showing the signal remains unchanged, the observation concerns an intracellular change rather than demonstrated spread to new cells. If the fraction of labeled cells rises, secondary infection is one possible explanation, but the analysis must distinguish newly infected cells from cells that were infected earlier and crossed the threshold for detection at different times. A time course with independent evidence of new infection provides a stronger basis for conclusions about spread.

A cell can experience physiological costs even when it continues releasing viral material without rapid lysis. Its energy and biosynthetic resources are finite. Maintaining ion gradients, producing cellular proteins and carrying out secretion already require substantial cellular activity. Additional biosynthetic demand and altered trafficking can affect the ability of the infected cell to maintain its ordinary function. However, simple depletion of cellular energy is not a universal explanation for every functional change. Targeted disruption of a membrane transporter or signaling regulator may have a large physiological consequence without a comparable decline in total cellular ATP. A cellular phenotype should be attributed to resource demand only when the relevant measurements support that mechanism.

Consider an epithelial cell whose ability to maintain its contacts is altered during infection. Even without immediate cell death, weakening of adhesion or redistribution of junctional components can affect barrier function. The neighboring cells may remain uninfected but experience a changed local environment. A secretory cell could likewise retain its shape and membrane integrity yet fail to deliver a protein to its proper destination. A neuron might lose a specialized function because of altered intracellular transport or signaling. These examples show why the physiological effect of infection cannot be summarized solely by counting lysed cells. The most relevant cellular measurement depends on what the cell normally contributes to the organism.

The relationship between cytopathic injury and the production of infectious particles is consequently not fixed. A mutation or cellular perturbation that alters membrane integrity could influence extracellular infectivity through effects on several stages. A decrease in extracellular infectious material might reflect fewer particles assembled, delayed release, reduced viability of the infected cell or decreased infectivity of particles that are released. To distinguish these alternatives, one needs evidence about material within the cell, extracellular material and their functional infectivity, as well as the timing of injury. It is inappropriate to assign an effect specifically to release just because only an extracellular measurement has changed.

Cellular alterations can thus be classified by their relationship to the infection cycle rather than simply by their severity. Some changes are associated with the establishment of viral gene expression, others with intracellular organization, others with assembly or egress, and still others with stress and injury. A single altered cellular pathway may affect more than one stage. This explains why the same intervention can have different consequences depending on when it acts. It also explains why a description of an altered cellular structure should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that the structure has a necessary role in viral propagation.

9. Host–virus antagonism and context-dependent outcomes

An infected cell is not a passive substrate. It contains mechanisms that detect disturbances and modify cellular activity in response. Viral nucleic acids or changes in their location may be recognized as unusual by cellular sensing systems. The response can alter transcription, translation, metabolism and the survival of the affected cell. Some responses can also change the state of nearby cells through extracellular signaling molecules. The immunological components of these processes were covered in the preceding subunit; here the question is how they influence the interpretation of intracellular events and visible cellular changes.

Interferons are a familiar example of signals that can induce changes in cellular gene expression associated with antiviral states. A cell receiving such a signal may differ in its ability to support a particular infection from a cell of the same type that has not received the signal. However, an extracellular interferon signal, activation of intracellular signaling intermediates and production of the resulting gene products are distinct events. Failure to detect a downstream gene product could result from failure to produce the signal, failure to respond to it or altered RNA or protein stability. These alternatives explain why one molecular measurement does not establish the complete mechanism of a viral interaction with cellular defense.

Some viruses encode proteins that alter aspects of these cellular responses. The term antagonism describes an interaction in which a viral activity reduces or redirects a host response; it does not mean that the response has disappeared from every infected cell. An infected-cell population may contain cells with different levels of viral gene expression, different signaling states and different degrees of physiological disturbance. An average measurement over the population can conceal these differences. Moreover, suppression of one branch of a response does not demonstrate suppression of all cellular responses. The cell retains many interconnected regulatory systems, and a disturbance in one can alter the behavior of another.

A distinction between the direct effects of viral components and the effects of host responses is particularly important at the level of tissues. If a cell has become damaged, the damage may follow from viral gene expression within it, from its own stress or defense response, or from signals or actions of surrounding cells. These causes can coexist and reinforce one another. An uninfected cell near a focus of infection can change its behavior without expressing a viral protein itself. Conversely an infected cell might show relatively little visible damage during the period in which it produces viral material. Establishing infection status and the timing of a cellular response is essential before asserting that the damage is directly caused by a particular viral product.

A useful counterexample is the difference between a cell that fails to support infection because it lacks a necessary intracellular condition and a cell that initially supports viral activity but later restricts it through a response. Both might show low amounts of viral products at a late observation time. Their earlier trajectories differ: in the first case, the relevant activity never becomes established; in the second, it begins and is subsequently limited. An early observation and a late observation are therefore not redundant measurements. Together they help distinguish failure of establishment from suppression after establishment.

The consequences of infection are also shaped by the normal developmental state of a cell. A proliferating cell expresses regulators and metabolic activities different from those in a quiescent or highly differentiated cell. A virus may perturb the same molecular target in two cells but produce different effects because the target contributes to different cellular processes or has different compensatory pathways. The genetic background of the cell can influence receptor abundance, basal stress responses and the availability of alternative routes. Differences between two cell types should not be attributed to a single viral mechanism unless the relevant cellular difference has been established.

These considerations apply across organisms without implying identical mechanisms in every host. Animal cells lack a rigid cell wall and have familiar adhesion and extracellular matrix systems. Plant cells possess a cell wall and connections through plasmodesmata, and their cellular responses to viral infection can alter development and photosynthetic or transport functions. Plant-virus movement and specific plant disease mechanisms are assigned to later nodes. Here the shared principle is reliance on host-cell biochemical systems and the possibility of altered cellular function; the anatomical consequences and pathways of spread must be adapted to the host rather than transferred uncritically from an animal model.

Evolutionary reasoning helps explain why viral manipulation and cytopathic severity do not follow a single relationship. Viral genetic variation and host variation can change interactions between molecular partners. A cellular protein required for ordinary physiology may be conserved among related hosts, but additional conditions can still determine whether infection proceeds. A phenotype that causes rapid injury in one cell type can differ in another cell type. There is no universal evolutionary rule that viruses become harmless or that more severe cellular damage always corresponds to greater transmission. Transmission depends on many events beyond the single infected cell. The relevant examination skill is to distinguish the measured phenotype from the larger biological outcome that is being inferred.

The variety of host-cell outcomes is particularly clear when productive, abortive and persistent infections are considered. Productive infection establishes infectious progeny; abortive infection fails to do so despite initiation of some infection-associated activity; persistence refers to prolonged maintenance of viral material or infection in a host system. Persistence can involve continued production or periods with little or no detectable production. These terms are not defined by a particular appearance under the microscope. The categories address different aspects of infection, and a persistent productive state is not a contradiction. In the following chapter, persistent changes in growth control are considered as a distinct phenomenon rather than as an inevitable result of prolonged infection.
