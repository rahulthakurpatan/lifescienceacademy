1. From a functioning cell to an infected cell

A cell is not simply a bag containing the substances needed to make a virus. It is a continuously operating system in which energy supply, protein synthesis, membrane traffic, gene regulation and degradation of damaged components are coordinated. A healthy epithelial cell, for example, maintains a physical barrier while transporting selected molecules and communicating with neighboring cells. These activities require thousands of proteins whose concentrations and locations are regulated. Infection introduces an additional source of genetic information into this coordinated system. The information can redirect existing activities and establish new molecular relationships without building an entirely new cell. Understanding the consequences of this redirection is the central problem of viral host-cell biology.

The extracellular infectious viral particle is called a virion. It carries a genome protected by protein, and in some viruses by a lipid envelope as well. The particle has no autonomous ribosomal machinery and cannot continuously synthesize proteins or regenerate energy independently. Inside a suitable living cell, however, its genetic information becomes accessible. Viral gene products may recruit cellular proteins, alter their activity, bind cellular membranes or change which messenger RNAs are used. The host supplies a biochemical environment within which these changes are possible. In contrast to a bacterium that contains its own ribosomes and many of its metabolic enzymes, a virus necessarily depends on the host for translation. This dependence explains why the alteration of cellular behavior is closely connected with viral reproduction rather than an incidental accompaniment of infection.

Attachment and internalization were the subjects of the preceding chapter. A particle can bind to a cell without establishing infection, and a cell can internalize a particle without releasing a functional genome. Once genome access has been established, the question changes from how a physical barrier is crossed to how intracellular processes are altered. These stages should not be collapsed into a single event. The presence of viral antigen inside a cell could reflect incoming material rather than new synthesis; detection of newly made viral RNA supports a later stage but does not by itself demonstrate production of infectious particles. This distinction becomes important whenever an examination asks which step of infection is affected by an experimental perturbation.

A cell that allows a virus to enter is described as susceptible in the relevant context. A permissive cell supports the intracellular events necessary for productive infection. Susceptibility and permissiveness can coexist but need not do so. A cell might display the appropriate surface receptor while lacking an essential intracellular function or maintaining a restrictive physiological state. In an abortive infection, some viral activities begin but infectious progeny are not produced. Nevertheless, abortive infection may perturb the host, for example through expression of an early viral protein or activation of a cellular stress response. Therefore failure to recover infectious progeny does not prove that the host remained unchanged.

Productive infection means that the sequence of intracellular events culminates in production of infectious progeny. It does not specify the fate of the host cell. Some cells are destroyed during or after productive infection, whereas others continue releasing progeny for appreciable periods. The term cytopathic effect, often abbreviated CPE, refers to recognizable structural or functional injury associated with infection; in cell culture it is especially used for observable changes in cell morphology. A cell can produce particles before developing an obvious morphological abnormality. Conversely a cell can die without completing production. These are different axes of observation: viral reproductive outcome, cellular function and visible morphology.

A useful causal sequence is genome accessibility, viral gene expression, interaction between viral and cellular components, altered cell physiology and, in suitable conditions, production and release of progeny. This sequence is not a rigid clock. Some changes originate from events at entry, others develop during genome synthesis, and still others occur as a consequence of host stress. Different host cells can respond differently to the same virus because their basal physiology, differentiation state, regulatory machinery and defense capacities differ. A complete explanation identifies the relevant stage, the altered cellular process, its consequence for the cell and the evidence linking the change to infection.

2. How cellular alterations became scientific evidence

Early studies of viruses were constrained by the inability to propagate many of them in ordinary nutrient media. Unlike many bacteria, viruses required suitable living material. Cultured cells eventually provided a setting in which investigators could observe the appearance of infected cells and compare them with uninfected cells maintained in the same environment. This was an important conceptual change: cellular injury could now be separated experimentally from many of the complex interactions that occur in an entire infected organism. Although culture systems do not reproduce every aspect of tissue physiology, they made it possible to ask directly whether infectious material could alter the structure or function of a particular cell type.

The work of John Enders, Thomas Weller and Frederick Robbins on poliovirus propagation in tissue culture was a landmark in this development. Their demonstration that poliovirus could be propagated using cultured human tissues broadened the available experimental systems. The achievement was not simply an improved way to detect virus; it established the usefulness of living-cell systems for studying viral growth outside a whole organism. Visible cellular injury became one important observation in such systems, but the ability to demonstrate propagation was the decisive feature. An abnormal-looking cell alone is insufficient evidence that a particular virus has multiplied.

The development of animal-virus plaque assays provided another important connection between local cellular damage and infectious propagation. A plaque is a localized area of altered or destroyed cells in a layer of susceptible cells under appropriate assay conditions. Its appearance is the cumulative consequence of infection and subsequent local spread. A count of plaques measures infectious units capable of initiating detectable foci under those conditions; it is not a direct enumeration of all physical particles. The distinction explains why molecular measurements of genome abundance can disagree with a biological measure of infectivity. A sample may contain genomes or particles that are unable to complete the events necessary to produce detectable infection.

Microscopy established that different infections produce different patterns of cellular alteration. Electron microscopy added information about changes in internal membranes, the accumulation of particles and the location of cellular structures. However, structural information must be interpreted in relation to time and biochemical function. A cluster of membranes may be associated with replication, assembly or cellular degradation. Its shape does not identify which of these processes occurs there. Localization of appropriate molecular markers and evidence that a process occurs at that site strengthen the interpretation. The same caution applies to conspicuous inclusions within cells: some are related to viral factories or accumulated viral products, while others include altered host components.

Studies of cellular protein synthesis added a different type of evidence. Investigators could distinguish a general decrease in protein production from a redistribution of synthesis toward viral proteins. Such results established that host shutoff need not mean that every ribosome has stopped functioning. The cellular machinery can continue to operate while becoming less available to ordinary cellular transcripts. Later work on initiation factors, RNA turnover and viral messenger RNA features helped explain why host and viral products could be affected unequally. This is a good example of how a physiological observation becomes a mechanistic question: first identify what changes, then identify the molecular interaction responsible.

No single experimental observation describes the entire infected cell. A microscope image reveals structure, a biochemical measurement reveals a selected molecular activity, and an infectivity measurement reveals the ability of material to initiate further productive infection under specified conditions. Their combination is more informative than any individual result. A comparison must also include appropriate untreated and uninfected controls, because changes in nutrition, culture density, chemical exposure and sample handling may alter cells independently of infection. Scientific reasoning therefore proceeds from converging observations rather than from naming a characteristic appearance and assuming its mechanism.

3. Redirection of host gene expression and protein synthesis

Gene expression involves several distinct stages. A cellular gene may be transcribed into RNA, the RNA may be processed and transported, and a mature messenger RNA may be translated on ribosomes. The resulting protein can be folded, modified, transported and eventually degraded. Changing any one of these stages can alter the quantity of functional protein in the cell. Consequently a reduction in the abundance of a host protein cannot automatically be attributed to inhibition of transcription. The messenger RNA may have become unstable, translation initiation may be impaired, or protein turnover may have increased. A mechanistic explanation requires a measurement that distinguishes these possibilities.

Most cellular messenger RNAs use a modified guanosine cap near the five-prime end to help recruit the eukaryotic translation initiation machinery. The cap is recognized by eIF4E, which participates in the eIF4F complex together with the scaffold eIF4G and the RNA helicase eIF4A. Through further interactions, an initiation complex containing the small ribosomal subunit is recruited and proceeds toward an appropriate initiation codon. The large ribosomal subunit subsequently joins. Elongation and termination occur after initiation. The important point here is that cap recognition and recruitment are regulatory steps upstream of peptide bond formation. A change in these steps can restrict translation without directly destroying the catalytic function of the ribosome.

Poliovirus supplies a textbook example of unequal effects on cellular and viral messenger RNA translation. A viral protease cleaves the cellular scaffold protein eIF4G, disrupting important interactions used for ordinary cap-dependent initiation. Poliovirus RNA contains an internal ribosome entry site, or IRES, through which translation initiation can occur without conventional cap recognition. The IRES is a functional RNA region that recruits components of the translation machinery through a different set of interactions. The viral RNA still requires host ribosomes and relevant initiation factors. The result is not independence from the cell, but a different dependency on its components.

It is tempting to imagine a viral protease cutting every initiation factor so that only viral proteins can be synthesized. This picture would be misleading. Translation of viral RNA remains a cellularly supported process, and the extent and timing of host shutoff depend on the virus and the physiological condition of the infected cell. Some cellular RNAs can continue to be translated under circumstances that suppress many other messages. Furthermore, additional changes in RNA metabolism and signaling can contribute to the net suppression of host protein synthesis. The cap-dependent versus internal-initiation comparison is a useful model of differential access to the machinery, not a claim that one molecular event explains every effect of every picornavirus.

Another mechanism acts at the level of messenger RNA abundance. Herpes simplex virus expresses a virion host shutoff protein, commonly called vhs, which promotes degradation of messenger RNA. Reduced RNA stability can decrease the amount of template available to translating ribosomes. A fall in cellular protein synthesis can therefore occur even when the host is still able to initiate transcription and its ribosomes remain intact. Because transcript degradation also affects the balance of viral messages in infection, it should not be described as a perfectly selective mechanism that recognizes every host message and spares every viral one. The molecular target and timing differ from proteolytic disruption of translation initiation.

Influenza virus provides a further illustration of distinct mechanisms operating at different stages of RNA expression. Its messenger RNAs obtain short capped leaders derived from host transcripts during viral transcription, a process known as cap snatching. This uses capped cellular RNA material to support viral gene expression. Separately, viral proteins including PA-X can contribute to host RNA shutoff. Cap acquisition and host RNA degradation should not be treated as identical events: one concerns the initiation of viral messenger RNA production, while the other changes the abundance of cellular RNA. Neither gives influenza an independent ribosome. These examples are useful because similar reductions in cellular protein synthesis arise through different molecular routes.

A cell may also reduce its own translation in response to infection. One well-known regulatory route involves phosphorylation of the initiation factor eIF2 alpha, which reduces the efficiency with which the machinery delivers the initiator transfer RNA to the small ribosomal subunit. Stress-responsive kinases can promote this phosphorylation. A consequence is decreased initiation on many messenger RNAs, although selected stress-associated transcripts can respond differently. This cellular response can reduce the availability of translation machinery to the virus, but it also changes normal cellular protein production. A decrease in translation can thus represent a direct viral effect, a host response or a combination of both. Observing the decrease does not establish which participant initiated it.

A reduction in ongoing synthesis does not instantaneously erase pre-existing proteins. Suppose a membrane transporter has a long cellular lifetime. Even if new synthesis is suppressed, its existing molecules may remain active for some time. In contrast, a rapidly degraded regulatory protein may decline soon after synthesis is reduced. This difference follows from the balance between production and removal. If the protein concentration is P, its synthesis rate is s and its first-order removal constant is k, the simplest model is dP/dt = s − kP. The model assumes a homogeneous pool and constant rates during the interval of interest. At steady state the production rate equals the removal rate, so P = s/k. After complete shutoff of synthesis, the concentration declines exponentially, with a half-life equal to ln(2)/k. Real proteins can depart from this model when trafficking, dilution or regulated degradation changes, but the model explains why loss of synthesis and loss of physiological function need not occur simultaneously.

Suppression of host gene expression can affect signaling receptors, cytoskeletal regulators, secreted factors and components of defense responses. A cell that previously maintained a stable membrane or secretory output may become unable to replenish proteins removed by normal turnover. Nevertheless, translation is only one determinant of cellular injury. A protein can remain abundant but become inactive if it is mislocalized or chemically modified. A change in RNA abundance can also occur without an immediate measurable change in protein abundance. Students should therefore separate transcriptional abundance, translational activity, protein amount and functional output. These are related measurements, not interchangeable ones.

A simplified experiment may compare a cellular cap-dependent reporter with a reporter whose initiation follows an alternative route. Reporter output is interpreted as a proxy for gene expression only if the relevant controls are considered. If the cap-dependent signal decreases while the alternative signal remains relatively stable, selective impairment of conventional initiation is consistent with the observation. However, unequal messenger RNA stability, reporter protein degradation and differences in baseline expression can also influence the signals. Measuring RNA amounts and verifying comparable cellular viability help narrow the interpretation. The experiment illustrates how an investigator distinguishes competing explanations; the numbers in later worked problems are hypothetical and are not reported measurements from historical studies.
