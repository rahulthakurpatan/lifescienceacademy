1. Infection is not malignancy

A normal multicellular organism depends on the ability of its cells to divide when replacement is necessary and to stop dividing when the need has been met. A cell in a renewing epithelium may undergo several rounds of division before its descendants specialize, whereas a differentiated cell may perform its assigned function for a long period without replicating its nuclear DNA. Neither condition is inherently abnormal. Cancer arises when a population of altered cells escapes the restraints that coordinate cell number, tissue architecture and survival. A virus can disturb these restraints, but the presence of viral material in a cell is not itself a diagnosis of cancer. The important question is what that material does to the cellular system in which it persists.

A virus has a genome and depends on host cells for essential aspects of gene expression and reproduction. Some viral genes affect cellular processes such as DNA synthesis, signaling and survival because the virus depends on a suitable intracellular environment. Those effects can also disturb the growth-control network of the host. Other infections create conditions in the tissue that indirectly increase the probability of malignant evolution. These two routes are not mutually exclusive, and they do not apply equally to every virus. In particular, a direct viral effect on a growth regulator and the long-term consequences of inflammation must be distinguished before considering how they can cooperate.

Infection means that a virus has established itself in susceptible cells; productive infection means that infectious progeny are made. Persistence means that the infection or viral genetic material remains over time. Latency refers to a state in which the viral genome is retained with restricted expression and little or no production of progeny, with the details depending on the virus. Transformation refers to a change in cellular properties associated with abnormal growth or survival. Immortalization means that cells can avoid the usual finite replicative lifespan under specified conditions. Malignancy adds properties such as invasion of tissue and, in some cases, the capacity to disseminate. A transformed or immortalized cell is therefore not automatically a malignant cell.

The distinction is important at the level of a population as well as at the level of one cell. A cancer begins in a lineage of cells, and a lineage must survive long enough for heritable changes to be retained. If an infection is cleared, the relevant exposure may end before a changed lineage establishes itself. If an infected cell dies, it may make no lasting contribution to the tissue. If a cell persists but remains subject to normal growth restraints, infection may have no malignant consequence. Only a minority of infected individuals develop the cancers associated with most oncogenic viruses. That observation is compatible with a causal role for the virus because causation in a multistep disease does not require every exposed person to become ill.

An oncogenic virus is one for which evidence supports a causal contribution to one or more cancers. It does not follow that every member of its viral family, every genotype, or every infected tissue carries equal risk. Tissue context matters: the normal life history of epithelial cells differs from that of lymphocytes and hepatocytes. A protein that interferes with a cell-cycle regulator can have different consequences depending on whether that cell is actively renewing, resting, differentiating or undergoing a damage response. The same general cellular pathways can therefore be disturbed in distinct biological settings without producing identical tumors.

The preceding cancer chapters explain oncogenes, tumor suppressors, checkpoints and cell-death control as properties of the host genome and cell. In viral oncogenesis the same concepts remain valid, but the perturbation can originate from a foreign gene product or from persistent injury. The molecular identity of the perturbation does not redefine the cellular pathway. A viral protein can diminish tumor-suppressor function even when the corresponding human gene has not acquired a disabling mutation. Conversely, a cell can carry an oncogenic virus and still require additional host changes before its descendants become malignant.

The preceding cancer chapters establish a distinction that remains essential when studying infectious causes of disease. A cancer is an expanding lineage of host cells whose growth, survival, differentiation or relationships with surrounding tissues have become abnormal. A virus, in contrast, is an infectious agent that depends on a host cell to reproduce. The number of viral genomes, the number of infected cells and the number of malignant cells are therefore different variables. An infection can produce large amounts of viral material while destroying the cells that support its replication. Such an outcome is not equivalent to formation of a persistent expanding host lineage. A virus-associated tumor must be explained at the level of the host population as well as at the level of the infectious agent.

A productive infection has a biological objective quite different from the apparent behavior of a cancer cell. In a productive infection, viral genetic information directs the production of components needed for infectious progeny. In a malignant lineage, host cells produce descendants that retain the altered cellular behavior. Viral replication can damage a host cell or lead to its loss; malignant progression instead requires that at least some altered host cells survive and contribute descendants. These outcomes can coexist in different cells or at different times. The detection of abundant infectious particles is therefore not a general requirement for identifying a virus-associated cancer. In some such tumors, expression of selected viral genes is more relevant than production of infectious progeny.

A causal connection can operate at several biological levels. A viral protein may interfere with a host regulator of cell division. A persistent infection may alter signals that normally control the survival of a particular cell population. Recurrent injury associated with infection may change the tissue environment and the demand for regeneration. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. A direct interaction between a viral protein and a host tumor suppressor does not eliminate the importance of immune control or tissue context. Conversely, a cancer associated with chronic injury need not contain the same direct molecular interaction found in the classic HPV example. Identifying the level at which a particular observation was made helps prevent inappropriate generalization across viral causes.

For an infection to contribute to a multistep process, its effect need not be sufficient to establish every feature of the final malignant population. A cell that has lost an effective restraint on S-phase entry may still undergo death when damage accumulates. A cell whose survival is prolonged may retain normal restrictions on proliferation. A tissue undergoing persistent injury may generate many dividing cells without any one lineage acquiring the properties needed for malignancy. The relevance of a viral contribution is that it can change one or more of these barriers, altering the likelihood that subsequent events will produce a harmful lineage. A distinction between contribution and sufficiency is therefore central to the interpretation of cancer causation.

It is useful to regard viral exposure as the beginning of several possible histories rather than the first step of a fixed sequence ending in cancer. An exposed individual may not establish infection; an infection may subsequently resolve; persistent infection may remain without a neoplastic lesion; a lesion may arise and fail to progress. Different individuals and tissues can follow different branches. The important event for each branch is a biological change in the state of the host or its cells. A molecular mechanism that explains one branch cannot automatically be used to calculate the frequency of every other branch. This distinction will become important in the quantitative section, where the denominator changes according to which transition is being examined.

Host specificity and tissue specificity are also distinct from cancer specificity. The capacity of a virus to persist in a particular tissue depends on its interactions with host cells. The capacity of an altered cell to form a tumor depends on the regulatory and developmental environment of that tissue. A viral gene expressed in a renewing epithelial compartment may affect the balance between proliferation and differentiation. A different viral gene acting in a lymphocyte may alter responsiveness to normal signals that regulate survival. These examples illustrate why the same general category of infection-associated cancer contains distinct biological mechanisms. Memorizing a virus name and a tumor name is less useful than explaining the connection through the relevant cellular lineage.

A tumor-associated change may also occur in cells that do not produce a complete viral genome. An altered tissue environment can influence neighboring cells, and a fragment of viral DNA retained in a host chromosome may have consequences that differ from those of an infectious particle. A finding that an altered cell does not release virus is thus compatible with some forms of viral contribution to cancer. It does not by itself prove that a fragment is biologically active. The relevant evidence would connect the retained genetic material or environmental alteration with a particular change in the host lineage. This is the reason the following sections distinguish physical persistence, gene expression and functional consequences.

2. From association to molecular evidence

Early cancer research had to reconcile two apparently different observations. Some tumors appeared as consequences of transmissible biological agents in animals, whereas other tumors arose without any identifiable infection. Work with animal tumor viruses established that an infectious agent could influence the development of a tumor under certain conditions. The importance of these observations was not the conclusion that cancer in general is contagious. Rather, they suggested that an agent outside the cell could initiate or sustain changes in cellular growth. The finding gave researchers a way to ask which cellular functions had been disturbed.

The discovery of viral oncogenes and related cellular genes provided a further conceptual change. A normal cellular gene can encode a protein that promotes division when appropriately regulated. Such a proto-oncogene is not a disease gene in its normal state. Viral oncogenes and viral proteins that interfere with growth restraints helped reveal that tumor development depends on altered regulation of ordinary cellular functions, rather than a completely new form of cellular chemistry. The term viral oncoprotein includes proteins that act on host regulators; it should not be treated as synonymous with a captured cellular proto-oncogene. HPV E6 and E7 illustrate the former type of relationship.

The 1964 report by Epstein, Achong and Barr identified virus-like particles in cultured cells derived from Burkitt lymphoma. The observation was important because it connected a human tumor with a candidate infectious agent at a time when the existence of such associations was a major question. Electron microscopy supplied information about the presence and morphology of particles rather than a direct measurement of cancer causation. The relevant limitation was that the cells were observed after establishment in culture; the observation did not establish whether every malignant cell in the original tissue contained the agent or whether the agent was sufficient to initiate the tumor. Subsequent molecular, epidemiological and functional studies addressed those different questions.

The evidence for a connection between hepatitis B and liver cancer illustrates a different historical approach. Beasley and colleagues' 1981 prospective study enrolled 22,707 men in Taiwan. They compared subsequent primary liver cancer incidence between men with and without detectable hepatitis B surface antigen; the reported rates differed markedly. The exposure marker was not itself a measure of malignant-cell behavior. A prospective comparison had the advantage of establishing that the measured infection-associated state was present before the observed cancer outcome. This temporal relationship is more informative about causation than simply finding viral markers in people who already have a tumor. It nevertheless does not specify which cellular interactions, chromosomal changes or injury-related processes produced each cancer. The historical finding established a strong population association that molecular and physiological studies could then investigate.

The 1989 study by Dyson and colleagues connected HPV16 E7 with the host retinoblastoma gene product. The important advance was identification of an association between a viral oncoprotein and a host protein already known to participate in tumor suppression. It supplied a molecular connection between two previously distinct lines of investigation: viral transformation and the genetics of inherited or somatically acquired cancer susceptibility. Protein association alone does not establish that all downstream gene expression changes are caused by that interaction. Additional evidence about functional restraint, protein state and cellular progression is needed. The significance of the original result is the discovery of a specific target rather than a complete demonstration of every step of HPV carcinogenesis.

In 1990 Scheffner and colleagues reported that E6 from the high-risk types HPV16 and HPV18 promotes p53 degradation. This finding helped explain how a host suppressor can be functionally weakened without requiring the initial deletion of the gene encoding it. It addressed a different property from protein association alone: the abundance of a target protein can change as a consequence of the viral protein's effect on turnover. The result supplied a mechanism compatible with reduced p53-dependent responses in infected cells. It did not show that E6 by itself generates every feature of malignancy or that all papillomavirus types have identical consequences. These historical studies illustrate why the direction of regulation matters in interpreting a viral oncoprotein.

A further line of evidence comes from prevention. If an infection contributes causally to particular cancers, an intervention that reduces the relevant infections should, over a suitable period, reduce outcomes along that causal pathway. Evidence of reduced persistent infection or reduced precursor lesions answers an earlier question in the sequence than evidence of reduced invasive cancer. The time needed to observe these endpoints differs because the progression histories differ. Thus the appropriate comparison must specify what was measured and over what period. Population evidence from prevention and molecular evidence from host regulatory interactions are complementary: each tests a different prediction of the causal explanation.

The historical examples establish why an examination question may supply several kinds of evidence without asking students to treat them as equivalent. A microscopic observation of viral material addresses presence. A prospective cohort addresses temporal association. A biochemical observation addresses an interaction or a change in activity. A functional comparison can address a role in a defined cellular phenotype. A preventive observation addresses the contribution of infection to subsequent outcomes. The best-supported explanation combines these findings while retaining the limitations of each. Students should not claim that a single association assay established an entire causal chain when the actual result supports only one link.

3. Persistence, genomic context and expression

A viral genome can persist in different physical forms. An episome is an extrachromosomal genetic element; in the context of a DNA virus it can be retained separately from the host's chromosomes. Integration refers to viral genetic material becoming part of chromosomal DNA. These states are not equivalent to the presence or absence of active viral gene expression. Chromosomal position, regulatory regions and cell type influence transcription, while an extrachromosomal element may also express genes. For cancer reasoning, it is therefore necessary to distinguish the location of the viral genome from the identities and amounts of its products.

An important example is high-risk HPV. During its ordinary epithelial infection, viral DNA can be maintained as an episome. In many HPV-associated cancers, viral DNA is integrated into the host genome, and normal control of viral gene expression can be disturbed. Integration is not universal among HPV-associated cancers, however. Some retain episomal DNA or contain a mixture of episomal and integrated forms. Therefore a statement that integration is necessary for every HPV-associated malignancy is too strong. The cancer-relevant issue is prolonged disruption of cellular regulation in a susceptible lineage; physical genome organization is one contributor to this state.

Integration also differs among unrelated viruses. HBV has a DNA phase and can leave integrated viral sequences in hepatocyte chromosomes, but integration is not an obligatory step of its normal replication cycle. HCV is an RNA virus without an obligatory DNA intermediate and does not require chromosomal integration to contribute to cancer. EBV commonly persists as an episomal DNA genome in latently infected cells. These contrasts are useful because they prevent students from generalizing an integration-dependent explanation to infections whose cancer biology follows a different route.

The duration of altered cellular regulation has consequences that a single observation cannot capture. A protein that changes checkpoint activity briefly may produce a reversible physiological disturbance; a prolonged change can be experienced over many generations of a cellular lineage. The persistence of an infection should nevertheless not be equated with uninterrupted cell division or constant production of viral particles. Latent viral programs can maintain restricted expression, chronic hepatitis can involve recurring periods of injury, and the immune system can exert control that changes with time. These are qualitatively different temporal patterns.

Clonality supplies another clue about the history of a tumor. If many malignant cells contain the same distinctive viral-genome configuration, common ancestry of those cells can explain the shared observation. Such a finding supports the idea that the viral material was present before expansion of that clone. It does not establish the exact date of acquisition, exclude earlier host changes, or show that viral material alone caused malignant growth. A lineage can inherit many neutral features along with the features responsible for its growth. Distinguishing a causal alteration from a historical marker requires more than observing its inheritance.

It is tempting to describe persistence and integration as successive mandatory stages, but their relationship depends on the virus. A useful classification therefore begins by asking whether viral genetic material remains, then whether the relevant cell expresses a viral product or experiences long-standing tissue injury, then whether the resulting changes are associated with cancer-related traits. This reasoning is compatible with all four major human examples in this chapter and avoids making the HPV pathway a universal model.

Persistence has a particular meaning in cancer biology because the relevant regulatory influence may need to be retained as host cells divide. A DNA molecule maintained outside the chromosomes must remain available to the descendants that express the relevant genes. Chromosomal integration provides another possible mode of retention, because the viral sequence becomes associated with a chromosome that the host copies and segregates. Neither description specifies which viral genes are active. A retained genome may be transcriptionally restricted, and an integrated sequence may be disrupted or silent. The effect on growth therefore depends on the expression of relevant genetic information and not just on the physical location of the DNA.

The physical form of a viral genome can be understood by comparison with normal chromosome inheritance. A cell carrying an integrated sequence may pass the sequence to both daughters if the affected chromosome is duplicated and segregated normally. The corresponding viral DNA can then become a marker of the lineage's history. An episomal genome can also persist across divisions through mechanisms appropriate to the virus, though its inheritance need not follow precisely the behavior of a single chromosomal allele. The distinction matters because a cell does not have to contain integrated viral DNA to maintain a long-term viral influence. Conversely, integration does not automatically establish stable expression or a causal role in the tumor.

Integration can change the relationship between viral regulatory regions and viral coding sequences. The consequences depend on which parts of the viral genome are retained and how the integrated DNA is regulated in its host context. In HPV-associated lesions, disruption of viral regulatory regions can be associated with altered expression of E6 and E7, but the relevant expression can also persist in lesions without the same integration event. Thus an examination question should distinguish a particular mechanism observed in a subset of lesions from a universally necessary stage. The evidence needed to assess functional disruption includes the state of the relevant transcripts or products and their effects on host pathways.

Host chromatin supplies another layer of context. The same DNA sequence may be transcribed differently according to its local regulatory environment and the factors available within the cell. This is true for host genes and can also be relevant to viral material retained within host cells. A measurement of viral copy number is therefore not a substitute for a measurement of the relevant transcript or protein. Even a comparison of transcript amounts does not automatically establish the strength of a host phenotype, since protein stability and activity can differ. The sequence from DNA retention to cellular function contains several distinguishable stages, each of which may be affected by a different regulatory process.

A useful distinction is between persistent infection at the organismal level and continuous expression within a particular neoplastic lineage. An organism can contain a reservoir of viral genomes in cells that are not themselves malignant. The finding that an individual has a persistent infection does not therefore show that every tumor that subsequently develops contains the virus or depends on its activity. Conversely, a tumor containing expressed viral sequences may maintain a local influence even if circulating measures of infection do not directly report that expression. Organism-level infection status, tissue-level localization and cell-level functional dependence answer distinct questions.

Latent infection does not mean absence of all biological activity. In the case of EBV, restricted programs of viral expression are associated with different cell states. A cell may retain viral genetic material and express a selected subset of viral products without undergoing the full productive program. The significance for oncogenesis is that a host cell can experience altered signaling or survival in the absence of abundant infectious particle formation. The exact retained expression pattern must be interpreted in the context of the disease. A diagram that depicts latency as a single universally silent state would obscure the important differences among the programs discussed later.

An apparent association between viral copy number and a host phenotype can arise because the viral material is more abundant within individual affected cells or because a larger fraction of the sampled tissue contains affected cells. These explanations have different implications for the biology of the lineage. For instance, a tissue sample containing a small number of cells with abundant viral DNA can yield a similar average signal to a sample containing many cells with fewer copies. The molecular readout alone does not distinguish those arrangements. Localization and cell composition provide important context when interpreting the persistence of a viral contribution.

4. The normal p53 and Rb control systems

The two major host regulators in the HPV example act within different but interacting systems. The retinoblastoma protein, commonly called pRb, is encoded by RB1. It participates in the regulation of genes needed for progression into S phase. When pRb is in a growth-restraining state, its interaction with E2F family transcription factors contributes to repression of a set of genes required for DNA synthesis and cell-cycle progression. This helps ensure that a cell does not initiate genome duplication merely because the machinery is present.

Normal extracellular growth signals influence cyclin-dependent kinase activity. Changes in pRb phosphorylation during the cell cycle are associated with changes in its ability to regulate E2F-dependent transcription. The transition from a growth-restraining state toward S-phase gene expression is normally coordinated with the needs of the tissue and other checkpoints. This is a simplification of the full RB-family and E2F-family network, but it captures the important distinction: pRb is a regulator of transcriptional access and cell-cycle commitment, not a DNA polymerase. An altered pRb interaction can thus change the timing of DNA synthesis without directly changing the chemical reaction catalyzed by DNA polymerases.

The p53 protein, encoded by TP53, is a transcription factor that can accumulate and become activated in response to cellular stresses, including DNA damage. Among its transcriptional targets is CDKN1A, encoding the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21. Increased p21 activity can limit cyclin-CDK activity and delay progression through the cell cycle. This delay is biologically useful because it separates the detection of a problem from the decision to continue replication. In other contexts, p53-dependent responses include senescence or apoptosis. The nature of the response depends on the type of damage, its magnitude, the cellular context and the status of other pathways.

A cell with damaged DNA does not always die. Repair, temporary arrest and resumption of proliferation can be appropriate outcomes. Conversely, survival after a damaging event does not demonstrate successful repair. In a question, the observation of a viable cell and the observation of an intact genome are distinct. p53 helps regulate responses to abnormal conditions, whereas DNA repair enzymes carry out the chemistry of repairing particular lesions. Loss of p53 activity is therefore not equivalent to deletion of every DNA repair gene. It changes the probability that an abnormal cell will continue through the cycle or be removed.

The connection between these safeguards can be understood by tracing p21 activity to CDKs and the regulation of pRb. A damage-responsive cell can restrict DNA synthesis in part through this chain. But pRb also responds to regulators outside the p53 pathway, and p53 has functions beyond p21. Neither protein should be interpreted as a complete replacement for the other. A measurement of E2F target-gene expression reports one aspect of the growth-control network; a measurement of a p53-responsive transcript reports another. Exam questions that give both measurements are often testing whether the student can distinguish cell-cycle commitment from stress-responsive arrest.

Protein abundance is another important distinction. A cell may transcribe a normal quantity of TP53 messenger RNA and nevertheless contain little functional p53 protein if protein turnover is increased. Conversely, an abundant protein may be functionally compromised by a mutation, inappropriate localization or altered binding. Thus gene sequence, RNA abundance, protein abundance and biological activity are four different kinds of evidence. Their relationship is central to the interpretation of HPV-associated cancer and connects the present chapter with protein degradation and gene expression in Unit 3.

The pRB and p53 systems were introduced in 4.E.04 as normal restraints that can be weakened in cancer through changes in host genes or their regulation. The viral example extends that reasoning by introducing a foreign protein that acts on the host's existing machinery. The host gene may retain a normal coding sequence while its product is bound, degraded or prevented from performing its usual role. This makes viral oncogenesis a particularly useful examination model for distinguishing a genotype from a functional pathway. If the question reports reduced p53 function despite a normal TP53 sequence, the two observations need not conflict. The relevant defect may concern a regulatory interaction rather than the coding sequence.

In a simplified cell-cycle model, pRB restrains activating E2F-dependent transcription associated with progression toward DNA synthesis. A viral interaction that reduces that restraint can increase the opportunity for expression of S-phase-associated genes. The resulting effect is not equivalent to supplying DNA polymerase, nucleotides or every other requirement for cell division. It modifies a particular regulatory relationship. If another essential component of proliferation is defective, weakening pRB may fail to produce a completed cell cycle. Examination questions can therefore distinguish release of a transcriptional restraint from proof that all cells have divided successfully.

In the normal damage-response system, p53 can coordinate transcriptional responses that influence arrest and cell fate. A reduction in p53 availability can weaken such responses even if the upstream signals associated with damage remain intact. A cell may thus possess substantial evidence of DNA damage without mounting the expected p53-dependent output. The downstream consequence depends on which protective processes remain functional. Some arrest or removal responses can still occur through other mechanisms. This is why an observation of impaired p53 signaling should not be interpreted as absence of every possible checkpoint or death pathway.

The two regulatory systems become especially informative when their relationship to one another is considered. Inappropriate entry into a proliferative state can expose a cell to replication-associated stress. A functioning damage response can limit the contribution of affected descendants to the population. If an alteration both weakens a growth restraint and reduces the response to damage, the two effects can cooperate. The first increases the opportunity for inappropriate progression; the second can reduce one of the consequences that would otherwise limit that progression. This relationship explains why the combination of viral effects can matter even though pRB and p53 do not perform identical molecular functions.

A viral interaction can also make the classification of an oncogenic alteration depend on the level at which the question is asked. The introduction or expression of a viral oncoprotein represents an additional biological activity in the host cell. Its effect may be loss of activity of a normal host tumor suppressor. The first description concerns the viral product; the second concerns the host pathway. The same observation can therefore be understood as acquisition of an interfering activity and functional loss of a growth restraint without contradiction. Students should avoid classifying a viral product simply as a host tumor-suppressor mutation because the downstream phenotype resembles suppressor loss.

Feedback makes the interpretation of host protein abundance more subtle. When the normal pRB-associated restraint is ineffective, regulatory responses may increase expression of proteins that normally oppose its inactivation. Increased abundance of p16 can be observed in relevant HPV-associated contexts, even though the pathway does not restore normal repression of the appropriate transcriptional outputs. The observation illustrates that expression of an upstream inhibitory component does not guarantee effective control of its downstream target. It also explains why an isolated increase in a growth-restraining protein should not automatically be taken as evidence that the growth restraint has been restored.

The relevant comparison with the two-hit model is functional rather than a claim that every virus-associated tumor has two altered alleles at RB1 or TP53. In the classical inherited suppressor model, a lineage may lose the normal contribution of both host alleles. In the viral example, a product can act on proteins derived from otherwise functioning alleles. The cell may thus exhibit a defect in the pathway without the same genomic pattern. Additional host alterations can accumulate during progression, but their presence cannot be inferred from the viral interaction alone. This is a general principle in cancer biology: distinct molecular routes may converge on similar failures of cellular regulation.

5. High-risk HPV and disruption of host growth control

Human papillomaviruses comprise numerous viral types. Only some are classified as high risk for cancer, and not every infection with a high-risk type progresses to cancer. Persistent infection is important in the causal pathway to cervical cancer and contributes to other anogenital cancers and a subset of oropharyngeal cancers. These examples concern particular tissues and virus types. An association with one epithelial cancer does not establish that a viral type causes every epithelial cancer, nor is an ordinary transient HPV infection equivalent to a precancerous lesion.

The two classical viral proteins are E6 and E7. In high-risk HPV, the cancer-associated action of E6 includes reduction of p53 through ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation. Ubiquitin is a small protein that can serve as a molecular tag on other proteins, and the cellular ubiquitin-proteasome system regulates the lifetime of numerous normal cellular proteins. The E6-associated protein E6AP, also known as UBE3A, is a cellular ubiquitin ligase involved in this relationship. The important lesson is that a foreign regulatory protein can redirect an existing host protein-turnover process. It does not need to mutate TP53 to lower the amount of its protein product.

When p53-dependent responses are attenuated, the relationship between cellular stress and cell-cycle restraint changes. For example, induction of a p53 target after damage may be reduced despite the retention of an apparently normal TP53 sequence. The result should be interpreted as functional disruption, not proof that the gene has been deleted. The downstream consequences differ with cell type and with the condition of other regulators. A tumor may have additional alterations, and residual p53-independent arrest and death responses can remain possible. Therefore it is inaccurate to describe E6 as eliminating every means by which cells detect or respond to damage.

E7 is classically associated with disturbance of the Rb pathway. Interaction with pRb compromises its ability to restrain E2F-dependent transcription and can be associated with loss of pRb protein. The E2F-regulated genes involved in progression toward S phase are consequently less effectively controlled by this restraint. The important distinction is between the cellular regulator pRb, the transcriptional regulators it influences and the resulting change in cell-cycle behavior. E7 is neither an E2F transcription factor nor the enzyme that synthesizes DNA. Its cancer-relevant effect is mediated through a disturbance of normal cellular regulation.

An instructive contrast is the expression of p16, a cellular CDK inhibitor, in many HPV-associated lesions. Increased p16 expression can reflect feedback arising from disruption of Rb-mediated control; it is not necessarily evidence that the affected cell has restored effective growth arrest. The biological meaning of p16 depends on the state of the downstream pathway. In a cell whose pRb function is compromised, strong expression of an upstream inhibitor may be accompanied by continued abnormal growth. In addition, p16 expression is not a universal or perfectly specific indicator of HPV causation; its interpretation depends on the disease setting and the accompanying evidence.

Because HPV biology is tied to epithelial differentiation, cell behavior must be interpreted within tissue structure. Basal epithelial cells are ordinarily the renewing population; differentiation changes the availability of cellular functions as cells move toward the surface. The growth-control effects of viral genes are related to the virus's dependence on host-cell functions, but malignant transformation disrupts normal tissue organization. A productive viral infection and a malignant epithelial tumor are distinct biological states. In particular, cancer-associated viral gene expression can persist even when production of complete infectious progeny is limited. This is why counting virus particles is not a substitute for assessing tumor biology.

The epithelial context of HPV-associated oncogenesis provides a biological reason for examining the relationship between differentiation and cell-cycle entry. Normal epithelial cells undergo regulated transitions as they move away from the proliferative compartment. In differentiated cells, continued synthesis of host DNA is ordinarily restricted. Viral genome maintenance or expression in such a tissue can be connected with alteration of host processes that determine whether cells remain competent for DNA synthesis. The cancer-relevant consequence of persistent interference is a disturbance of the normal relationship between a cell's location, differentiation and proliferative behavior. The complete life cycle and entry process were treated in the host–pathogen subunit; the present focus is the effect on growth control.

The E6 protein of high-risk HPV provides an example of a molecular interaction that changes the fate of another protein. In the classical model, E6 participates in a complex involving the cellular E3 ubiquitin ligase E6AP, also called UBE3A, and p53. The resulting interaction can promote ubiquitination and degradation of p53. E6 should not be described as a protease that directly hydrolyzes p53, nor as an enzyme that removes the TP53 gene. The cellular protein-turnover machinery performs the relevant reactions. This distinction matters because a molecular intervention that alters degradation can change protein abundance without changing the sequence or transcription of the corresponding host gene.

The role of E6AP is an example of altered substrate selection by a regulatory interaction. An E3 ubiquitin ligase normally contributes to recognition of substrates in the ubiquitin-transfer system. The presence of a viral protein can change the interaction context in which a host protein becomes subject to this pathway. Attachment of ubiquitin and proteasomal processing are separate processes, each with its own molecular requirements. A finding of reduced p53 abundance is consistent with enhanced turnover but does not alone demonstrate the participation of every component in the proposed chain. Evidence about association, ubiquitination and degradation helps distinguish the more specific mechanism from other causes of low p53 abundance.

The energy required for ubiquitin-dependent protein turnover also illustrates the use of cellular resources by a viral regulatory influence. Activation of ubiquitin uses ATP, and proteasomal processing includes ATP-dependent activities. A virus-associated alteration of substrate recognition is therefore not a source of energy for degradation. It changes how existing cellular machinery is directed toward a particular target. This is a useful conceptual bridge from the earlier chapters on protein chemistry: the regulatory effect of a binding interaction is distinct from the chemical work performed by the enzymes and complexes that carry out subsequent reactions.

The high-risk HPV E7 protein supplies a complementary example. Its interaction with pRB involves a conserved binding motif commonly described as LXCXE, where X represents a variable amino acid. The motif is a feature of the protein sequence that contributes to recognition of a host interaction surface. Interference with pRB-mediated restraint and, in relevant contexts, reduced pRB stability can change the availability of activating E2F-associated transcription. The exact effect depends on the viral type, target proteins and cellular context. The useful examination distinction is that E7 affects the pRB-associated growth restraint, whereas E6 is classically associated with reduced p53 availability through E6AP-dependent turnover.

Because both viral proteins affect host regulators, they can produce complementary rather than identical consequences. E7-associated interference can favor inappropriate progression toward DNA synthesis. E6-associated reduction of p53 can weaken a response that might otherwise restrain damaged or abnormally proliferating cells. The presence of E7 without a corresponding reduction in protective responses need not produce the same population outcome as the combined influences. The conceptual relationship is cooperation between changes affecting different barriers. It should not be treated as a guarantee that expression of these two proteins alone establishes invasion, metastasis or every other cancer-associated capability.

The expression of viral early genes can depend on the regulatory state of the viral genome. E2 participates in regulation of viral transcription and maintenance of the genome in its appropriate context. In some HPV-associated cancers, integration disrupts normal E2-associated regulation and is linked with increased or persistent E6/E7 expression. An analysis of invasive cervical cancers with purely episomal HPV16 reported expression of E6/E7 transcripts despite the absence of viral integration. However, integration patterns are heterogeneous, and some tumors retain episomal viral DNA or mixed forms. The mechanistic conclusion must therefore be phrased in terms of an altered expression program rather than an absolute requirement for one physical arrangement of viral sequences. This is a common exception tested by questions comparing persistence and integration.

High-risk and low-risk HPV types should be distinguished by their documented association with cancer rather than by assuming that all members of the group behave identically. Some types are commonly associated with benign lesions, whereas persistent infection with particular high-risk types is an established cause of several cancers. The fact that related viruses encode similarly named proteins does not establish equal functional consequences of those proteins. Differences in their interactions, expression and cellular context contribute to distinct outcomes. Students should not infer a cancer-associated phenotype solely from the presence of a gene name without specifying the viral type and the relevant evidence.

The anatomical distribution of HPV-associated cancers is another reminder that a molecular mechanism operates within a tissue. Cervical epithelium provides a well-studied context, but high-risk HPV also contributes to cancers at other anogenital and oropharyngeal sites. The fact that a regulatory mechanism can be relevant in multiple epithelial contexts does not establish that all tumors at those anatomical sites are HPV-associated. A tumor at a particular site can arise through different molecular histories. Appropriate classification requires evidence about the relationship between the virus and the particular tumor, not merely knowledge that infection-related cancers sometimes arise at that location.

A persistent precursor lesion and an invasive cancer are also distinct outcomes. In a precursor lesion, abnormal regulation can disturb the organization and maturation of the epithelium. Invasion requires an additional change in the relationship of the abnormal population to surrounding tissue boundaries. A viral protein that interferes with cell-cycle restraint does not automatically explain how a lineage acquires the properties needed to grow beyond those boundaries. Additional cellular changes and environmental interactions can contribute during progression. The later invasion and metastasis chapters develop these properties explicitly, which prevents the current explanation from assigning every stage of malignancy to the original viral interaction.

The distinction between the presence of a viral sequence and continued biological dependence can be illustrated by a hypothetical comparison. Suppose a cancer population contains viral DNA and shows reduced p53-associated output. A second observation establishes expression of E6 within the affected cells. Those findings strengthen the connection between the viral sequence and the molecular phenotype but do not exclude unrelated changes in the host pathway. Evidence showing that the expected host response depends on the relevant viral activity would be more directly informative about functional maintenance. Even such a result would need to be interpreted in relation to the defined cell population and measured outcome.

The classic HPV model is valuable because it makes the relationship between viral and nonviral cancer mechanisms explicit. The same host pathways can be disrupted by genetic alterations in a tumor without HPV, or by regulatory interference in an HPV-associated context. The convergence does not imply that the two tumors have identical genotypes, expression profiles or histories. It shows why the functional categories developed in the earlier cancer chapters remain useful when a new source of perturbation is introduced. Students who can trace the effect of E6 and E7 through p53 and pRB should be able to interpret unfamiliar examples without memorizing every possible combination of viral and host genes.

6. EBV and the lymphocyte context

Epstein–Barr virus is a DNA herpesvirus that can persist in the host. Its association with several malignancies includes Burkitt lymphoma, certain Hodgkin lymphomas, nasopharyngeal carcinoma and lymphoproliferative disease in settings of impaired immune control. These malignancies differ in cell lineage and viral gene-expression patterns, so a mechanism described for one cannot automatically be applied to all EBV-associated tumors. In particular, a B-cell signaling model does not completely explain an epithelial tumor in the nasopharynx.

A normal B lymphocyte integrates signals that regulate activation, expansion and differentiation. A transient increase in proliferation during an immune response is beneficial, but its duration is normally constrained by antigen availability, interactions with other cells and the eventual contraction of an expanded population. Persistent viral effects on these signals can change the relationship between external cues and intracellular growth or survival. This is a different cellular starting point from the stratified epithelium in the HPV example, even though the broad concepts of growth regulation and apoptosis remain relevant.

In some EBV latency programs, the viral protein LMP1 is associated with signaling resembling aspects of the normal CD40 pathway. NF-κB-dependent transcription is one important downstream feature. EBNA2 is a viral nuclear regulator associated with changes in transcription in appropriate latency contexts. These are examples of how viral gene expression can influence cellular signaling and transcription, not a universal description of all EBV-positive cancers. The term latency does not imply that every viral gene is inactive; different latency programs retain different subsets of gene expression.

The distinction is particularly relevant to Burkitt lymphoma, in which rearrangement involving MYC is a hallmark of the cancer. Not all Burkitt lymphomas contain EBV, and many EBV-positive Burkitt lymphomas exhibit restricted viral latency gene expression. A simple claim that persistent expression of LMP1 explains every EBV-associated lymphoma is therefore incorrect. Instead, EBV status and MYC rearrangement are separate biological observations. Their association with the same disease in some populations illustrates how a viral contribution and host-genomic alteration can coexist in one malignant lineage.

Immune control helps explain why an infection and a malignant outcome can be separated by a long period. Recognition of infected cells by the immune system limits the expansion of many abnormal cell populations. A reduction in effective immune surveillance can alter the balance between expansion and removal, but it does not establish that a particular viral gene directly caused a mutation. The distinction between immune-mediated control of infection and the intrinsic growth properties of a cancer cell will recur in the chapter on the tumor microenvironment.

The B-cell example illustrates the importance of distinguishing a normal survival signal from a persistent imitation of that signal. Normal B lymphocytes receive information through their antigen receptors and through interactions with other immune cells. These interactions influence whether a B cell survives, expands or differentiates. The response is ordinarily linked to the availability of the relevant physiological signals and the stage of the cell's development. In certain EBV latency programs, viral proteins can influence signaling pathways related to those normal processes. The resulting effect may favor persistence of infected cells or expansion of their descendants. The important comparison is not between a virus and a complete immune cell, but between a normal regulatory input and the output associated with the viral product.

Latent membrane protein 1, usually abbreviated LMP1, is a useful example of this signaling principle. Its activity in appropriate EBV-associated contexts can resemble aspects of signaling initiated by CD40, a receptor involved in normal B-cell interactions. LMP1 is a viral membrane protein rather than the physiological extracellular ligand for CD40. It can engage host signaling components and influence transcriptional outputs, including those associated with NF-kappaB. The important cancer-relevant consequence is that a pathway supporting survival or activation can be influenced in a context different from the ordinary receptor–ligand relationship. An observed change in NF-kappaB-associated transcription alone does not establish the presence of LMP1, because other regulatory inputs can also affect that pathway.

LMP2A provides another example of a viral influence related to normal lymphocyte signaling. In relevant cellular contexts, it can support signaling functions resembling aspects of B-cell receptor-associated survival signals. This does not make LMP2A an antibody or establish that every function of the B-cell receptor is reproduced. The distinction is between a molecular source of signaling and selected downstream consequences of that signaling. A B-cell lineage can possess intact host signaling proteins while the conditions controlling their activity have changed. As with HPV interference with host suppressors, the viral contribution can therefore be understood through the normal host pathway rather than as a wholly separate set of cellular processes.

EBNA2 illustrates a different regulatory level. It is a viral nuclear protein that can influence transcription through association with host regulatory proteins. Its effects in the EBV growth program contribute to changes in the expression of viral and cellular genes associated with B-cell activation and proliferation. This is not a claim that EBNA2 directly binds every DNA sequence whose expression changes. A transcriptional regulator can influence many targets through other proteins and through indirect changes in cellular state. The contrast between EBNA2 and a membrane-associated viral protein helps organize the EBV example by molecular location: a signal can be modified near the membrane, while gene expression can also be altered through regulatory interactions in the nucleus.

The EBV latency programs are especially important for avoiding a misleading generalization from a single protein to all EBV-associated cancers. The commonly taught latency III growth program includes a relatively broad set of latent gene products. More restricted patterns include latency II, in which selected nuclear and membrane products are expressed, and latency I, classically associated with expression of EBNA1 and noncoding viral RNAs. These categories summarize characteristic expression patterns, and actual tumors can vary. The fact that LMP1 participates in growth-promoting signaling in one context does not establish that every EBV-positive lymphoma expresses it. Students must identify the relevant latency program before assigning a particular mechanism to a disease.

EBNA1 contributes to maintenance of the viral episomal genome in appropriate latent contexts. This role provides an instructive contrast with the activities of proteins that directly influence growth-associated signaling. Retention of genetic information is a prerequisite for continued expression of that information, but maintenance of the genome is not the same as direct stimulation of S-phase entry. A protein contributing to persistence can be important to a long-term biological association even if its principal role differs from that of a growth-promoting signal. In interpreting EBV-associated cancers, it is useful to distinguish the machinery supporting continued presence of the virus from the particular expression products that influence the host phenotype.

Burkitt lymphoma illustrates the cooperation between viral association and host genetic changes. A characteristic feature is dysregulation of MYC, often through a chromosomal rearrangement placing it in an immunoglobulin-associated regulatory context. EBV association varies among the epidemiological forms of the disease, and many EBV-positive Burkitt lymphomas show a restricted latency program. It is therefore incorrect to explain every case by continuous expression of the entire EBV growth program. The MYC-associated alteration and viral context represent different dimensions of the tumor's history. This example connects directly with 4.E.02 and 4.E.03, where host rearrangements and activation of growth-promoting genes were considered.

The comparison between EBV-associated lymphoid and epithelial malignancies reinforces the same principle. A viral genome can be associated with tumors of different cellular origins, but the meaning of a particular signaling interaction depends on which host regulatory machinery is present and how the relevant viral genes are expressed. A pathway described in a B lymphocyte should not automatically be assigned an identical role in an epithelial cell. Similarly, a virus-associated tumor need not show the same expression pattern as a proliferating infected B-cell culture. Observations in a defined cellular model establish possibilities and dependencies in that model; the state of a particular tumor requires corresponding evidence from its own biological context.

Immune surveillance influences the persistence of cells expressing viral products. A host population can contain infected cells that are ordinarily restricted by immune responses. When the capacity to control such populations is impaired, the relative opportunity for expansion can change. This helps explain the occurrence of some EBV-associated lymphoproliferative disorders in contexts of impaired immune control. The observation does not mean that loss of immune control causes every EBV-associated cancer or that infection alone is sufficient. It shows that the net expansion of an infected lineage depends partly on the relationship between its survival-promoting influences and the organism's capacity to restrict abnormal cells.

7. HBV, HCV and the chronic-injury route

The liver provides a useful contrast with the preceding examples. Hepatocytes normally retain the capacity to participate in tissue repair after injury. This regenerative capacity is necessary to preserve the many metabolic and synthetic functions of the organ. Persistent liver injury, however, can create repeated cycles of cell loss, inflammatory signaling and compensatory proliferation. A growth response that is useful after a brief injury can become part of a disease process when injury does not resolve.

Chronic hepatitis B and hepatitis C increase the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma. In both cases, persistent infection can lead to inflammation, tissue damage, fibrosis and sometimes cirrhosis. Fibrosis refers to excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix in response to injury. Cirrhosis involves advanced disruption of normal liver architecture. These tissue changes are relevant because a liver cancer can emerge from a cellular lineage developing within a chronically altered environment rather than from a single immediate change in a previously healthy isolated cell.

Inflammation involves signals and cellular activities that normally help remove harmful stimuli and promote repair. In chronic injury, repeated exposure to inflammatory mediators and reactive chemical species can contribute to cellular stress and DNA damage. Compensatory proliferation can expand surviving lineages and provide opportunities for changes to become established in their descendants. The resulting disease is not simply a consequence of an increased rate of cell division; damage, repair, death, cellular survival and selection all contribute. The process also links molecular events with the architecture and physiological condition of an organ.

HBV and HCV differ in genome organization. HBV infection can involve viral DNA integration, and HBV-related carcinogenesis may include direct effects associated with viral proteins and integration in addition to chronic injury. Hepatocellular carcinoma associated with HBV may occur without established cirrhosis. These facts make it misleading to claim that cirrhosis is an obligatory intermediate in every hepatitis-associated cancer. The relative importance of inflammation, viral genome alterations and host changes varies among cases.

HCV has an RNA genome and does not ordinarily integrate into the host genome. Its cancer association is strongly linked to long-standing liver disease and the tissue effects of chronic inflammation. Viral protein interactions with host pathways may also contribute, but the absence of a DNA integration requirement is an essential mechanistic contrast with HBV. A student should be able to explain how a chronic RNA-virus infection can contribute to cancer without ever invoking insertion of viral DNA into a human chromosome.

The course of hepatitis-associated disease depends on numerous factors beyond infection status, including the duration and severity of liver injury and other causes of liver disease. Successful treatment or suppression of infection can lower risk, but an already damaged liver may retain residual risk. This distinction makes sense when the cancer-producing process is understood as the cumulative evolution of tissue and cell lineages rather than as the immediate presence of a virus. The preventive implication is not that every past exposure produces malignancy, but that limiting chronic injury and preventing infections can reduce opportunities for disease to develop.

The hepatitis-associated examples are particularly useful because they connect cancer causation with tissue-level physiology. The liver performs metabolic, synthetic and detoxification functions and can respond to loss of functional tissue through regenerative processes. During a persistent injury, the balance between damage and restoration can repeatedly shift. A cell that participates in replacement is not necessarily transformed; its activity may be an appropriate response to the loss of surrounding cells. However, repeated cycles of loss and replacement create opportunities for altered descendants to arise or expand. This provides a biological bridge between a long-lasting tissue disturbance and selection within a host cell population, without requiring every regenerating cell to be malignant.

Inflammation can alter the environment in which a cell experiences stress and receives growth-regulatory information. Signals produced by responding cells can affect the survival and proliferation of nearby populations. The presence of inflammation is therefore not merely an incidental observation in a chronically injured organ. Its relevance depends on how it changes the conditions under which affected lineages persist and divide. At the same time, inflammation is a normal protective response in many settings and is not equivalent to cancer. The distinction concerns persistence, tissue context, accumulated cellular changes and the consequences of repeated injury, rather than the presence of any single inflammatory signal.

Fibrosis describes accumulation and remodeling of extracellular material in response to injury. In chronic liver disease, extensive changes in tissue architecture can occur over time, and cirrhosis represents a major alteration of the organ's structure. These tissue changes influence the environment in which hepatocytes survive and regenerate. Their association with liver cancer does not establish that every case must pass through an identical sequence of stages. In particular, HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma can occur without established cirrhosis. The distinction is informative for comparing routes of carcinogenesis: a pathway involving chronic injury and altered tissue architecture can be important without being the only possible pathway.

HBV provides a contrast between maintenance of viral genetic information and integration into host chromosomes. Its nuclear covalently closed circular DNA, commonly abbreviated cccDNA, acts as a transcriptional reservoir in the viral life cycle. Integrated HBV DNA is a different physical entity. The presence of integrated sequences can have consequences for host chromosomal organization or gene regulation, but those sequences are not simply identical to the nuclear reservoir supporting the ordinary viral replication cycle. The distinction is especially important in cancer interpretation because a measure of one physical form cannot be automatically assigned to the other. This is a brief prerequisite distinction; the detailed replication cycle belongs to the host–pathogen material.

The effects of HBV integration depend on the location and properties of the retained sequences. A chromosomal event can affect the regulatory environment of a host gene, alter the structure of a region or provide a marker of a cellular lineage. Detection of such an event in a tumor is therefore biologically informative, but not every integration is necessarily a driver. A recurrent association with a particular functional change is stronger evidence of contribution than mere occurrence of an integrated fragment. This reasoning is analogous to the distinction between driver and passenger somatic alterations in earlier cancer chapters. The virus-associated event must be interpreted in relation to its consequence rather than classified solely by its origin.

HBx is a viral regulatory protein that has been linked to effects on host gene expression and signaling in HBV-associated carcinogenesis. These observations illustrate that the HBV example is not reducible solely to tissue injury. However, the effect of HBx and other viral products depends on biological context, and their presence does not establish a single universal causal chain in every HBV-associated tumor. A student should distinguish the possibility of direct effects on host cellular regulation from the independent contribution of chronic injury and regeneration. The combination of influences can help explain why two infected lineages or two affected individuals do not necessarily follow the same progression history.

HCV differs from HBV in its genome organization and ordinary replication strategy. It is an RNA virus without an obligatory chromosomal DNA integration stage in its normal life cycle. Its association with hepatocellular carcinoma therefore demonstrates that integration of a viral DNA genome is not required for infection-associated carcinogenesis. Persistent infection and associated liver injury provide a major route through which the cellular environment can change over time. Viral products can also affect host cellular processes, so chronic injury should not be interpreted as proof that the virus has no direct molecular interactions with the affected cells. The useful comparative point is the difference in genome context and the relative contribution of the major mechanisms.

The contrast between HBV and HCV can be framed as a question about what evidence would discriminate mechanisms. A finding of a viral DNA–host chromosome junction is relevant to a physical integration event in the HBV example. Evidence of persistent RNA virus infection together with recurrent injury and altered tissue architecture addresses a different history in the HCV example. Neither observation alone establishes the complete sequence of changes in a malignant lineage. The biological interpretation becomes stronger when the infection history, tissue context and evidence of host-cell alterations are considered together. The student should not infer a shared molecular mechanism simply because both infections are associated with cancers arising in the same organ.

The persistence of cancer risk after an infection-associated disturbance has been reduced illustrates why a multistep process has a history. Earlier tissue damage, altered architecture or accumulated cellular alterations may remain even when the initiating infectious influence has changed. The removal of one source of stress does not logically require that all earlier consequences immediately disappear. Conversely, reduction of an important causal influence can reduce the opportunity for subsequent injury or progression. These ideas are consistent rather than contradictory. They distinguish the ongoing contribution of infection from the consequences already established within the tissue, without attempting to make clinical predictions about a particular individual.

8. Comparative reasoning, selection and boundaries

The examples can now be organized around the affected biological process rather than memorized as a list of viruses. High-risk HPV is a classic illustration of direct interference with host tumor-suppressor pathways. EBV demonstrates the importance of cellular lineage and latency-associated signaling. Chronic hepatitis illustrates the consequences of tissue injury and regeneration, with additional mechanisms relevant to HBV. The different routes converge at the level of abnormal population behavior: cells acquire or retain altered growth and survival properties in a specific tissue context.

A tumor population expands when cell production and loss are not balanced over time. Increased proliferation, reduced death and altered differentiation can each contribute to this imbalance. A measurement that shows more cells in S phase does not reveal whether the eventual increase in cell number is due to faster division, diminished cell loss or both. Similarly, a large tissue lesion is not necessarily malignant; invasion and tissue organization must be assessed independently. These distinctions apply whether the original perturbation is a viral protein, a host-genomic alteration or a long-standing tissue disturbance.

Clonal selection describes changes in the relative abundance of lineages that differ in their properties. It is not a purposeful process and does not mean that a virus intends to generate a cancer. A lineage that survives and divides more effectively under a particular tissue condition may become more abundant, while other lineages disappear. Its descendants inherit some of its molecular features, but their environment and additional changes can influence future behavior. Cancer progression is thus a population process as well as an intracellular process. In many infections malignancy is an uncommon outcome that offers no obvious advantage to the virus.

The term cancer susceptibility refers to probability, not inevitability. A viral exposure may increase risk relative to an unexposed population while the absolute probability remains low. For example, a twofold relative risk has a different practical meaning when the background risk is one per thousand than when it is one per ten. The duration of follow-up also matters, because an observed proportion depends on how long outcomes are counted. Neither a risk ratio nor the proportion of tumors testing positive for a virus can be interpreted without specifying the population and outcome.

Virus-associated cancer is not normally transmitted between people as a cancer. A transmissible agent can contribute to the development of cancer in a different host, but the malignant cellular clone usually arises in the affected person's own tissue. This is an important distinction between infectious disease and transmissible tumor cells. It also explains why vaccines that prevent certain viral infections can reduce cancer incidence without acting directly as vaccines against all tumor cells.

A final comparison concerns causality and necessity. An infection may be necessary for a specifically defined virus-associated cancer subtype but not sufficient to produce that cancer in every infected person. A host alteration may arise independently in both virus-associated and virus-independent tumors. The absence of viral material from one tumor cannot establish the absence of a viral contribution to every tumor of the same anatomical type. The correct generalization depends on how the disease category is defined and on the quality of the available evidence.

A useful way to compare viral contributions to cancer is to separate three variables: the cellular target, the retained source of viral influence and the host process affected. In the HPV example, epithelial growth control is influenced through viral products associated with p53 and pRB. In EBV-associated contexts, a persistent viral genome and its restricted expression programs can influence cell-type-specific survival or activation. The classification identifies prominent explanatory relationships rather than placing each virus in a mutually exclusive category. This is why the same tumor can contain evidence relevant to more than one mechanism.

Animal tumor-virus studies supplied an important historical framework for the distinction between viral and cellular oncogenes. The 1976 report by Stehelin and colleagues identified DNA related to the avian sarcoma virus transforming gene in normal avian DNA, helping establish the cellular origin of a viral oncogene. This shifted the explanation of cancer away from the idea that growth-promoting genetic information must always originate outside the host. A normal cellular proto-oncogene can participate in appropriate regulation, while altered expression or activity can contribute to transformation. The historical comparison complements the HPV example, in which a viral product acts on a host suppressor rather than simply reproducing the same mechanism as the classical viral oncogene. The broader significance is convergence on normal cellular growth-regulatory systems.

Cooperation between viral influence and host alteration is better understood as a relationship among cellular barriers than as a fixed number of causal events. A virus-associated signal may favor entry into a proliferative state, while a host alteration affects the ability of a cell to survive a stress. A chronic tissue disturbance may repeatedly favor regenerative activity, while a lineage-specific genetic change reduces the restraint ordinarily imposed on that activity. These combinations can change the future reproductive contribution of a lineage. Their importance depends on the existing regulatory background. A claim that a particular combination is present in all tumors would require evidence beyond the conceptual observation that the changes can cooperate.

Selection can operate at several levels in an infected tissue. The infected cell population can change in composition if some descendants persist more effectively than others. The virus-associated expression state can also differ among cells. When a lesion is sampled, the most abundant cellular population may represent the outcome of earlier selection rather than the initial infection state. This distinction matters when using a tumor specimen to reconstruct causation. A property observed in the final population may have arisen after an earlier viral contribution, while another property may have been retained from an early stage. The final state alone cannot uniquely establish the order of all preceding events.

Comparison with cancer-associated infections that act indirectly through immune suppression offers a further conceptual boundary. HIV-associated impairment of immune function can increase susceptibility to certain cancers, including cancers linked to other infectious agents. This does not make HIV the source of the classic HPV E6 and E7 proteins or mean that every affected malignant cell is directly transformed by HIV. The relevant mechanism can operate at the organismal level by altering control of infected or abnormal populations. Students should distinguish the infectious agent acting directly within a malignant lineage from an infection that changes the conditions under which another oncogenic influence operates.

9. Reading biological evidence

Consider a report that detects viral DNA in a collection of tumor specimens. The most direct supported claim is that viral DNA was detected in the samples under the stated detection conditions. A more specific claim, that the malignant cells themselves contain the virus, requires evidence localizing the signal to those cells. An even stronger claim, that the viral genes contribute to the malignant phenotype, requires evidence connecting expression and cellular function. A simple positive-versus-negative test cannot supply all three conclusions at once.

Histological localization is important because a tumor is a mixture of malignant cells and nonmalignant cells, including lymphocytes and stromal cells. A nucleic acid signal from a sample containing all those cell types may reflect infection of only one population. Conversely, an apparent absence of signal may reflect limitations of sensitivity or sampling. Interpretation should therefore consider the nature of the specimen, the target being measured and the meaning of the negative control. These principles are applicable to many studies beyond cancer virology and will be expanded in the Methods in Biology unit.

Evidence about protein abundance and evidence about activity can also be dissociated. A hypothetical tumor profile might show normal TP53 RNA, low p53 protein and diminished expression of a p53-responsive gene. Such observations are consistent with post-transcriptional loss of p53 function but do not identify a single cause without other evidence. A different profile with abundant p53 protein and poor target-gene expression could indicate inactive protein. In neither case can the exact underlying genotype be inferred from a protein measurement alone. The student's task is to identify the conclusions directly supported by the data before considering explanations.

Association studies must account for the possibility that different groups have different baseline risks. Age, duration of follow-up, underlying tissue disease and other exposures may influence the observed proportions. Comparing an infection-positive group with an infection-negative group does not automatically isolate the effect of infection. Evidence from independent study designs, molecular findings and prevention studies can strengthen the causal inference when they converge. Random assignment to harmful infections would be unethical, so human causal evidence depends on appropriate observational and preventive research rather than deliberate infection.

The distinction between direct and indirect cancer mechanisms is a useful organizing tool rather than an absolute partition. Viral products can influence cellular processes while an inflammatory or immune context influences whether affected cells persist. Chronic injury can affect cells that do not contain detectable viral material. A biopsy collected at one time reflects the current condition of the lesion, whereas causal events may have occurred earlier in its history. Interpretation of a negative viral test is consequently different for an ongoing viral-protein-dependent mechanism than for a disease mechanism involving cumulative injury.

Preventive evidence illustrates another important inferential boundary. HPV and HBV vaccination can reduce the risk of their associated cancers by preventing relevant infections and their long-term consequences. HCV treatment can reduce future liver disease risk, and management of HBV can reduce disease burden. None of these observations implies that a preventive vaccine is a universal therapy for established malignant clones. Prevention acts at an earlier stage of a causal sequence, while cancer treatment addresses the properties of a tumor that already exists. Screening, prevention and therapy consequently answer different biological questions.

A strong causal argument for virus-associated oncogenesis requires the convergence of evidence from different levels. A prospective comparison can establish that the relevant infection-associated state was present before the cancer outcome. Localization of viral material in affected cells can connect the association to a specific tissue population. Evidence of expression can identify whether relevant viral products are present. Biochemical evidence can demonstrate an interaction with a host regulatory component, and functional observations can link the interaction to a cellular phenotype. These forms of evidence are complementary because they answer distinct questions. No single measurement automatically establishes all the links from infection to invasion or metastasis.

The classic HPV biochemical studies illustrate the difference between association and turnover. An observation that E7 and pRB occur in an associated complex supports a physical relationship under the stated conditions. A separate observation that E6 promotes degradation of p53 supports a change in the fate of a host protein. These observations concern different molecular targets and effects. A functional comparison of downstream transcriptional responses can help establish whether the relevant host restraint is impaired. The existence of a protein complex alone does not establish the magnitude of that impairment in every cellular background. Likewise, reduced abundance alone is not evidence that every downstream pathway has been removed.

A hypothetical comparison can demonstrate the importance of distinguishing protein degradation from reduced synthesis. Suppose two cell populations have comparable amounts of TP53 messenger RNA, but one has less p53 protein and a shorter measured protein lifetime. Under the assumptions of the comparison, increased turnover is consistent with the observations. If the same population also shows reduced induction of a p53-responsive target after stress, the findings connect a change in protein availability to a functional output. However, these observations alone do not identify the viral protein or host ligase responsible. Evidence about the relevant interaction would be required to narrow the mechanistic explanation.

Localization is particularly important in lymphoid and epithelial specimens. A tissue can contain malignant cells, infected nonmalignant cells, immune cells and other components. Detection of viral nucleic acid from the entire specimen does not establish which cells contributed it. If a cell-resolved observation identifies a viral expression product within the malignant population, the evidence addresses a more specific association. Even this result must be interpreted in relation to the type of viral product and the relevant disease. Detection of a persistent viral genome is distinct from detection of a particular oncoprotein, and the absence of one expression product does not establish absence of all viral activity.

A longitudinal comparison also has advantages over a cross-sectional comparison when studying persistent infection. A single observation can show that an individual has a detectable marker at one time. Repeated observations can support persistence over the interval studied, subject to the properties of the measurement and the possibility of intervening changes. Comparing subsequent outcomes among populations with different infection histories can help distinguish the relevance of persistence from that of a single detected exposure. The interpretation must account for differences in the groups, since other factors can affect both infection history and cancer risk. This is the reason risk estimates are not substitutes for molecular evidence, even when the association is strong.

10. Quantitative interpretation of cancer risk

A simple absolute risk is a proportion of a defined at-risk population that develops an outcome within a specified interval. If n individuals experience an outcome among N initially at-risk individuals who are completely followed over the interval, the observed cumulative risk is n divided by N. Both quantities are counts, so the risk is dimensionless. Multiplying by 100 expresses it as a percentage. This model assumes that the population and time interval are defined and that ascertainment of the outcome is adequate. If follow-up is unequal or substantial numbers are lost, the simple fraction can be misleading.

The risk ratio compares the risk in one group with the risk in another. If R1 and R0 are risks defined over equivalent intervals, RR equals R1 divided by R0. A value of one indicates equality of the observed risks. A value above one indicates greater observed risk in group one, and a value below one indicates lower risk in group one. This is a ratio of two probabilities, not a percentage of individuals who become ill. It does not by itself quantify the difference in absolute numbers or establish that a particular exposure caused the observed difference.

The absolute risk difference is R1 minus R0. A change from 0.001 to 0.004 yields a risk ratio of four and a risk difference of 0.003, or 0.3 percentage points. The two statistics describe the same hypothetical observations from different perspectives. The ratio highlights relative contrast, whereas the difference describes the observed increase in outcomes per population at risk over the stated interval. The ratio is undefined when the comparison risk is zero, but the difference can still be calculated. These examples do not represent measured risks for any actual virus.

Students must also distinguish cumulative risk from an incidence rate. An incidence rate has units of cases per unit of person-time, such as cases per 100,000 person-years. It accounts for the duration of observation by summing person-time at risk. It is not directly interchangeable with a probability, especially across long intervals or when risk changes over time. A rate of a certain number per person-year does not automatically give the same numerical percentage of people who will develop cancer in a year without further assumptions.

An additional statistic commonly encountered in interpretation of test data is the proportion of tumors with a particular characteristic. If 80 of 100 sampled tumors test positive for a viral marker, the observed marker prevalence among those tumors is 80 percent. It does not mean 80 percent of virus-positive people develop tumors. The denominators are fundamentally different: the first calculation conditions on having a tumor, while the second would condition on infection status. Confusion of these conditional probabilities is a common examination error and can produce implausible interpretations of common infections.

A numerical answer should always be accompanied by a biological statement. The interpretation of a relative risk is different from the interpretation of a change in cellular protein abundance, and neither statistic alone specifies a molecular mechanism. For examination purposes, use risk arithmetic to distinguish population outcomes, and use molecular and tissue evidence to distinguish mechanisms. In a realistic scientific argument these forms of information support one another, but they cannot substitute for one another.

Consider an idealized sequence in which an infected population can either clear an infection or retain it, and a persistent infection can be followed by a specified precursor outcome. If a fraction p of the initially infected group remains persistently infected and a fraction q of that persistent group experiences the precursor outcome during a defined follow-up, the fraction of the original group passing through both states is pq under the stated conditional definition. The calculation does not require that p and q be statistically independent, because q is defined within the group reaching the second state. It does require that the denominators and observation intervals correspond. This distinction helps students avoid multiplying two unrelated percentages drawn from different populations.

The same reasoning can be extended to a third conditional outcome. Suppose a fraction r of individuals in the specified precursor group progresses to an invasive outcome within the stated interval. The fraction of the original infected group following that defined sequence is pqr, provided the populations and time windows are compatible and the events are nested as specified. The result is not a universal cancer risk because other pathways, changing rates, losses to follow-up and differences in individual circumstances can alter the observed outcome. Its educational value is to show how a large initial population can yield a much smaller final group without contradicting the causal importance of infection.

The difference between risk and incidence rate is also useful for interpreting historical cohort evidence. A cumulative risk is a proportion of an initially at-risk population experiencing an outcome during a specified interval. An incidence rate relates the number of events to the total observed time contributed by at-risk individuals. If observation times vary, these two quantities cannot generally be equated by simply using the initial number of individuals as the denominator. A rate reported per person-year has units of inverse time, whereas a cumulative risk is dimensionless. This distinction is particularly relevant when comparing cohorts followed for different durations or interpreting a disease that can arise after a long progression history.

The relationship between protein synthesis and removal can be expressed as a simple model to connect the HPV example to biophysical reasoning. Let P denote the concentration of a host protein, s its constant synthesis rate in concentration per time and k its first-order removal constant in inverse time. Then dP/dt = s − kP, with a steady state P = s/k. If the effective removal constant increases while synthesis remains fixed, the predicted steady-state abundance decreases. This model represents a possible consequence of altered protein turnover, not a complete representation of p53 signaling. Feedback, changes in expression and nonlinear molecular interactions can all modify the observed response.

11. Biological integration and prevention

Viral oncogenesis brings several parts of cell biology together. The HPV example links protein degradation to damage-responsive transcription and cell-cycle restraint. EBV links persistent gene expression to cell-type-specific signaling and immune surveillance. Chronic hepatitis links recurring tissue injury to regenerative proliferation, extracellular matrix changes and population selection. Each route can be described at a molecular level, but the biological consequences become clear only when the altered cell is placed in the tissue and organism that regulate its survival.

The preventive implications follow from these distinctions. Preventing an oncogenic infection removes or reduces an early contributor to disease. HPV vaccination and HBV vaccination provide examples of cancer prevention through infection prevention. Screening for cervical precancer addresses a different stage by detecting cellular changes before invasive cancer develops. Treatment of chronic viral hepatitis addresses ongoing infection and associated liver injury. These approaches operate at different points of the causal chain and are not interchangeable with treatment of an established malignancy.

The evolutionary perspective should be used cautiously. Viruses depend on host-cell functions, and host-cell regulation can restrict the conditions available for viral reproduction. Some viral activities affect those functions. The emergence of a malignant host-cell lineage is not evidence that natural selection favored the virus specifically to cause cancer. In particular, a tumor may not support productive infection. The distinction between viral fitness and the fitness of a host-cell lineage prevents an unsupported purposeful explanation of oncogenesis.

The following node turns to the tumor microenvironment. The hepatitis examples already show that altered tissue conditions can affect the fate of a cell lineage, and the EBV examples show why immune control matters. In the next chapter, those principles will be generalized to interactions between malignant cells and fibroblasts, immune populations, blood vessels and extracellular matrix. The present chapter does not require detailed treatment of angiogenesis, metastatic dissemination or cancer therapies. Its central objective is to explain how a virus-related disturbance can enter a multistep causal chain of cancer development, and how different kinds of evidence reveal different parts of that chain.

The rationale for prevention is strongest when the targeted influence lies upstream of several possible progression events. Prevention of infection with relevant HPV types or HBV can reduce opportunities for the corresponding infection-associated cancer pathways to begin. This does not imply that a preventive measure treats every existing cancer or removes all other cancer causes. The biological distinction concerns prevention of a causal exposure versus intervention after a malignant lineage has already formed. An intervention may also affect a particular intermediate endpoint without immediately changing the measured occurrence of invasive disease. This is why prevention studies must specify the outcome and follow-up interval rather than treating all endpoints as interchangeable.

The following chapter on the tumor microenvironment develops a connection already visible in the hepatitis and EBV examples. A cancer-associated phenotype can arise partly through a change within the affected cell and partly through interactions with other cells, tissue structures and signaling environments. The importance of these interactions does not diminish the molecular evidence for viral interference with p53 or pRB. It shows that an intracellular mechanism and the surrounding tissue can jointly determine whether an altered lineage persists. The progression from molecular cancer genetics to the microenvironment is therefore a progression from one level of explanation to a more integrated account, not a replacement of the earlier molecular model.

Worked analytical examples

1. Risk ratio and risk difference

Hypothetical groups of 10,000 people each have 40 and 10 events over equal follow-up. R1 = 40/10,000 = 0.004; R0 = 10/10,000 = 0.001. RR = 4; absolute difference = 0.003 or 0.3 percentage points. These are observed risks, not a causal estimate or measured risks for any actual virus.

2. Conditional probability

Among 100 tumor samples, 80 have a marker. Marker prevalence conditional on having a tumor is 80/100 = 80%. No denominator for risk among infected people is supplied. The proportion cannot be reversed.

3. Incidence rate

A hypothetical follow-up records 12 cases over 6,000 person-years. Rate = 12/6,000 = 0.002 cases per person-year, or 200 cases per 100,000 person-years. It is a rate, not an exact individual cumulative probability.

4. Disentangling RNA and protein

A hypothetical specimen has normal TP53 RNA, low p53 protein, and reduced p53-target transcription. The three observations support post-transcriptional dysfunction as a possibility. They do not establish the specific cause or prove viral causation.

5. Tissue localization

A whole-tissue viral signal is detected but a tissue-localization result assigns it to infiltrating lymphocytes. The whole-tissue result is compatible with infection of nonmalignant cells; it is insufficient to assign infection to the malignant lineage.

6. Relative versus absolute comparisons

Group A has 2/1,000 events and group B has 1/1,000 events, giving RR = 2 and risk difference = 0.1 percentage points. Group C has 200/1,000 and group D 100/1,000, also RR = 2 but difference = 10 percentage points. The same relative effect does not imply the same absolute burden.

7. Conditional transitions

In a hypothetical cohort of 5,000, 20% remain persistently infected and 5% of that subgroup reach a specified precursor outcome. The nested fraction is 0.20 × 0.05 = 0.01, or 50 of the original 5,000. This does not estimate real-world cancer risk.

8. Protein turnover

Under a constant synthesis rate of 8 nM/h, the model P = s/k gives 40 nM at k = 0.2 h⁻¹ and 10 nM at k = 0.8 h⁻¹. The fourfold increase in removal rate produces one-quarter of the original abundance under the model assumptions.

Revision and student mastery

Explain each relationship in your own words, then identify what evidence would support it and what it does not prove.

  1. Define infection, persistence, latency and malignancy independently.
  2. Distinguish productive infection from transformation.
  3. Explain why persistence can matter without integration.
  4. Compare episomal and integrated DNA.
  5. Describe the normal role of pRb.
  6. Explain the p53–p21 connection.

  9. Identify the classic E7-associated host pathway.

  11. Describe EBV latency at an examination level.

  13. Compare HBV and HCV genome contexts.

  15. Distinguish risk ratio, risk difference and marker prevalence.
