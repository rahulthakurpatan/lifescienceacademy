1. Why small molecules control large metabolic networks

A cell can possess a perfectly folded enzyme and an abundant substrate yet be unable to complete a reaction. Imagine a mitochondrion receiving glucose-derived pyruvate while its supply of thiamin diphosphate gradually declines. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex remains present, but the chemistry needed to remove carbon dioxide from pyruvate becomes inefficient. Carbon accumulates upstream, and the supply of acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents falls. A missing small molecule can thus change the behaviour of an entire network. This observation gives vitamins their proper place in biochemistry: they provide chemical capabilities that are not always available from the twenty common amino acid side chains.

A vitamin is an organic compound required in small amounts for normal biological function when an organism cannot synthesize an adequate supply. Essentiality is species-dependent: many animals make ascorbate, whereas humans lack functional L-gulonolactone oxidase. Human skin can produce vitamin D under suitable ultraviolet exposure, and tryptophan can contribute to niacin synthesis. These qualifications do not abolish the vitamin concept; they show why dietary requirements depend on endogenous production and circumstances. Minerals such as magnesium, iron and zinc are also required in small amounts, but their elemental nature distinguishes them from organic vitamins.

The classical discovery of vitamins began with feeding observations rather than with knowledge of enzymes. Diets containing purified carbohydrate, fat, protein and mineral salts failed to sustain normal growth unless additional food fractions were supplied. Observations linking polished rice with beriberi and fresh plant foods with the prevention of scurvy suggested that energy intake alone could not account for normal physiology. Isolation of the missing compounds and subsequent enzyme studies established specific chemical functions. The modern explanation of a deficiency therefore proceeds from the nutrient to its active molecular form, from the active form to a reaction, and from that reaction to a cellular or tissue consequence.

The body does not obtain appreciable metabolic energy by oxidizing vitamins as fuels. Instead, many B vitamins are converted to coenzymes that make the oxidation of fuels possible. Other vitamins function as antioxidants, hormone precursors, light-sensitive chromophores or cofactors for post-translational chemistry. We shall build the chapter by grouping these molecular tasks, rather than memorizing deficiency names first. The final sections reconnect those tasks to the pathways already established in Unit 1. By the end, a student should be able to predict the biochemical effect of an unavailable vitamin, distinguish competing explanations for a metabolite pattern, and trace the redistribution of fuel between organs.

2. From vitamin to coenzyme: chemical tools, not fuels

A cofactor is a nonprotein component necessary for a particular enzyme's activity. It may be a metal ion or an organic molecule. An organic cofactor is called a coenzyme; many, but not all, coenzymes are vitamin derivatives. The protein portion without its required cofactor is termed the apoenzyme, and its functional cofactor-containing form is the holoenzyme. These terms apply to enzymes that actually need a cofactor: a hydrolase that operates entirely through amino acid residues need not be assigned an apoenzyme–holoenzyme pair.

Coenzymes solve distinct chemical problems. An electron acceptor must accommodate changes in electronic structure; a group carrier must form a temporary covalent bond and later release its cargo; a decarboxylation catalyst must stabilize otherwise unfavourable carbon-centred intermediates. Conjugated aromatic rings, positively charged heterocycles, thiols and metal-containing rings are recurring solutions. They extend the active site's repertoire, while the protein determines substrate orientation, specificity and the local environment. The same coenzyme can therefore participate in quite different reactions without making the enzymes interchangeable.

A cosubstrate associates transiently and leaves the enzyme in a chemically changed form. NAD⁺ is an example: a dehydrogenase converts it to NADH, and a different reaction can restore NAD⁺. A prosthetic group is tightly associated with its protein, sometimes covalently; FAD in succinate dehydrogenase and biotin attached to a carboxylase illustrate this arrangement. The distinction concerns binding and regeneration, not a universal classification of each molecule: flavins may be bound with different strengths in different proteins. An enzyme-bound prosthetic group must return to its starting state before another catalytic turnover.

A useful accounting rule separates the immediate reaction from the entire coenzyme cycle. For lactate dehydrogenase, pyruvate + NADH + H⁺ ⇌ lactate + NAD⁺. NADH is consumed in the forward reaction, but no net NAD pool need be lost when glycolysis regenerates NADH from NAD⁺. By contrast, NAD⁺ used by certain ADP-ribosyltransferases is cleaved, and its products must be salvaged or replaced by synthesis. Thus a coenzyme is not necessarily unchanged in every individual reaction, and even a recyclable carrier eventually undergoes turnover.

Vitamins differ from their active forms. Riboflavin becomes FMN and FAD by enzyme-mediated modification; pyridoxine and related vitamers yield pyridoxal phosphate; folates must be reduced and enter a family of substituted tetrahydrofolates. A lack of an activating enzyme or a defect in transport can create a functional deficiency even when intake is adequate. Conversely, abundant active coenzyme cannot compensate for a missing apoenzyme. This distinction will be especially important when we interpret reconstitution experiments.

The traditional water-soluble group includes eight B vitamins and vitamin C; the fat-soluble group consists of A, D, E and K. Solubility is a useful guide to absorption and storage but not an absolute predictor of toxicity, retention or mode of action. Cobalamin, for example, has substantial hepatic reserves despite belonging to the water-soluble group. ATP, lipoamide and ubiquinone have important cofactor roles, but are not among the thirteen essential human vitamins. Lipoate is synthesized by human cells for protein lipoylation; ubiquinone is synthesized endogenously for electron transport. Do not infer that every organic electron carrier must come from the diet.

3. Absorption, activation, transport and turnover

The journey from food to enzyme begins with physical chemistry. Hydrophilic vitamins generally dissolve in intestinal contents and are absorbed through specific transporters or, in some circumstances, limited passive routes. Their phosphorylated or protein-bound forms often require hydrolysis or release before uptake. After intestinal entry, many travel in portal blood, enter tissues via transport proteins and are converted to charged coenzyme derivatives inside cells. Phosphorylation helps trap some metabolites within the cell, but intracellular distribution still depends on transport and compartment-specific enzymes.

Fat-soluble vitamins are hydrophobic and travel with dietary lipids. Bile salts promote the formation of mixed micelles, which deliver these substances to the brush border. Enterocytes incorporate much of the absorbed material into chylomicrons, which enter lymph before reaching the systemic circulation. Chylomicron remnants reach the liver; from there individual vitamins use distinct transport mechanisms. Retinol may circulate bound to retinol-binding protein, vitamin D metabolites are carried largely by vitamin D-binding protein, and vitamin E travels with lipoproteins. Failure to digest lipids or supply bile may reduce uptake of several vitamins together, so a combined biochemical pattern can point to absorption rather than to four independent dietary deficits.

Storage modifies the time course of deficiency. Vitamin A may be stored as retinyl esters in the liver, while vitamin D and vitamin E are distributed among tissues. Vitamin K stores are relatively limited and its efficient recycling is important. Water-soluble vitamins are generally more readily lost in urine than hydrophobic vitamins, but cobalamin's hepatic reserve and enterohepatic circulation can delay deficiency for years. Thiamin has much smaller reserves. These contrasting time courses explain why an identical period of restricted intake need not affect every vitamin equally.

A vitamin is not necessarily the form recognized by its target enzyme. An intestinally absorbed vitamin may first be oxidized, reduced, phosphorylated, adenylated, hydroxylated or attached to a protein. Each modification changes chemical reactivity or localization. The kidney and liver activation of vitamin D, the intracellular conversion of cobalamin into two distinct coenzyme forms and the ATP-dependent attachment of biotin to carboxylases are examples of this principle. A transport defect affects the tissues that cannot receive the molecule; an activation defect changes the active-to-inactive ratio; an abnormal recycling pathway may leave an initially adequate pool trapped in an unusable state.

For examinations, distinguish dietary supply, absorption, storage, activation, enzyme binding, catalytic recycling and excretion as separate steps. An intervention that corrects one step need not correct another. Measurements of circulating vitamin concentration, functional enzyme activity and downstream metabolites are related but are not identical measures. Their interpretation must respect the compartment and biochemical pathway sampled.

4. Thiamin (B1): stabilizing carbon during decarboxylation

Thiamin contains a substituted aminopyrimidine ring linked to a positively charged thiazolium ring. Conversion by thiamin pyrophosphokinase produces thiamin diphosphate (TDP, also called TPP), whose diphosphate group binds enzymes with the help of magnesium. The thiazolium ring is the reactive centre. Loss of the proton at carbon 2 gives a nucleophilic ylide, a species whose neighbouring positive nitrogen stabilizes the unusual distribution of charge. This allows formation of a carbon–carbon bond to a carbonyl substrate and stabilizes the intermediate produced when carbon dioxide is released.

Consider pyruvate dehydrogenase. Pyruvate enters as a three-carbon α-keto acid. TPP attacks its keto carbon, and the carboxyl carbon leaves as CO₂. The remaining two-carbon fragment is held as hydroxyethyl-TPP. Oxidation and transfer to the lipoyl arm of E2 produce an acetyl group, which is transferred to CoA. The reduced lipoyl arm must be reoxidized by E3, using FAD and then NAD⁺. This sequence explains why several different coenzymes are required for one net reaction: pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺. TPP does not carry the final electrons to oxygen, and it is not consumed stoichiometrically in that net equation.

The same strategy operates in α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase. Their carbon skeletons differ, but each contains the susceptible α-keto acid motif. Transketolase in the nonoxidative pentose phosphate pathway uses TPP for a different outcome: transfer of a two-carbon ketol fragment between sugars without net decarboxylation. The shared chemical theme is stabilization of a carbon nucleophile near a carbonyl; the enzyme decides whether it is used in oxidative decarboxylation or carbon transfer.

When cellular TPP falls, oxidative use of pyruvate is restricted. Lactate dehydrogenase may divert more pyruvate toward lactate to regenerate cytosolic NAD⁺. Impaired α-ketoglutarate oxidation limits TCA cycle throughput, and transketolase activity may also fall. Energy-demanding nervous and cardiac tissues are particularly sensitive; beriberi and Wernicke encephalopathy are classical manifestations of thiamin deficiency. These observations do not imply that all lactate elevations are caused by B1 deficiency; low oxygen, respiratory inhibition and many other disturbances also change pyruvate fate.

In a functional assay, erythrocyte transketolase can be measured before and after adding TPP in vitro. An increase on addition suggests that some enzyme molecules lacked sufficient coenzyme under the assay conditions. Suppose basal activity is 40 units and supplemented activity is 60 units. The activation coefficient is 60/40 = 1.5, corresponding to a 50% rise. The assay does not measure dietary intake directly. Enzyme abundance, sample conditions and other limitations can influence the result, so it is the biochemical principle of cofactor rescue rather than a single clinical cutoff that matters.

5. Riboflavin (B2): one- and two-electron chemistry

Riboflavin consists of a planar, conjugated isoalloxazine ring attached to a ribityl chain. Riboflavin kinase transfers phosphate from ATP to produce flavin mononucleotide (FMN); FAD synthetase adds an AMP moiety from ATP to FMN, forming flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). The isoalloxazine ring, not the adenine portion, carries the changing electronic state. Its conjugation permits oxidized, semiquinone and fully reduced states, enabling sequential single-electron steps as well as an overall two-electron transfer.

A simplified reduction is FAD + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ ⇌ FADH₂. Actual protonation states and redox potentials depend on the protein and pH. Unlike the freely diffusible NAD pool, flavins in many dehydrogenases remain closely associated with the protein. They receive reducing equivalents from a substrate and pass electrons to another acceptor within the same enzyme system. This is especially valuable when a two-electron substrate reaction must be connected to carriers that exchange one electron at a time, such as iron–sulfur centres.

At respiratory complex II, succinate is oxidized to fumarate. Bound FAD accepts the reducing equivalents; electrons then travel through iron–sulfur centres to ubiquinone. The pathway bypasses the proton-pumping activity of complex I and complex II itself does not pump protons. In fatty-acid oxidation, acyl-CoA dehydrogenases use bound FAD and transfer electrons through electron-transfer flavoprotein and its dehydrogenase to ubiquinone. Do not imagine a molecule of FADH₂ diffusing from every dehydrogenase to an identical respiratory entry point. The route of electron transfer is determined by the enzyme system.

FMN is the first redox cofactor in complex I, accepting electrons from NADH before transfer through iron–sulfur centres. FAD is also used by glutathione reductase, which transfers reducing equivalents from NADPH to oxidized glutathione. These examples connect riboflavin to ATP production and antioxidant protection rather than to a single pathway. Additional flavoproteins participate in other vitamin transformations, illustrating that deficiencies can interact.

Riboflavin deficiency can produce angular cheilitis, glossitis and other epithelial changes. Such findings are not uniquely diagnostic, but reduced activity of flavin-dependent enzymes offers a mechanistic basis. An erythrocyte glutathione reductase assay comparing activity with and without added FAD is conceptually analogous to the transketolase assay. If supplemented activity rises, cofactor occupancy is one possible limiting factor. If it does not rise, the result cannot establish normal whole-body riboflavin status by itself. For NET questions, remember the central distinction: flavin chemistry can bridge one- and two-electron carriers, whereas NAD normally transfers a hydride in substrate dehydrogenation.

6. Niacin (B3): NAD, NADP and separate redox economies

Niacin refers to nicotinic acid and nicotinamide, compounds based on a pyridine ring. Cells convert dietary precursors into NAD⁺ through salvage or biosynthetic pathways. Tryptophan catabolism can also contribute to NAD synthesis, although the efficiency depends on nutritional and metabolic conditions. The active nicotinamide ring in NAD⁺ accepts a hydride ion, equivalent to two electrons and one proton, at carbon 4. The aromatic oxidized ring becomes a reduced dihydropyridine. NADP⁺ carries an additional phosphate on the adenosine ribose; this phosphate is a recognition feature for enzymes rather than an extra redox-active centre.

For a dehydrogenation written as RH₂ + NAD⁺ ⇌ R + NADH + H⁺, one hydrogen is transferred as hydride to the coenzyme and the other appears as a proton in solution. NADH is therefore a two-electron carrier even though only one hydrogen atom is added to the nicotinamide ring. NADH generated by glycolysis must be reoxidized, by respiration with appropriate shuttles or by fermentation reactions. If NAD⁺ becomes limiting, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase cannot continue at the previous flux even when glucose is abundant.

Cells maintain distinct redox environments. The NAD system is often relatively oxidized to favour fuel oxidation, while NADPH is maintained relatively reduced to supply electrons to biosynthetic enzymes and antioxidant systems. This separation is functional, not a claim that all cells have identical ratios or that the two pools never communicate. Cytosolic pentose phosphate reactions generate NADPH; other enzymes such as NADP-dependent malic enzyme and isocitrate dehydrogenase contribute in particular compartments. Mitochondrial transhydrogenase can use proton motive force to influence the NADPH pool. The inner mitochondrial membrane restricts direct movement of NAD(H); shuttles transfer reducing equivalents rather than freely equilibrating these nucleotide pools.

NADPH is essential for fatty-acid and cholesterol synthesis, reductive biosynthetic reactions and glutathione recycling. NADPH oxidases also deliberately transfer electrons to oxygen in certain biological responses. Consequently, NADPH is neither universally antioxidant nor exclusively anabolic; its role follows the electron acceptor. NAD⁺ has additional nonredox roles as a substrate for sirtuin deacylation and ADP-ribosylation. Those reactions consume the nucleotide, linking cellular repair and regulatory processes to salvage and synthesis.

At fixed pH, the transformed Nernst relation for the NAD couple is E′ = E°′ + (RT/2F) ln([NAD⁺]/[NADH]), approximating activities by concentrations. R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is absolute temperature and F = 96485 C mol⁻¹. At 298 K, a tenfold rise in the oxidized-to-reduced ratio increases E′ by (8.314 × 298 / (2 × 96485)) ln 10 ≈ 0.0296 V. The more oxidized pool is then a more favourable electron acceptor, all else equal. This is a thermodynamic tendency, not a prediction of enzyme rate without kinetic information.

Niacin deficiency causes pellagra, classically associated with dermatitis, diarrhoea and neurological deterioration. The affected tissues depend on continuous metabolic turnover and NAD-dependent chemistry. The manifestations are a useful reminder that one coenzyme pool supports many reactions. When comparing niacin and riboflavin, do not confuse the nicotinamide ring that receives hydride with the isoalloxazine ring capable of forming a semiquinone.

7. Pantothenate (B5): carrying acyl groups across pathways

Pantothenic acid is an amide formed from pantoic acid and β-alanine. It is incorporated into the 4′-phosphopantetheine portion of coenzyme A (CoA). CoA synthesis begins with phosphorylation by pantothenate kinase, followed by addition of cysteine, decarboxylation and nucleotide-dependent steps. The terminal sulfhydryl group is the chemical handle. It forms thioesters with acyl groups, producing molecules such as acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA and long-chain fatty acyl-CoA.

A thioester is not simply a permanent storage bond. Sulfur participates less effectively in resonance stabilization of the adjacent carbonyl than oxygen does in an ordinary oxygen ester. Hydrolysis or transfer of the acyl group can therefore be thermodynamically favourable. Enzymes exploit this potential to couple carbon transfer to reactions that would otherwise be difficult. In citrate synthase, acetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate and the thioester is hydrolysed, helping drive citrate formation. In succinyl-CoA synthetase, thioester energy is conserved through substrate-level phosphorylation.

CoA participates in both fuel breakdown and fuel construction. Fatty acids are activated to acyl-CoA before oxidation; β-oxidation releases acetyl-CoA and a shortened acyl-CoA. Pyruvate dehydrogenase transfers acetyl groups from lipoamide to CoA. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase converts acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA for biosynthesis, while ketogenesis uses acetyl-CoA units in liver mitochondria. The partition between free CoA and acylated forms matters: a low free CoA pool can limit reactions requiring CoA-SH even if the total pool is large.

Acyl carrier protein (ACP) in fatty-acid synthesis also contains a 4′-phosphopantetheine arm, covalently attached to a serine residue. A terminal thiol holds the growing fatty-acid chain and allows the chain to reach successive catalytic domains. Free CoA and ACP share pantothenate-derived chemistry but differ in molecular organization. CoA is a diffusible cosubstrate within its accessible compartment; ACP is a protein-bound carrier. This parallel illustrates how evolution reuses chemical solutions with different delivery systems.

Pantothenate is widespread in food, so isolated deficiency is uncommon. Its biochemical consequences would be broad rather than selective for only fat metabolism. For examination reasoning, an experiment reducing available CoA predicts difficulties at pyruvate dehydrogenase, fatty-acid activation and numerous acyl transfers; it does not directly predict loss of hydride transfer by an NAD-dependent enzyme that does not use CoA. Distinguish the reactive sulfur of pantetheine from the adenine nucleotide portion and from the sulfur-containing lipoyl arm of dehydrogenase complexes.

8. Vitamin B6: the chemistry of amino groups

The vitamin B6 family includes pyridoxine, pyridoxal and pyridoxamine and their phosphorylated forms. Their shared substituted pyridine structure provides the basis for the active derivative pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP). Phosphorylation and oxidation of appropriate vitamers generate PLP; pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP) is the aminated intermediate of transaminase reactions. PLP contains an aldehyde that reacts with a primary amino group to form a Schiff base, or imine, releasing water.

In many PLP enzymes, the aldehyde initially forms an internal aldimine with a lysine side chain. An incoming amino acid displaces that lysine to create the external aldimine. The conjugated pyridine-imine system stabilizes negative charge formed when a proton or another group is removed from the amino acid's α-carbon. Enzyme positioning governs which bond is aligned for reaction. As a result, PLP-dependent enzymes may catalyse transamination, decarboxylation, racemization or elimination, even though they all exploit related electron delocalization.

During alanine aminotransferase catalysis, alanine transfers its amino group to PLP, giving PMP and pyruvate. In the second half-reaction, PMP transfers the amino group to α-ketoglutarate, yielding glutamate and regenerating PLP. The net process is alanine + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ pyruvate + glutamate. Free ammonia is not released as an obligatory intermediate. The reaction is typically reversible and links amino-acid carbon skeletons to central metabolism while collecting nitrogen in glutamate. The analogous aspartate aminotransferase connects oxaloacetate and aspartate, supplying nitrogen for urea and nucleotide metabolism.

PLP-dependent decarboxylases remove CO₂ from amino acids, forming compounds such as GABA from glutamate and histamine from histidine. Other PLP enzymes include cystathionine β-synthase and cystathionine γ-lyase of the transsulfuration pathway, and aminolevulinate synthase, which combines glycine and succinyl-CoA in haem biosynthesis. Glycogen phosphorylase is an important exception to a simplistic amino-group rule: its bound PLP phosphate participates in acid–base chemistry of phosphorolysis rather than forming the usual substrate Schiff base.

Deficiency may impair neurotransmitter synthesis and haem production; microcytic or sideroblastic anaemia can follow reduced haem synthesis in suitable contexts. Sensory neuropathy may also occur from excessive B6 exposure, showing that water solubility does not guarantee unlimited safety. The goal here is mechanistic understanding, not supplement treatment. In a cell with normal PLP but an inactive aminotransferase, other PLP enzymes may remain functional. A generalized B6 activation defect could instead affect multiple PLP reactions, yielding a broader metabolic pattern.

One should also separate nitrogen transfer from nitrogen disposal. PLP-dependent transaminases move nitrogen between organic molecules. Glutamate dehydrogenase can then release ammonia through oxidative deamination using NAD(P)⁺, and the urea cycle disposes of much of the nitrogen in mammals. The connection involves distinct enzymes and cofactors; a transaminase does not directly synthesize urea.

9. Biotin (B7): ATP-dependent carboxylation

Biotin has a fused ureido and sulfur-containing ring system with a valeric acid side chain. Holocarboxylase synthetase uses ATP to attach the side-chain carboxyl group to the ε-amino group of a specific lysine in a carboxylase. The resulting biotinyl-lysine acts as a mobile arm. Biotinidase releases biotin from degradation products of biotin-containing proteins, enabling recycling. Biotin must therefore be present and correctly attached for the enzyme to function.

Carboxylation involves a chemical challenge: bicarbonate must be activated so that a carbon atom can be added to an organic substrate. In the biotin carboxylase reaction, ATP activates bicarbonate and the carboxyl group is transferred to a nitrogen in biotin's ureido ring. The carboxylated biotin swings to a second catalytic site, where the group is transferred to the acceptor substrate. ATP hydrolysis supplies the energy needed to make the transient carboxyl donor; biotin itself is restored after transfer.

Pyruvate carboxylase catalyses pyruvate + HCO₃⁻ + ATP → oxaloacetate + ADP + Pi (proton bookkeeping omitted). In liver mitochondria, acetyl-CoA activates the enzyme, favouring oxaloacetate supply when fatty-acid oxidation produces abundant acetyl-CoA. Oxaloacetate supports gluconeogenesis or anaplerosis, the replenishment of TCA cycle intermediates. This reaction is a different solution from pyruvate dehydrogenase: the former adds a carbon using biotin, while the latter removes one using TPP.

Acetyl-CoA carboxylase makes malonyl-CoA, the donor of two-carbon extension units in fatty-acid synthesis after decarboxylation. Malonyl-CoA also inhibits CPT I, helping coordinate synthesis with mitochondrial entry of long-chain acyl groups. Propionyl-CoA carboxylase converts the three-carbon propionyl group to D-methylmalonyl-CoA; epimerization yields the L isomer, which is rearranged by the B12-dependent methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. The product, succinyl-CoA, can replenish the TCA cycle. Methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase participates in leucine degradation. These four examples cover the principal human biotin-dependent carboxylase activities without requiring specialist enzyme subunit details.

A useful carbon-accounting problem compares fatty-acid synthesis and gluconeogenesis. Seven acetyl-CoA carboxylation events produce the seven malonyl-CoA units needed to extend an acetyl starter to palmitate; each event costs one ATP. The seven bicarbonate-derived carbons are subsequently released as CO₂ in the condensation steps. Thus they provide activation chemistry but do not make a net contribution to the sixteen-carbon palmitate backbone. In gluconeogenesis from two pyruvate, two pyruvate carboxylase events cost two ATP; the added carboxyl groups are lost in the PEPCK reactions in the simplest direct route.

Biotin deficiency or defective recycling can impair several carboxylases together. Avidin in raw egg white binds biotin strongly, illustrating how binding can reduce bioavailability. Biotin deficiency may be associated with dermatitis, alopecia and neurological abnormalities, but the key exam concept is simultaneous failure of ATP-dependent bicarbonate transfer. Not all carboxylases are biotin-dependent: RuBisCO fixes CO₂ in plants through a different catalytic mechanism. This exception prevents the incorrect rule that every biological carbon fixation reaction requires B7.

10. Folate (B9): one-carbon units for nucleotides and methylation

Folate molecules contain a pteridine ring linked through p-aminobenzoate to one or more glutamate residues. Folic acid is an oxidized form; dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), using NADPH, reduces it through dihydrofolate (DHF) to tetrahydrofolate (THF). Natural food folates include reduced derivatives. Intestinal deconjugation and intracellular polyglutamation affect uptake and retention; the extra glutamates help retain folates within cells and promote interactions with enzymes.

THF is a carrier of single-carbon units in several oxidation states. Carbon can be bound at N5, N10 or across both positions. The biologically important derivatives include 10-formyl-THF, 5,10-methylene-THF, 5-methyl-THF and intermediates that interconvert these pools. The different forms are not identical interchangeable methyl donors. A formyl group is more oxidized than a methyl group, and changing the oxidation state requires specific reactions and, in some cases, reducing equivalents.

Serine hydroxymethyltransferase, a PLP-dependent enzyme, transfers the side-chain carbon of serine to THF to produce glycine and 5,10-methylene-THF. This provides a prominent link between amino-acid metabolism and one-carbon chemistry. 10-formyl-THF contributes two distinct carbons, C2 and C8, to the purine ring. A defect in THF supply may therefore limit de novo AMP and GMP synthesis even if PRPP and amino-acid precursors are abundant. Salvage may partly relieve a de novo requirement but depends on available bases and nucleosides.

Thymidylate synthase uses 5,10-methylene-THF to convert dUMP to dTMP. The transferred carbon becomes the methyl group on thymine; the folate derivative supplies both the carbon and reducing equivalents, becoming DHF. DHFR must reduce DHF back to THF with NADPH for repeated cycling. A simple cycle is THF + serine ⇌ 5,10-methylene-THF + glycine; dUMP + 5,10-methylene-THF → dTMP + DHF; DHF + NADPH + H⁺ → THF + NADP⁺. Cancellation yields dUMP + serine + NADPH + H⁺ → dTMP + glycine + NADP⁺, for this simplified route. The regeneration cost is one NADPH per thymidylate synthesized through this cycle.

Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) converts 5,10-methylene-THF to 5-methyl-THF, a reaction strongly favoured in the forward direction in cells and involving FAD. Methionine synthase uses methylcobalamin to transfer that methyl group ultimately to homocysteine, producing methionine and restoring THF. Methionine can react with ATP to generate S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), which donates methyl groups to many substrates; S-adenosylhomocysteine is subsequently converted to homocysteine. These routes connect one-carbon allocation to both nucleotide synthesis and cellular methylation, without implying that SAM itself is a vitamin.

When folate-dependent nucleotide synthesis is limited, rapidly dividing cells may fail to replicate DNA at the rate required for normal cell maturation. Nuclear maturation lags relative to cytoplasmic growth in erythroid precursors, producing megaloblastic changes. DHFR inhibition by antifolate compounds illustrates the same biochemical dependency experimentally; cells differ in their ability to bypass a block through salvage or supplied reduced folates. Folate deficiency may also raise homocysteine. It does not directly block methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, which requires cobalamin; this distinction is central to differentiating biochemical patterns.

11. Cobalamin (B12): methyl transfer and molecular rearrangement

Cobalamin is structurally exceptional among vitamins. A corrin ring coordinates a cobalt ion, with a variable upper axial ligand and a lower nucleotide-associated ligand. Common forms include cyanocobalamin and hydroxocobalamin, while methylcobalamin and 5′-deoxyadenosylcobalamin are the principal active coenzymes in human metabolism. Different cobalt oxidation states and cobalt–carbon bonding permit chemistry unavailable to most organic cofactors.

Food-bound cobalamin must be released by gastric acid and proteolysis. It first associates with haptocorrin; pancreatic proteases release it in the small intestine so that it binds intrinsic factor. The intrinsic factor–cobalamin complex is recognized by receptors in the terminal ileum. Absorbed vitamin enters circulation in association with transport proteins, notably transcobalamin for delivery to cells. The elaborate sequence explains why impaired intrinsic factor production or terminal ileal absorption can create deficiency even with adequate intake. Large hepatic stores and recycling can delay the appearance of biochemical effects.

In cytosolic methionine synthase, enzyme-bound cobalamin receives a methyl group from 5-methyl-THF and passes it to homocysteine. The products are THF and methionine. This reaction couples the folate pool to methyl-group metabolism. If cobalamin is unavailable, the reaction slows, and folate can accumulate as 5-methyl-THF at the expense of other usable forms. This is the methyl-folate trap. MTHFR directs carbon toward 5-methyl-THF but the reverse route is not a significant rescue under normal cellular conditions. Total folate may therefore be present while THF-dependent nucleotide synthesis is functionally restricted.

In mitochondria, methylmalonyl-CoA mutase requires adenosylcobalamin. Homolytic cleavage of its cobalt–carbon bond generates a 5′-deoxyadenosyl radical, which abstracts hydrogen from the substrate. Rearrangement of the carbon skeleton and hydrogen return produce succinyl-CoA, regenerating the coenzyme's catalytic state. The route is not a carboxylation and does not require an ATP molecule for the rearrangement itself. Its upstream propionyl-CoA carboxylase step, however, uses biotin and ATP. These two consecutive steps are an excellent example of different vitamin-dependent reactions cooperating within a single pathway.

Propionyl-CoA arises from odd-chain fatty acids and from degradation of several amino acids, including valine and isoleucine. A block at methylmalonyl-CoA mutase promotes methylmalonate accumulation and reduces conversion into succinyl-CoA. Both cobalamin and folate deficiency may impair DNA synthesis and elevate homocysteine, but elevated methylmalonic acid is particularly associated with impaired cobalamin-dependent mutase function. Renal impairment and inherited defects can also affect this marker, so it is not absolute proof of dietary B12 deficiency.

Cobalamin deficiency may produce neurological abnormalities as well as megaloblastic anaemia. The neurological consequences are not fully explained by the defect in nucleotide synthesis alone; disturbed methylation and metabolism of propionyl-derived intermediates are relevant. Restoring nucleotide precursors or folate availability can improve some haematological features without restoring the B12-dependent mutase pathway. This is why folate and B12 must not be treated as equivalent merely because both participate in one-carbon metabolism.

The exam-relevant distinction is between methylcobalamin-dependent methyl transfer and adenosylcobalamin-dependent radical rearrangement. If a mutation selectively disables mitochondrial conversion to adenosylcobalamin, the methylmalonate pathway may be impaired while cytosolic methionine synthase remains relatively functional. A generalized absorption failure, in contrast, can eventually affect both.

12. Vitamin C: electron donation, collagen and redox protection

Ascorbic acid is a six-carbon lactone with an enediol group. At physiological pH it is largely present as ascorbate, which readily donates electrons to form a resonance-stabilized radical and subsequently dehydroascorbate. Cellular reducing systems can regenerate ascorbate; irreversible degradation of oxidized forms leads to loss from the pool. Humans depend on external vitamin C because the final enzyme of the usual mammalian biosynthetic pathway is nonfunctional.

A central function is the maintenance of iron in a catalytically competent state in certain Fe²⁺/2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases. Collagen prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases use oxygen and 2-oxoglutarate to hydroxylate specific residues, with succinate and CO₂ produced from 2-oxoglutarate. Ascorbate helps restore reduced catalytic iron following oxidation, especially in uncoupled turnovers. It is not incorporated as the hydroxyl group in collagen; oxygen supplies the incorporated oxygen atom. Hydroxyproline contributes to triple-helix stability, while hydroxylated lysines participate in collagen modification and cross-link-related chemistry.

When ascorbate is inadequate, newly synthesized collagen may be less stable and connective tissue maintenance deteriorates. Bleeding gums, poor wound healing and fragile vessels are classical consequences of scurvy. Existing collagen does not simply disappear overnight; the defect affects ongoing synthesis, maturation and renewal. This connection explains how a small redox-active nutrient influences an apparently distant mechanical property of tissues.

Ascorbate also facilitates nonhaem iron absorption through reduction of ferric iron to ferrous iron and maintenance of soluble iron complexes. It participates in carnitine biosynthesis and assists dopamine β-hydroxylase in catecholamine synthesis. Its capacity to donate electrons is useful in aqueous antioxidant networks, where it can help regenerate α-tocopherol from its radical at membrane interfaces. This is a cooperative process, not a guarantee that any isolated antioxidant will suppress every oxidative reaction.

Ascorbate can behave as a reductant in metal-catalysed chemistry under particular conditions; its physiological role depends on compartment, metal availability and redox context. The educational lesson is to identify the electron acceptor and the subsequent regeneration pathway. Vitamin C is not an NAD-like universal cosubstrate of dehydrogenases, nor is it the sole reason collagen becomes stable. Collagen's sequence, hydroxylation, triple-helix organization and extracellular maturation all contribute.

13. Vitamin A: a single structural family, two very different functions

Retinoids share an extended conjugated polyene chain associated with a β-ionone-like ring. The end group distinguishes retinol (alcohol), retinal (aldehyde) and retinoic acid (carboxylic acid). Retinyl esters are storage forms, whereas β-carotene is a provitamin with the potential to yield retinal after enzymatic cleavage. Conversion depends on the carotenoid structure and the organism's enzymes; not every carotenoid yields vitamin A.

Intestinal uptake follows fat-associated absorption. Retinyl esters are hydrolysed and retinol may be re-esterified in enterocytes for chylomicron transport. The liver stores substantial retinyl esters, especially in stellate cells. Mobilized retinol circulates with retinol-binding protein, and tissues convert it to functional derivatives as required. Retinol and retinal can interconvert through dehydrogenases, whereas oxidation of retinal to retinoic acid is effectively irreversible in normal human metabolism. Retinoic acid cannot replace retinal in the visual cycle.

In rod photoreceptors, 11-cis-retinal is linked through a Schiff base to a lysine of opsin to form rhodopsin. Absorption of a photon changes retinal geometry to the all-trans configuration, inducing a conformational change in the protein. Activated rhodopsin stimulates transducin and a phosphodiesterase, reducing cGMP and closing cGMP-gated channels. The photoreceptor hyperpolarizes. This is an elegant example of molecular conformation being converted into cellular information. The detailed signal transduction and eye physiology belong to later units; here the important point is the conjugated chromophore and the need for enzymatic recycling to 11-cis-retinal.

A second branch of retinoid metabolism produces all-trans-retinoic acid, which binds nuclear retinoic acid receptors. These receptors regulate transcription in conjunction with partner receptors and DNA response elements. Retinoic acid thus contributes to cell differentiation and epithelial maintenance through altered gene expression rather than by transferring electrons or an acyl group. It is inappropriate to label retinoic acid a classic catalytic coenzyme.

Reduced supply of functional retinal compromises dark adaptation, explaining night blindness as an early classical manifestation of vitamin A deficiency. Severe deficiency can impair epithelial integrity, including the ocular surface. Excess preformed vitamin A can accumulate and disturb normal regulation. The structural and functional split predicts an experiment: supplying retinoic acid to a system that lacks retinal regeneration may support some transcriptional responses but cannot directly restore the chromophore required for photon capture. The same vitamin family supports two functions because different oxidation states have distinct chemical properties.

14. Vitamin D: photochemistry becomes endocrine regulation

Vitamin D₃ (cholecalciferol) is a secosteroid derived from 7-dehydrocholesterol in skin. Ultraviolet B radiation opens one steroid ring to form previtamin D₃, followed by thermal isomerization to vitamin D₃. Vitamin D₂ (ergocalciferol) is derived from ergosterol and differs in its side chain. Both can enter the activation pathways. The broken-ring structure is a consequence of photochemistry and distinguishes these compounds from intact steroid nuclei.

Neither newly synthesized vitamin D₃ nor absorbed D₂ represents the final active endocrine signal. In the liver, 25-hydroxylation produces 25-hydroxyvitamin D (calcidiol), the major circulating form typically measured to assess supply. In the kidney, 1α-hydroxylation by CYP27B1 produces 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol, for D₃), the strongly active hormonal form. The circulating metabolites are transported with vitamin D-binding protein. CYP24A1 initiates catabolic hydroxylation of vitamin D metabolites, providing a route to inactivation.

Regulation responds to mineral balance. A fall in ionized calcium can increase parathyroid hormone secretion, promoting renal calcitriol production. Calcitriol increases intestinal capacity for calcium and phosphate absorption by regulating expression of transport-related proteins. FGF23 contributes negative regulation of renal calcitriol production in phosphate homeostasis, and calcitriol participates in feedback. This is an example of signal-controlled conversion of a vitamin precursor, not simple unrestricted accumulation of the active form.

Calcitriol binds the vitamin D receptor, which partners with RXR and alters transcription at responsive elements. The downstream effects support mineral homeostasis and skeletal mineralization. When vitamin D-dependent mineral supply is inadequate, growing bones may develop rickets and adult bones may develop osteomalacia. These are failures of mineralization, not simply the same mechanism as collagen weakness in vitamin C deficiency.

A useful inference separates precursor status from active hormone formation. A subject with impaired renal 1α-hydroxylase may have a normal or near-normal 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration but inadequate calcitriol production. Conversely, compensation by parathyroid hormone can maintain calcitriol during some stages of low vitamin D supply. Measuring only the active hormone does not always reveal the size of the precursor pool. Some tissues produce calcitriol locally, but the renal pathway is central to systemic mineral regulation. Detailed endocrine physiology is reserved for later units; the focus here is steroid-derived vitamin structure, activation and feedback.

15. Vitamin E: stopping lipid radical chain reactions

Vitamin E comprises tocopherols and tocotrienols. Their chromanol ring contains a phenolic hydroxyl group that can donate hydrogen, while their hydrophobic side chain favours association with membranes and lipoproteins. Tocotrienols have unsaturated side chains; tocopherols have saturated side chains. α-Tocopherol is preferentially retained in human tissues through hepatic α-tocopherol transfer protein, which contributes to its prominence as the biologically relevant dietary form.

Unsaturated membrane lipids can undergo radical chain oxidation. An initiating radical may abstract hydrogen from a lipid LH, producing L•. Oxygen rapidly adds to form a lipid peroxyl radical LOO•. This radical can abstract hydrogen from another lipid, producing a lipid hydroperoxide LOOH and a new L•. The chain can therefore amplify damage without needing a new initiating event for every oxidized lipid.

α-Tocopherol, represented as TocOH, competes for the peroxyl radical: LOO• + TocOH → LOOH + TocO•. The tocopheroxyl radical is comparatively stabilized and less likely to propagate the lipid chain. Under suitable conditions, ascorbate can reduce it back to TocOH at aqueous–lipid interfaces. Glutathione peroxidase systems, particularly GPX4 for phospholipid hydroperoxides, help remove hydroperoxide products. Glutathione reductase restores reduced glutathione using NADPH and FAD. The combined network connects vitamins E, C, B2 and B3 to the NADPH-generating pathways discussed previously.

The localization is as important as chemical reactivity. A hydrophobic chain-breaking antioxidant is well positioned to intercept lipid radicals within a membrane, whereas a water-soluble reductant has different accessibility. Vitamin E does not itself enzymatically reduce every hydroperoxide to an alcohol; termination of the radical chain and disposal of the oxidized lipid product are distinct operations. Selenium-dependent peroxidase chemistry is not vitamin E chemistry, even though the systems can cooperate.

Severe vitamin E deficiency can compromise membrane integrity and contribute to haemolysis or neurological dysfunction, particularly when fat absorption or transport is impaired. Mature erythrocytes are an instructive case because they lack mitochondria and rely strongly on metabolic antioxidant support in a haemoglobin-rich environment. Do not confuse oxidative membrane injury with a primary defect in ATP synthesis or with a clotting defect. A comparison of membrane lipid peroxidation with and without tocopherol in a controlled model can reveal chain-breaking activity, provided lipid composition and initiation rates are held constant.

16. Vitamin K: modifying proteins to bind calcium

Vitamin K compounds have a 2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone ring with hydrophobic side chains. Phylloquinone (K1) is prominent in green plants; menaquinones (K2) have varying isoprenoid chain lengths and can arise from microbial and dietary sources. The quinone ring can cycle among oxidized and reduced states. Intestinal absorption is linked to lipid handling, and gut microbial production does not guarantee adequate availability to the host.

In the endoplasmic reticulum, γ-glutamyl carboxylase adds CO₂ to the γ-carbon of selected glutamate side chains in proteins. The reaction uses reduced vitamin K hydroquinone, oxygen and carbon dioxide; vitamin K becomes its epoxide. The resulting γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla) residue bears an extra carboxylate and can coordinate Ca²⁺. Multiple Gla residues enable certain clotting proteins to associate with negatively charged phospholipid surfaces in a calcium-dependent manner, bringing reaction components together at appropriate locations.

Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) converts the epoxide back toward the quinone form, and reduction regenerates hydroquinone. This recycling makes a limited vitamin supply useful over many rounds of protein modification. Warfarin inhibits VKOR, reducing the availability of reduced vitamin K and therefore the production of fully carboxylated functional vitamin K-dependent proteins. Existing already-carboxylated proteins are not immediately stripped of their modification; the functional effect depends on protein turnover.

Factors II, VII, IX and X and regulatory proteins C and S are classical vitamin K-dependent proteins. Gla-containing proteins also occur in bone and other tissues. A liver may synthesize the polypeptide chain in normal amounts yet release a protein with reduced activity because the required post-translational carboxylation failed. Thus a protein abundance measurement and a functional coagulation measurement need not agree.

Distinguish vitamin K-dependent γ-carboxylation from biotin-dependent carboxylation. Vitamin K participates in oxidation-coupled modification of a glutamate side chain in a protein. Biotin uses ATP-activated bicarbonate to add carbon to metabolic substrates such as pyruvate and acetyl-CoA. Both add carbon dioxide equivalents, but their substrates, chemical machinery and purpose differ. Vitamin K deficiency is classically associated with a bleeding tendency; this is not evidence that platelet number is necessarily reduced. The biochemical focus is the generation and recycling of the reduced quinone cofactor.

17. A reaction-based map of the vitamins

The most productive way to consolidate the thirteen vitamins is to classify them by chemical task. TPP stabilizes carbon-centred intermediates; flavins and nicotinamide nucleotides accept electrons in different ways; pantetheine carries acyl groups; PLP controls chemistry at the amino acid α-carbon; biotin carries activated carbon dioxide; THF carries single-carbon fragments at varying oxidation states; cobalamin supports methyl transfer and radical rearrangement. Ascorbate and tocopherol participate in redox protection in different physical environments, while retinal, retinoic acid, calcitriol and vitamin K solve specialized problems of light sensing, transcription and protein carboxylation.

Chemical structures explain these roles. The thiazolium ring permits ylide formation, the isoalloxazine ring permits intermediate redox states, the nicotinamide pyridinium ring accepts hydride, and a terminal sulfhydryl group forms acyl thioesters. PLP's aldehyde creates an imine; the ureido group of biotin binds carboxyl carbon; folate nitrogens bind one-carbon groups; cobalt in a corrin ring supports unusual metal–carbon chemistry. In fat-soluble vitamins, a polyene chromophore absorbs light, a secosteroid derivative binds a receptor, a phenolic group intercepts radicals, and a quinone cycles through oxidation states. Students need not memorize every atom of an entire coenzyme to understand why the reactive group is suited to its job.

The same pathway may depend on many coenzymes, and the same coenzyme may appear in many pathways. Pyruvate oxidation needs TPP, lipoyl groups, CoA, FAD and NAD⁺. The pentose phosphate pathway uses NADP⁺ in its oxidative reactions and TPP in transketolase. Amino-acid degradation supplies carbon to the TCA cycle using PLP, NAD(P) and sometimes biotin and B12. Purine synthesis uses folate derivatives; dTMP synthesis links folate, NADPH and serine metabolism. A table of vitamin–enzyme pairs is useful only after this network logic is understood.

Consider an experiment in which total protein and substrate concentration are held constant while an extract is dialysed. Small freely diffusible molecules may be removed, decreasing an enzyme's rate. Restoration by adding NAD⁺ supports the presence of an NAD-dependent reaction; restoration by FAD may indicate flavin dependence. Dialysis may also remove ions and destabilize proteins, so include an untreated extract, a buffer-only control, and a reaction with known active coenzyme. A tightly bound prosthetic group may not be removed by the procedure. Negative rescue is not proof of independence from that cofactor.

The correct interpretation of a functional vitamin deficiency depends on the location of the lesion. For an enzyme defect, substrate accumulates relative to product near the block; for an impaired shared cofactor, several unrelated pathways may be affected. Even this rule is conditional because flux can be rerouted, metabolites transported away, and feedback can reduce upstream production. A metabolite pool is not the same as metabolic flux. These principles now allow us to interpret the integrated behaviour of fuel pathways.

18. The metabolic crossroads: carbon, energy, reducing power and nitrogen

The metabolic map is best understood by following four conserved currencies. Carbon enters as glucose, fatty acids and amino-acid skeletons. Nitrogen is transferred chiefly through amino-acid reactions and must be incorporated into nitrogenous biomolecules or disposed of safely. ATP and related nucleotides represent immediately usable phosphoryl-transfer capacity. NADH and NADPH carry reducing equivalents with distinct physiological roles. A pathway can satisfy one demand while worsening another; for example, rapid glycolysis without adequate NAD⁺ regeneration cannot sustain carbon flux even though glucose is present.

Glucose-6-phosphate is a branch point between glycogen storage, glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway. Glycolysis delivers pyruvate, whose fates depend on redox state, oxygen use and cellular needs. Lactate formation supports NAD⁺ regeneration; pyruvate dehydrogenase supplies acetyl-CoA for oxidative metabolism; pyruvate carboxylase replenishes oxaloacetate and supports glucose formation. Acetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle if oxaloacetate and oxidative capacity are available. When energy and carbon are abundant, citrate can be exported from mitochondria and cleaved by ATP citrate lyase to supply cytosolic acetyl-CoA for fatty-acid and cholesterol synthesis.

Acetyl-CoA is central but is not a universal source of net glucose. In mammals, the pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is effectively irreversible, and the TCA cycle does not generate net oxaloacetate from acetyl-CoA alone: the entry of two acetyl carbons is matched by two decarboxylations per turn in the steady-state carbon balance. Their individual isotope identities need not coincide in the first turn. Glycerol from triacylglycerol, propionyl-CoA from odd-chain fatty acids and glucogenic amino-acid skeletons can contribute to net glucose formation. Plants and certain microorganisms possessing a glyoxylate cycle have an additional strategy for conserving acetyl carbon; that exception does not apply to humans.

Nitrogen is gathered into glutamate by aminotransferases and can be transported as glutamine or alanine. In liver, ammonia and aspartate supply the nitrogen atoms of urea, whereas the carbon comes from bicarbonate. Carbon skeletons feed into pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, acetoacetate or TCA intermediates. Aspartate, glutamine, glycine, bicarbonate and folate-derived carbons also contribute to nucleotide construction. The oxidative pentose pathway supplies ribose precursors and NADPH; the nonoxidative reactions can rearrange sugars to match relative needs for ribose and reducing power.

This network is spatially partitioned. Glycolysis occurs in cytosol, whereas pyruvate dehydrogenase and most TCA reactions occur in mitochondria. Fatty-acid synthesis mainly uses cytosolic acetyl units, and mitochondrial β-oxidation accesses long-chain acyl groups via the carnitine system. Acetyl-CoA cannot simply diffuse across the inner mitochondrial membrane; citrate export solves the problem of moving acetyl equivalents for synthesis. Nucleotide synthesis and vitamin activation likewise depend on compartment-specific transport and enzyme localization. The next unit on cellular organization will explain the physical basis of this biochemical partitioning.

19. How cells choose a pathway: regulation on several timescales

Regulation is required because opposite pathways often coexist. A cell that simultaneously synthesized and hydrolysed large amounts of the same storage compound would spend ATP with little useful result. Some substrate cycling is physiologically useful for responsiveness or heat production, but net flux must generally match the cell's current demand. Reciprocal regulation changes the relative activities of opposing pathways rather than assuming one direction is always completely silent.

Allosteric signals act rapidly. ATP and citrate can inhibit phosphofructokinase-1 under appropriate conditions, whereas AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate promote glycolytic activity. Acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase, and a high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio can restrain oxidative dehydrogenases. Substrate availability and product removal alter actual reaction free energies. These controls work together: a pathway can be thermodynamically favourable yet have low flux if its controlling enzyme is inhibited or if its substrate is unavailable in the correct compartment.

The adenylate energy charge is defined as ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP]+[ADP]+[AMP]). Its numerator counts ATP as fully charged and ADP as half charged relative to AMP; the ratio is dimensionless and ranges from zero to one for nonnegative concentrations. For ATP = 6 mM, ADP = 2 mM and AMP = 1 mM, the value is (6+1)/9 = 0.778. After ATP consumption giving 4, 3 and 2 mM respectively, the charge is (4+1.5)/9 = 0.611. The declining value indicates lower phosphorylation status, not the exact ATP-production rate. Adenylate kinase interconverts 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP, so AMP can change substantially during energetic stress.

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a cellular energy sensor influenced by adenine nucleotide binding and activating phosphorylation. In energetic stress it tends to restrain energy-consuming processes and favour ATP-generating ones. Phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase can reduce malonyl-CoA formation, relieving inhibition of CPT I and supporting fatty-acid oxidation. This provides a direct molecular connection between an energy signal, a biotin-dependent biosynthetic enzyme and mitochondrial fuel entry. AMPK is not synonymous with glucagon: it responds to intracellular energy state, whereas glucagon communicates systemic needs to target tissues.

Hormones add coordination across tissues. Insulin signalling promotes storage and use of glucose in responsive cells, while glucagon stimulates hepatic glucose production through cAMP-dependent mechanisms. Covalent phosphorylation changes the activity of glycogen enzymes and the liver bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2, thereby shifting the concentration of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. The liver isoform must be specified because the response of other tissue isoforms can differ. Longer-term changes in expression of metabolic enzymes reinforce adaptations. Insulin can promote lipogenic enzyme expression, whereas prolonged fasting induces enzymes supporting oxidation and glucose production.

No single enzyme is universally the rate-limiting step for every condition. Flux control can be distributed among several reactions and transport processes; its distribution changes with substrates, hormones and tissue context. In exam questions, identify the stated control point, follow its immediate substrate/product consequence, then ask how the rest of the network may compensate. Avoid replacing mechanism with a slogan such as 'insulin activates all enzymes' or 'fasting turns off glycolysis everywhere.'

20. The fed state: store surplus without stopping essential work

After a mixed meal, glucose and amino acids enter portal blood, while much dietary long-chain lipid reaches circulation in chylomicrons. The rise in insulin relative to glucagon signals that external nutrients are available. The liver takes up glucose through GLUT2 according to concentration gradients and phosphorylates it through glucokinase. Unlike the insulin-responsive GLUT4 system in muscle and adipose tissue, hepatic GLUT2 is not recruited to the membrane by insulin in the same way. Insulin nonetheless strongly influences hepatic enzyme activity and gene expression.

Liver glucose-6-phosphate is used for glycogen synthesis and glycolysis. When glycogen needs and immediate energy demands are met, carbon may flow through glycolysis, acetyl-CoA and citrate into lipid synthesis. The pentose phosphate pathway provides NADPH, supplemented by other NADPH-generating enzymes. Fatty-acid synthesis consumes acetyl units, ATP for malonyl-CoA formation and NADPH for reduction. Triacylglycerols produced in the liver can be exported in VLDL. Cholesterol synthesis also consumes acetyl-CoA and NADPH; it is regulated independently as well as by nutritional state.

In skeletal muscle, insulin promotes GLUT4 translocation, increasing glucose entry. Glucose can be oxidized to support work or stored as glycogen for later local use. Amino acids provide substrates for protein synthesis in the fed state. Muscle glycogen is not primarily a reserve for blood glucose because skeletal muscle lacks the hepatic glucose-6-phosphatase system needed for substantial free-glucose export. This tissue specificity is a key distinction between two organs that contain the same glucose polymer.

Adipose tissue uses glucose-derived dihydroxyacetone phosphate to generate glycerol-3-phosphate for triacylglycerol synthesis. Lipoprotein lipase at capillary surfaces hydrolyses circulating triacylglycerols, supplying fatty acids for uptake. Insulin favours net storage and suppresses adipocyte lipolysis. Adipocytes have comparatively low glycerol kinase activity in the classical model, so glycolytic provision of glycerol-3-phosphate is important, although glyceroneogenesis also contributes under some conditions.

The brain continues to use glucose as a major energy substrate, and erythrocytes continue glycolysis because mature red cells lack mitochondria. Thus an anabolic whole-body state does not mean every tissue is synthesizing fatty acids or glycogen. Energy production and cellular maintenance remain necessary. The fed liver can still oxidize fuel; the distinction is net allocation, not the absolute presence or absence of a pathway. Nutrient composition, physical activity and time after the meal influence the exact pattern.

21. Fasting: moving from stored glucose to stored fat

As intestinal nutrient delivery declines, insulin falls relative to glucagon. Hepatic glycogenolysis initially supports blood glucose, while gluconeogenesis contributes increasingly as glycogen stores diminish. Glucagon signals through hepatic cAMP and protein kinase A, favouring glycogen breakdown and lowering fructose-2,6-bisphosphate through the liver PFK-2/FBPase-2 system. The resulting decrease in PFK-1 stimulation and relief of inhibition on fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase promote net gluconeogenesis.

In adipose tissue, reduced insulin-mediated suppression and catecholamine signalling promote lipolysis. Fatty acids travel to oxidative tissues largely bound to albumin, while glycerol can be used by liver and kidney for glucose synthesis. In the liver, decreased acetyl-CoA carboxylase activity and reduced malonyl-CoA favour CPT I-mediated entry of long-chain fatty acyl groups. β-Oxidation generates NADH, FAD-linked reducing equivalents and acetyl-CoA. Respiratory metabolism supplies the ATP required for gluconeogenesis, and acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase.

As oxaloacetate and related intermediates are directed toward gluconeogenesis and high mitochondrial NADH influences TCA redox equilibria, acetyl-CoA supply may exceed its use in the cycle. Liver mitochondria convert surplus acetyl units into ketone bodies, principally acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate. These water-soluble fuels are exported to extrahepatic tissues, where they can be converted back to acetyl-CoA. Liver lacks the key SCOT/OXCT1 activity necessary for effective ketone utilization, preventing the main producing organ from consuming its exported product through the usual ketolytic route.

During more prolonged fasting, the brain increases ketone use, reducing but not eliminating glucose demand. Erythrocytes cannot oxidize ketone bodies or fatty acids because they have no mitochondria. Their glycolysis continues to produce lactate, which can return to liver for gluconeogenesis. The kidney contributes increasingly to glucose production and can use glutamine metabolism to support acid–base homeostasis. Skeletal muscle changes fuel choice with duration and activity; early it may use ketones, whereas later greater fatty-acid oxidation helps conserve ketones for other tissues.

Protein cannot serve as the unlimited preferred energy reserve because functional proteins are required for structure, movement and catalysis. Early fasting mobilizes amino acids for gluconeogenesis; ketone adaptation reduces some glucose demand and helps spare protein. The timing of these shifts varies with glycogen reserve, preceding diet, energy expenditure and physiological state. Fixed hour thresholds are not reliable universal rules. Nutritional ketosis also differs from uncontrolled diabetic ketoacidosis in degree, regulation and acid–base consequences. The purpose here is understanding coordinated flux, not prescribing fasting or treating disease.

22. Exercise, interorgan cycles and tissue-specific exceptions

During muscular contraction, ATP hydrolysis accelerates. Phosphocreatine and adenylate kinase reactions buffer ATP briefly; glycolysis and oxidative metabolism provide longer-term support. Contraction-associated calcium and energy signals can stimulate glucose uptake and glycogen mobilization independently of the fed-state insulin signal. The intensity and duration of exercise, oxygen delivery and fibre composition influence fuel choice. High-rate glycolytic flux can generate lactate even when some oxygen is available, because pyruvate formation and cytosolic NADH production can exceed the immediate capacity for mitochondrial oxidation.

Lactate is neither an intrinsically useless waste product nor proof of complete tissue anoxia. It can travel to oxidative muscle fibres and heart for oxidation, or to liver and kidney for gluconeogenesis. The Cori cycle describes glucose conversion to lactate in one tissue and glucose resynthesis from lactate in another. Two lactate molecules yield two pyruvate and ultimately one glucose. Glycolysis of that glucose provides two ATP to the producing tissue, whereas gluconeogenesis from the two pyruvate consumes four ATP and two GTP in the liver. The whole-body cost is four ATP equivalents per idealized cycle. This energy transfer is possible because another fuel, often fatty acids, supports hepatic metabolism.

The glucose–alanine cycle carries both carbon and amino nitrogen. Muscle can transfer an amino group from glutamate to pyruvate via PLP-dependent alanine aminotransferase. Alanine travels to liver, where its amino group is transferred back to α-ketoglutarate, yielding pyruvate and glutamate. Pyruvate can enter gluconeogenesis, and nitrogen can be directed toward urea synthesis. Compared with the Cori cycle, the alanine cycle adds the task of safe nitrogen transport and disposal, which itself has an energetic cost.

Heart muscle normally oxidizes a mixture of fatty acids, glucose, lactate and ketone bodies according to availability and conditions. The brain's reliance on glucose shifts partially toward ketones in prolonged fasting, whereas erythrocytes remain dependent on glycolysis and use the pentose phosphate pathway for NADPH. Liver orchestrates systemic glucose supply and ketone export but also requires fuel for its own work. Adipose tissue buffers energy supply by storing and releasing triacylglycerol. The kidney is not merely a filtration organ: its cortex can perform gluconeogenesis, especially during prolonged fasting.

The distinction between systemic and local regulation is crucial. Glucagon's principal role in this context is hepatic; skeletal muscle glycogenolysis during contraction is controlled by local energy demand, calcium and adrenergic signals rather than direct glucagon control. Adipose lipolysis cannot be inferred from glucagon alone in humans. A metabolic perturbation may affect tissues in opposite ways. For example, hepatic fatty-acid oxidation can increase while hepatic fatty-acid synthesis decreases; at the same time, muscle may continue to oxidize glucose when work is intense. The correct description of a state specifies organ, pathway, direction of net flux and relevant time scale.

23. Reading metabolic evidence: from cofactor deficiency to flux

A mechanistic explanation is most convincing when it predicts observations that can distinguish it from alternatives. Suppose cultured cells receive equal amounts of glucose and have comparable mitochondrial mass, but group X has low TPP availability. One expects lower pyruvate dehydrogenase flux if enzyme cofactor occupancy becomes limiting, with possible accumulation of pyruvate or lactate. A fall in oxygen consumption may follow if substrate delivery to the respiratory chain is restricted. Yet adding succinate to permeabilized cells may partly bypass pyruvate oxidation, provided respiratory complex II and downstream components are functional. Such a rescue tests the location of the block rather than proving every vitamin-dependent reaction is restored.

A second model concerns one-carbon metabolism. In a cell culture, compare folate deprivation, generalized cobalamin deprivation and isolated methylmalonyl-CoA mutase impairment. The first two may constrain thymidylate synthesis or elevate homocysteine, but the third directly affects propionyl-derived carbon metabolism rather than folate recycling. Measuring methylmalonate as well as homocysteine improves discrimination. Adding thymidine can bypass the de novo dTMP reaction if the salvage machinery is functional, but it will not restore purine synthesis or mutase activity. Thus rescue of DNA replication is not equivalent to correction of all metabolic defects.

Tracer experiments distinguish flux from concentration. If glucose labelled at carbon 1 enters the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, that carbon is released as CO₂ during the oxidative phase, whereas carbon 6 is not released in that first oxidative passage. Comparing initial labelled CO₂ production from carbon-1-labelled and carbon-6-labelled glucose can help estimate pentose pathway contribution under appropriate conditions. Later glycolysis, TCA oxidation, recycling and isotope exchange complicate the pattern. A labelled product identifies a possible path of atoms but does not by itself quantify net flux without a model and appropriate time controls.

Quantitative redox accounting supplies an additional check. One passage of one glucose-6-phosphate through the oxidative pentose pathway produces two NADPH and one CO₂, leaving a five-carbon sugar phosphate. Synthesis of one palmitate requires fourteen NADPH for seven reduction pairs. If the oxidative pentose pathway is the only NADPH source and each glucose-6-phosphate molecule passes once, seven glucose-6-phosphate molecules supply fourteen NADPH, with seven CO₂ and seven pentose phosphate products. This is a partial ledger: it does not include the separate acetyl-CoA, ATP and carbon recycling requirements of lipogenesis. In real cells NADPH may come from several enzymes, and pentose carbons can return to glycolysis.

Another calculation connects vitamin metabolism to energy yield. With the simplifying respiratory yields of 2.5 ATP per mitochondrial NADH and 1.5 ATP per FAD-linked reducing equivalent entering the ubiquinone pool, oxidation of one pyruvate through PDH and one TCA turn yields four NADH, one FAD-linked equivalent and one GTP. The approximate total is 4 × 2.5 + 1 × 1.5 + 1 = 12.5 ATP equivalents. This calculation excludes glycolysis and transport costs. If pyruvate dehydrogenase is completely blocked, one cannot merely subtract the single PDH NADH and assume the TCA turn still occurs on that same pyruvate carbon: acetyl-CoA delivery itself is lost. Alternative fuels may sustain the cycle, but that is a changed experimental system.

For a simple mass-balance exercise, let the liver release glucose at 12 arbitrary units per time while peripheral tissues consume 14 and intestinal delivery contributes 2. The blood glucose pool is then stationary because 12+2−14=0. If intestinal delivery becomes zero and other rates do not initially change, the pool falls at 2 units per time. Restoring steady state requires increased endogenous production, reduced utilization, or both. A steady concentration never means that turnover is absent. The same argument applies to coenzyme pools: synthesis, recycling, transport and consumption must be balanced to sustain usable concentration.

24. Part B: concepts and assertion–reason

These are original examination-level questions. Select your answer before revealing the explanation.

Question 1

Which vitamin-derived coenzyme stabilizes a carbanion-like intermediate in α-keto acid decarboxylation?

A. FAD; B. TPP; C. THF; D. Biotin

Answer and explanation

B. The thiazolium ylide of TPP stabilizes the intermediate. FAD carries electrons, THF carries one-carbon fragments, and biotin transfers activated CO₂.

Question 2

Which structural moiety of NAD⁺ receives hydride?

A. Adenine; B. Pyrophosphate; C. Nicotinamide ring; D. 2′-phosphate of NADP⁺

Answer and explanation

C. The nicotinamide ring accepts hydride at C4. Adenine and phosphate groups participate in recognition and binding, not the redox reaction.

Question 3

Which pair contains two biotin-dependent enzymes?

A. PDH and transketolase; B. Pyruvate carboxylase and acetyl-CoA carboxylase; C. Methionine synthase and mutase; D. Glutathione reductase and succinate dehydrogenase

Answer and explanation

B. Both use ATP-dependent bicarbonate activation. A uses TPP; C uses B12; D uses flavins.

Question 4

Which active form is required by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase?

A. Methylcobalamin; B. Adenosylcobalamin; C. PLP; D. 5-methyl-THF

Answer and explanation

B. The cobalt–carbon bond generates a radical for rearrangement. Methylcobalamin is used by methionine synthase; PLP and folate have different chemistry.

Question 5

Which statement about ACP is correct?

A. It carries acyl groups on a phosphopantetheine thiol; B. It contains retinal; C. It transports NADH into mitochondria; D. It is a free steroid

Answer and explanation

A. ACP has a covalently attached pantetheine arm, related to CoA. The other options describe unrelated molecules or processes.

Question 6

Which reaction is directly dependent on 5,10-methylene-THF?

A. dUMP → dTMP; B. Succinate → fumarate; C. Pyruvate → oxaloacetate; D. Alanine → pyruvate by transamination

Answer and explanation

A. Thymidylate synthase uses methylene-THF; the remaining reactions depend on FAD, biotin and PLP respectively.

Question 7

Which vitamin directly supports formation of Gla residues?

A. C; B. E; C. K; D. D

Answer and explanation

C. Reduced vitamin K supports γ-glutamyl carboxylase. C supports hydroxylases, E interrupts lipid radical chains, and D regulates transcription.

Question 8

Why can retinoic acid not directly substitute for 11-cis-retinal in vision?

A. It lacks all carbon; B. It has a carboxyl group and cannot provide the required retinal aldehyde by normal reverse conversion; C. It is a flavin; D. It is phosphorylated biotin

Answer and explanation

B. The oxidation to retinoic acid is effectively irreversible and the aldehyde forms the Schiff base with opsin. The other choices are chemically false.

Question 9

Which tissue cannot oxidize ketone bodies because it lacks mitochondria?

A. Heart; B. Erythrocytes; C. Skeletal muscle; D. Brain during prolonged fasting

Answer and explanation

B. Mature red cells rely on glycolysis. The other tissues possess mitochondria and can use ketones in appropriate conditions.

Question 10

Assertion: FAD can form a semiquinone. Reason: Its conjugated isoalloxazine ring accommodates sequential one-electron changes.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. The electronic properties of the ring allow the intermediate radical state. The reason directly explains the assertion.

Question 11

Assertion: B12 deficiency can cause functional folate deficiency. Reason: Methionine synthase requires cobalamin to regenerate THF from methyl-THF.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Reduced methionine synthase activity traps folate in the methylated pool and limits other folate forms.

Question 12

Assertion: Every water-soluble vitamin has only negligible body stores. Reason: Cobalamin can be retained in the liver.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

D. B12 provides a major exception to the proposed absolute rule; its substantial reserves can delay deficiency.

25. Part C: quantitative and analytical reasoning

Assume the stated conditions. Track carbon, cofactor chemistry and tissue context before calculating.

Question 1

A dialysed enzyme extract has activity 20 units. Addition of NAD⁺ yields 75, FAD 22, PLP 21, and buffer 20. Which conclusion is best?

A. NAD⁺ availability was a likely limitation; B. All enzymes use NAD⁺; C. FAD is absent from all proteins; D. Dialysis proves a genetic defect

Answer and explanation

A. NAD⁺ rescue is selective under the given conditions. The other choices extrapolate from one assay to all proteins or infer a genetic cause without evidence.

Question 2

Transketolase activity is 36 units without TPP and 54 with TPP. Find the activation coefficient and percent rise.

A. 0.67 and 50%; B. 1.5 and 50%; C. 1.5 and 150%; D. 2 and 18%

Answer and explanation

B. 54/36=1.5. (54−36)/36 ×100=50%. 150% is the supplemented activity relative to baseline, not the increase.

Question 3

At 298 K and fixed pH, NAD⁺/NADH rises from 5 to 50. What is the approximate shift in potential?

A. −29.6 mV; B. +29.6 mV; C. +59.2 mV; D. 0 mV

Answer and explanation

B. ΔE′=(RT/2F)ln(50/5)=0.0296 V. The positive sign reflects greater oxidant availability. C incorrectly uses one electron.

Question 4

A cell oxidizes 3 pyruvate molecules completely via PDH and TCA. Use 2.5 ATP/NADH and 1.5 ATP/FAD-linked pair. Ignore transport costs. What is the ATP equivalent?

A. 30; B. 36; C. 37.5; D. 45

Answer and explanation

C. Per pyruvate, 4 NADH, 1 FAD-linked pair, 1 GTP give 12.5; multiplying by 3 gives 37.5. The other totals miscount reducing equivalents or omit GTP.

Question 5

A cell must synthesize two palmitate molecules. How many malonyl-CoA-producing carboxylations and NADPH molecules are required?

A. 14 and 28; B. 16 and 16; C. 7 and 28; D. 14 and 14

Answer and explanation

A. Each palmitate needs seven malonyl units and fourteen NADPH, so two require fourteen and twenty-eight. The acetyl starter does not require malonyl activation.

Question 6

If a cell needs 28 NADPH exclusively from single-pass oxidative PPP, how many G6P molecules enter and how many CO₂ molecules result?

A. 28, 28; B. 14, 14; C. 7, 28; D. 14, 28

Answer and explanation

B. Each G6P yields two NADPH and one CO₂. Divide 28 by 2 to obtain 14 passages; the 14 CO₂ follow the 1:1 carbon-loss ratio.

Question 7

A cell synthesizes 120 dTMP via the serine–THF–DHFR cycle. How many NADPH molecules are consumed by DHFR?

A. 60; B. 120; C. 240; D. 0

Answer and explanation

B. Each thymidylate synthesis converts one methylene-THF to DHF, and one NADPH reduces one DHF to THF. This excludes additional NADPH costs elsewhere.

Question 8

Group X has elevated homocysteine and methylmalonate; group Y has elevated homocysteine but no methylmalonate rise. Which explanation fits best, all else equal?

A. X folate, Y B12; B. X B12, Y folate; C. Both isolated B7; D. X vitamin D, Y K

Answer and explanation

B. Both folate and B12 can affect methyl transfer, but only B12 directly affects mutase among those choices. Other causes of these markers exist, so this is not a standalone clinical diagnosis.

Question 9

Cells lack functional methionine synthase but have normal methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Which change is expected?

A. Reduced THF regeneration from methyl-THF; B. Direct block of succinate dehydrogenase; C. Loss of all CoA; D. Direct loss of retinal binding

Answer and explanation

A. The methyl-folate trap restricts usable folates. The other reactions use unrelated cofactors, and mutase remains functional by premise.

Question 10

Three samples have ATP/ADP/AMP in mM: X=8/1/1, Y=4/4/2, Z=2/3/5. Rank adenylate energy charge.

A. Z>Y>X; B. X>Y>Z; C. Y>X>Z; D. All equal

Answer and explanation

B. X=(8+0.5)/10=0.85; Y=(4+2)/10=0.60; Z=(2+1.5)/10=0.35. Equal total adenylate pools do not imply equal phosphorylation states.

Question 11

A hepatocyte has high acetyl-CoA and low malonyl-CoA during fasting. Which combination is most consistent?

A. Inhibited CPT I and low pyruvate carboxylase; B. Increased CPT I access and activation of pyruvate carboxylase; C. Direct net glucose from acetyl-CoA; D. Increased fatty-acid synthesis solely because acetyl-CoA is high

Answer and explanation

B. Low malonyl-CoA relieves CPT I inhibition, and acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase. C violates mammalian net carbon accounting; D ignores reciprocal regulation.

Question 12

Two glucose molecules complete an idealized Cori cycle. What is the net whole-body ATP-equivalent expenditure?

A. 4; B. 8; C. 12; D. 0

Answer and explanation

B. Per glucose, peripheral glycolysis gains two ATP and hepatic gluconeogenesis spends six equivalents: net cost four. Two cycles cost eight. Twelve is the gross hepatic cost.

Question 13

An experiment supplies labelled carbon-1 glucose or carbon-6 glucose and measures initial labelled CO₂ production. Carbon-1 yields much more label early. Which pathway is implicated?

A. Oxidative PPP; B. Glycogen storage exclusively; C. Direct conversion to lactate only; D. Protein translation

Answer and explanation

A. The oxidative PPP releases carbon 1 during the initial passage. Glycogen and lactate formation do not release CO₂, and translation does not explain position-specific glucose decarboxylation. Later metabolism complicates interpretation.

Question 14

A membrane model has equal PUFA content and identical radical initiation. Tocopherol addition decreases the rate of new lipid peroxyl radical formation but hydroperoxides remain. What follows?

A. Tocopherol directly removes every hydroperoxide; B. Chain termination and hydroperoxide removal are distinct; C. Tocopherol makes ATP; D. No antioxidant effect occurred

Answer and explanation

B. Tocopherol intercepts chain-propagating radicals; hydroperoxides require other removal systems. The remaining hydroperoxides do not negate the reduced propagation.

Question 15

A liver cell secretes normal quantities of a clotting protein that cannot bind Ca²⁺-dependent phospholipid surfaces efficiently. Which defect is plausible?

A. Reduced vitamin K-dependent carboxylation; B. Excess retinal isomerization; C. Defective glycolytic ATP yield alone; D. Lack of FMN at complex I directly removes Gla

Answer and explanation

A. Inadequate Gla formation reduces calcium-mediated membrane binding even when polypeptide abundance is normal. The other changes do not specifically explain this modification defect.

Question 16

In a renal 1α-hydroxylase-deficient model with preserved hepatic 25-hydroxylation, which metabolite pattern is most directly predicted?

A. Normal 25-OH D may coexist with low calcitriol; B. Both must be zero; C. Retinal accumulates obligatorily; D. Calcitriol rises independently of activation

Answer and explanation

A. The blocked conversion is downstream of 25-OH D, so precursor supply can remain adequate while active hormone falls. B and D ignore the specific lesion; C is unrelated.

Question 17

Glucose enters blood at 4 units/min from gut and 10 from liver; peripheral removal is 14. Gut entry stops. If liver output increases to 12 and removal stays 14, what is the initial net change?

A. +2; B. −2; C. −4; D. 0 units/min

Answer and explanation

B. After gut delivery stops, net input is 12−14=−2 units/min. The earlier pool was stationary, but increased liver production has not fully compensated.

26. Consolidation: connect chemical structure to metabolic behaviour

Vitamins are required in small amounts because their major roles are catalytic, regulatory or protective, rather than bulk fuel supply. Their chemical structures explain why the active derivatives can stabilize reactive intermediates, transfer reducing equivalents or groups, or interact with receptors and proteins. Each reaction depends on more than the vitamin alone: a functional enzyme, suitable substrate, appropriate compartment, and a way to regenerate or replace the active form are necessary.

The strongest examination reasoning starts with the reaction chemistry. An α-keto acid decarboxylation suggests TPP; electron transfer through a flavoprotein suggests FMN or FAD; hydride transfer suggests NAD(P); acyl thioesters suggest CoA; amino acid Schiff-base chemistry suggests PLP; ATP-dependent bicarbonate transfer to a metabolic substrate suggests biotin. One-carbon transfer among nucleotide precursors suggests THF, while methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase identify the two active cobalamin forms. For non-B vitamins, distinguish collagen hydroxylation support, retinal chromophore, retinoic acid and calcitriol signalling, lipid radical termination and vitamin K-dependent Gla formation.

The same causal logic applies to the integrated metabolic state. Identify carbon entry points, the supply and demand for ATP and reducing equivalents, and the fate of nitrogen. Then identify the organ and its enzyme repertoire. The liver can release glucose and ketones; muscle primarily stores glucose for its own use; adipose buffers lipid; erythrocytes lack mitochondria; brain substrate preference adapts during prolonged fasting. Finally account for hormonal and intracellular regulation rather than applying a whole-body generalization to every cell.

Unit 1 has traced how atoms and bonds give rise to biomolecular structures, how molecular conformation enables catalysis, and how reactions exchange carbon, electrons and energy. These chemical events take place in spatially organized cells. The inner mitochondrial membrane sustains respiratory gradients, membrane transporters delimit substrate access, and organelles house specific reaction systems. Unit 2 develops this physical organization of metabolism, beginning with cell walls, membranes and transport. Understanding the chemistry in this unit will make those structural distinctions biologically meaningful.
