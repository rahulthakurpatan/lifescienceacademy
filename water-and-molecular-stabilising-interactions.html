1. Why molecular stability matters

Imagine placing a small amount of salt, sugar and oil in three glasses of water. The salt crystals disappear, sugar forms a clear solution, and oil separates into droplets. All three substances are made of atoms connected through chemical interactions, yet their behaviour is remarkably different. The explanation is not simply whether a molecule contains carbon, oxygen or hydrogen. It depends on what happens when its surface encounters water, how strongly molecules attract one another, and how those interactions change as the molecules move closer together. A cell faces the same problem, but on the scale of nanometres and with thousands of different molecules sharing one environment.

In the previous chapter, Atomic Structure and Chemical Bonding, we learned how electrons determine molecular shape, charge distribution and covalent connectivity. A covalent bond can keep two atoms joined within a molecule. That is indispensable, but covalent connectivity alone cannot explain why a polypeptide folds, why two DNA strands associate selectively, or why a membrane assembles in water without a new covalent bond joining every lipid to its neighbour. We now need the rules by which existing molecules recognise, attract, repel and organise each other.

Permanent identity versus reversible organisation

A useful distinction is between the covalent framework of a molecule and the interactions that help determine its three-dimensional organisation. Breaking an ordinary carbon–carbon or peptide bond changes covalent connectivity. Separating two hydrogen-bonded groups generally does not. The latter kind of change can be reversed when favourable conditions return. That reversibility is essential: a receptor must bind and release ligands, DNA must be accessible to copying machinery, and enzyme substrates must eventually leave the active site. If every biological association required a strong permanent bond, a cell would struggle to reorganise itself.

Noncovalent does not mean insignificant. A protein surface can contact another protein through dozens of complementary atom pairs. A small energetic preference repeated over many contacts may become a very substantial preference for the assembled state. Nevertheless, one cannot simply add the energies of all contacts visible in a structure. The groups may already interact with water before binding, and they may lose conformational freedom after binding. The stability of an association is the difference between complete initial and final states, including the solvent.

Thermal motion and the meaning of stability

At ordinary temperatures molecules move, rotate, vibrate and collide. For a single molecule the thermal energy scale is kBT; for a mole it is RT, where kB is the Boltzmann constant, R is the molar gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, RT is approximately 2.48 kJ mol⁻¹. This is not a fixed energy possessed by every molecule, nor an automatic threshold at which a bond breaks. It is a useful scale for judging how strongly thermal fluctuations compete with energetic preferences. Weak interactions may rearrange continuously even in a stable macromolecule.

At constant temperature and pressure a process is thermodynamically favoured in the direction of decreasing Gibbs free energy, G. We will use the relation ΔG = ΔH − TΔS as a working tool in this chapter. ΔH represents an enthalpy change and ΔS an entropy change of the system defined for the process. The full treatment of equilibrium, standard states and spontaneity belongs to 1.A.05. Here our goal is to understand why the environment can change molecular stability. In particular, a favourable direct interaction does not guarantee favourable association, and a favourable association does not imply that assembly is instantaneous.

The learning journey begins with the shape of a single water molecule, then moves to water networks and solvent behaviour. From there we can derive electrostatic interactions and examine hydrogen bonds, dispersion, packing and hydrophobic association. Finally we will combine all these ideas to predict the behaviour of real biological structures.

2. Molecular structure, polarity and dipole moment of water

Oxygen has six valence electrons and hydrogen has one. In the Lewis description of H₂O, oxygen shares one pair of electrons with each of two hydrogen atoms and retains two lone pairs. There are consequently four electron domains around oxygen. The electron-domain arrangement is approximately tetrahedral, whereas the molecular shape, which counts atomic positions rather than lone-pair positions, is bent. VSEPR describes the observed bond angle as smaller than the ideal tetrahedral angle partly because of the different spatial demands of lone pairs and bonding pairs. The gas-phase H–O–H angle is about 104.5° and the O–H bond length is about 0.096 nm. These values describe an isolated molecule; thermal motion and local interactions cause fluctuations in liquid water.

Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so electron density within each O–H bond is displaced toward oxygen. We represent the resulting partial charges by δ− on oxygen and δ+ on hydrogen. Partial charges are not full ionic charges. In neutral water there are still ten electrons and ten protons in total; the electron distribution is unequal, not the total charge. If we replaced water with a hypothetical molecule with two equal but oppositely directed bond dipoles, those dipoles would cancel. The bent geometry prevents this cancellation. The result is a permanent molecular dipole.

μ = qd (for a simple pair of opposite point charges); μnet = 2μbond cos(θ/2) (for two equal bond dipoles).

In the first relation q is the magnitude of either charge in coulombs, d is their separation in metres, and μ is the magnitude of the electric dipole moment in C m. Dipole moments are often expressed in debye (D), where 1 D ≈ 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. In the second relation θ is the angle between two equal bond dipole vectors; their components perpendicular to the molecular bisector cancel while the components along the bisector add. This is a simplified vector model, not a complete electron-density calculation. The measured isolated water dipole is about 1.85 D; effective dipoles in liquid water depend on the surrounding molecules and the model used.

Worked example: geometry determines polarity

Suppose each of two identical bond dipoles has magnitude 1.50 D and their angle is 104.5°. Their resultant is 2 × 1.50 × cos(52.25°) ≈ 1.84 D. If the angle instead becomes 180°, the resultant is zero even though each bond remains polar. This explains the contrast between bent H₂O and linear CO₂: polar bonds do not necessarily produce a polar molecule. The calculation is illustrative, not an independent measurement of individual O–H dipoles.

Water's polarity provides two complementary ways to interact with other substances. The oxygen-rich region attracts positive charges and can accept hydrogen bonds, while the hydrogen-rich regions can orient toward negative charges and act as hydrogen-bond donors. Water also responds to an electric field by partially reorienting its dipoles. These microscopic properties explain both hydration and dielectric screening, but neither requires water molecules to split into separate ions.

A useful comparison is H₂S, another bent molecule with a central atom from the oxygen group. It is much less effective at forming the extensive strong directional hydrogen-bond networks characteristic of water. Although H₂S is heavier than H₂O, it has a much lower boiling point at ordinary pressure. Molecular mass alone therefore cannot predict physical properties: we must also account for the nature and number of intermolecular contacts. This observation leads directly to the collective behaviour of water.

3. Hydrogen-bond networks: from a single molecule to liquid water

The partial positive charge on a hydrogen atom attached to oxygen can be attracted to the electron-rich region of a neighbouring oxygen. A directional contact of this kind is a hydrogen bond. The oxygen–hydrogen covalent bond within the donor molecule remains intact during ordinary hydrogen-bond rearrangements. We write the contact as O–H···O, using a solid line for the covalent bond and dots for the intermolecular interaction. This difference matters when explaining evaporation, freezing and protein denaturation: these processes need not break ordinary covalent O–H bonds.

An isolated water molecule has two hydrogens available to donate and approximately two lone-pair directions available to accept hydrogen bonds. In an ideal tetrahedral network it can therefore participate in four hydrogen bonds, two as donor and two as acceptor. This is a useful model for ordinary ice, but do not imagine every liquid molecule permanently attached to four neighbours. In liquid water some bonds are distorted or absent, and the network continually breaks and reforms. The exact average count depends on temperature and how a hydrogen bond is defined.

Hydrogen-bond exchange occurs on very short molecular timescales, often described on the picosecond scale. The water molecules nevertheless remain in the liquid because the loss of one contact can be followed by formation of another. A liquid can thus have extensive local structure without possessing the long-range positional order of a crystal. This combination of mobility and local organisation allows water to flow through tissues, dissolve polar compounds, reorganise around a protein and still maintain cohesive bulk properties.

How do we know the network is real?

Several independent observations point toward this picture. Infrared spectra reveal that the O–H stretching vibration changes as a donor becomes hydrogen bonded: the band commonly shifts to lower frequency and becomes broad when many slightly different environments are present. Diffraction measurements can give distributions of distances between oxygen atoms, distinguishing the more organised arrangement in ice from the fluctuating arrangement in liquid. Neither measurement requires treating the hydrogen bonds as permanent links. Together with the unusual boiling point and thermal properties, they support a molecular model in which interactions constantly rearrange.

Heating increases the importance of thermal motion, and the typical number and geometry of favourable contacts change. Cooling generally promotes more extensive local hydrogen-bond order until freezing creates a crystal. It would be misleading to say that heating liquid water simply destroys all its hydrogen bonds: liquid water at high temperatures still has intermolecular attractions. The transition to vapour separates molecules much more completely, yet the covalent bonds within ordinary vapour molecules remain intact.

This network is especially important at interfaces. A water molecule in the interior of a large liquid sample has neighbours in many directions; a water molecule next to an air bubble or a nonpolar molecular surface encounters a different set of possible partners. The response is local rearrangement of the hydrogen-bond network, not a conscious movement away from the interface. This is the molecular foundation for surface tension and for the solvent-mediated association of hydrophobic groups discussed later.

4. Physical properties of water and their biological consequences

Density anomaly: why ice floats

Most substances become denser as their liquids cool toward freezing because reduced thermal motion permits closer packing. Water also contracts on cooling across much of its liquid range. However, at ordinary pressure pure water reaches maximum density near 4 °C. Below this temperature the growing preference for more open hydrogen-bonded arrangements begins to dominate ordinary thermal contraction. In common ice (ice Ih), approximately tetrahedral coordination creates an open crystal structure with more volume per molecule than liquid water. Solid water is therefore less dense than the liquid near its freezing point.

This effect has a familiar ecological consequence: a freshwater lake can freeze from the surface while water below remains liquid. As surface water cools to around 4 °C it tends to sink, but further cooling produces less dense water that remains near the top. The resulting ice cover can reduce heat exchange with the cold air and protect organisms below. Salinity, pressure, mixing and depth affect the behaviour of natural water bodies, so the 4 °C maximum should not be applied to every aqueous solution without qualification.

Specific heat and latent heat

Specific heat capacity is the heat required per unit mass to raise a substance's temperature by one degree. For water near room temperature it is approximately 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. When thermal energy enters liquid water, molecular motion and the distribution of intermolecular arrangements both respond. The attractive network contributes to water's relatively high heat capacity. Consequently, an aqueous biological system generally changes temperature less than many other materials for the same mass and heat input.

Q = mcΔT; Q = mL for a phase transition at fixed temperature.

Q is heat transferred in joules, m is mass in grams, c is specific heat in J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, and ΔT is the temperature change in kelvin or degrees Celsius. L is the specific latent heat in J g⁻¹ for the relevant transition. These relations assume the stated heat capacity or latent heat applies under the specified conditions and that heat losses and other processes are neglected. Heating within a single phase and changing phase are different operations; during a phase change at equilibrium, energy changes molecular arrangement without raising temperature.

Worked example: thermal buffering and evaporation

Heating 100 g of water from 25 to 30 °C requires 100 × 4.18 × 5 = 2090 J. If a different 100 g liquid has c = 2.0 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, the same input raises its temperature by about 10.45 K. Water thus buffers the temperature change more effectively. Evaporating 1.0 g of water near room temperature instead takes roughly 2.4 kJ. If that heat came entirely from 100 g of remaining water, the idealised cooling would be about 5.7 K. Actual sweating and transpiration involve additional heat transfer, replenishment and humidity effects.

Water's high enthalpy of vaporisation reflects the energy required to take a molecule out of the attractive liquid environment. Evaporation preferentially removes molecules able to escape, and the remaining material can cool unless heat is supplied from elsewhere. Sweat evaporation and leaf transpiration exploit this principle. Sweat that drips off without evaporating is less effective at evaporative cooling. At high humidity the driving force for evaporation decreases. These are physiological applications of a molecular property rather than separate chemical effects.

Cohesion, adhesion, surface tension and capillarity

Cohesion means attraction among molecules of the same substance; adhesion refers to attraction between unlike substances or interfaces. Water's cohesive contacts help maintain a continuous liquid column, while its adhesion to a polar wall favours wetting. Surface tension is the reversible work required per unit increase of interfacial area at fixed relevant conditions. It can also be expressed as a force per unit length. The water–air interface near room temperature has surface tension around 0.072 N m⁻¹. Creating additional surface costs free energy because the local environment of water changes. A droplet therefore tends toward a shape with less area, if gravity and interactions with surrounding surfaces do not dominate.

ΔG ≈ γΔA; h = 2γ cos θ/(ρgr).

The first expression estimates the free-energy cost of increasing area ΔA in m² for a constant surface tension γ in N m⁻¹ (equivalently J m⁻²). The second is the equilibrium height h of a liquid in an ideal vertical cylindrical capillary of radius r, assuming hydrostatic balance, negligible evaporation, a uniform contact angle θ and liquid density ρ. To see its origin, the upward vertical component of surface tension is 2πrγ cos θ, while the weight of the column is ρgπr²h. Equating these gives the equation. g is gravitational acceleration. For a wetting surface θ is less than 90°, and the height increases as radius decreases.

Worked example: a thin wetting tube

With r = 0.10 mm = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m, γ = 0.072 N m⁻¹, ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³, g = 9.81 m s⁻² and θ ≈ 0°, h = 0.144/0.981 ≈ 0.147 m. Halving the radius doubles the predicted height. This example does not explain water reaching the tops of tall trees by capillarity alone. Xylem transport relies mainly on transpiration-driven tension, cohesion and the hydraulic properties of the pathway, developed in Unit 6.

Viscosity measures resistance to flow, and is distinct from surface tension. Water has sufficient intermolecular attraction to be cohesive, yet its small molecules and mobile network permit relatively easy flow. Increasing temperature usually decreases liquid water's viscosity. Solutes, polymers and particles can change viscosity markedly; blood and cytoplasm cannot always be modelled as pure water. The important lesson is that one molecular network produces several bulk properties, but those properties describe different physical responses.

5. Hydration, solvation and molecular solubility

We can now return to the salt, sugar and oil in the opening observation. Dissolving a substance is a competition between the interactions it has before mixing and those available in the resulting solution. The surrounding solvent molecules reorganise around each dissolved particle. This process is called solvation; when the solvent is water it is called hydration. A hydration shell is the region where the nearby water orientations, positions or dynamics are influenced by the solute. Such a shell exchanges molecules with bulk water and is not a rigid protective membrane.

Ions and the competition with a crystal lattice

In sodium chloride, oppositely charged ions attract within a crystal. To dissolve the salt, these interactions must be replaced by interactions with water and other ions in the solution. Water orients oxygen toward Na⁺ and its hydrogen-rich region toward Cl⁻. The resulting ion–dipole attractions can make hydration energetically favourable. Dispersion of ions through the available volume can also contribute favourable entropy, although local solvent ordering around an ion may counteract part of that gain. Dissolution is favourable when the total free-energy change under the given conditions is negative.

It is important not to infer that all ionic compounds are highly soluble. A crystal with particularly favourable lattice interactions may not be compensated adequately by hydration and mixing. Calcium carbonate and many other sparingly soluble salts illustrate this point. Solubility is an equilibrium property, not a direct measurement of the strength of a single ionic interaction. A solution can also become saturated, at which point further net dissolution of the solid is not favoured under those conditions.

For ions of the same charge, smaller ionic radius generally gives higher charge density and stronger hydration. Mg²⁺ is particularly strongly hydrated compared with a larger monovalent ion such as K⁺. Its high charge density orients water strongly and often makes dehydration costly. A biological ion-binding site must compensate for any lost favourable hydration, for example using appropriately arranged carbonyl oxygen atoms or carboxylate groups. The size of the bare ion is therefore only part of the story; its surrounding solvent and the geometry of the binding site also matter.

Polar neutral molecules and nonpolar surfaces

Sugar dissolves without normally being converted into free ions. Its many hydroxyl groups provide donor and acceptor sites for water. In favourable circumstances solute–water contacts replace solute–solute and water–water contacts while allowing mixing. Ethanol has both a polar hydroxyl and a short nonpolar carbon chain; the hydroxyl makes water interactions sufficiently favourable for complete mixing under ordinary conditions. Longer hydrocarbon chains attached to an analogous hydroxyl group tend to reduce water solubility, because an increasingly large nonpolar surface must be accommodated by the solvent. Molecular size, shape, crystal stability and temperature complicate this trend, so simply counting polar groups is not enough to predict exact solubility.

Oil consists largely of nonpolar molecular surfaces. Water and hydrocarbons do interact through dispersion forces, so it is incorrect to say that they have no attraction whatsoever. However, a hydrocarbon cannot generally provide the same favourable directional hydrogen-bond opportunities as another water molecule. Accommodating many exposed nonpolar surfaces changes solvent organisation and has a free-energy cost. When oil molecules collect into a droplet, less total nonpolar area contacts water. The underlying hydrophobic effect will be analysed in detail after we develop the other interactions.

Solvent exchange and desolvation

When a ligand binds a protein pocket, both the ligand and the pocket initially have solvent contacts. Some water may remain in the interface and bridge groups; other water is released. The free-energy contribution of removing solvent from a site is often called desolvation. Removing a strongly hydrated charge can be costly. Removing water from a poorly accommodating nonpolar pocket can be favourable. The net binding result depends on what replaces those contacts and how much freedom the interacting partners and solvent gain or lose.

Consider an enzyme whose binding pocket contains a positively charged side chain. A negatively charged ligand may interact strongly with that group, but a chemically similar neutral ligand could still bind well if it has better packing or pays less desolvation cost. We must compare the actual solvated unbound molecules to the solvated complex, not isolated charged groups. This distinction explains why an attractive interaction measured in a vacuum is usually a poor predictor of affinity in an aqueous cell.

Hydration is also responsible for many effects of dissolved salts on biological samples. At modest concentrations salt can reduce unfavourable electrostatic contacts between protein molecules and improve solubility, whereas sufficiently high concentrations of certain salts can promote precipitation by changing protein–water interactions. These are commonly called salting-in and salting-out respectively. The response depends on the protein, salt identity, pH and concentration. Their experimental use in purification is studied later; the central point here is that the solvent participates actively in molecular stability.

6. Electrostatic interactions: force, energy and distance

Charged particles exert forces on each other. A positive charge and a negative charge attract, whereas two charges of the same sign repel. Electrostatic interaction is not restricted to ions floating freely in water. At ordinary biological pH, many protein carboxylate groups carry negative charge, lysine and arginine side chains commonly carry positive charge, and nucleic acids carry negatively charged phosphate groups. The strength and distribution of these interactions influence how macromolecules approach each other and which configurations they prefer. We begin with an ideal pair of point charges before introducing solvent and salt.

Fr = q₁q₂/(4πε₀εrr²); U(r) = q₁q₂/(4πε₀εrr).

Here q₁ and q₂ are signed charges in coulombs, r is their centre-to-centre separation in metres, ε₀ is vacuum permittivity (approximately 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C² J⁻¹ m⁻¹), and εr is the relative permittivity of an assumed uniform linear dielectric medium. The radial force Fr in newtons is positive for repulsion and negative for attraction if the positive direction points toward increasing separation. U is the potential energy in joules for a single pair, defined to be zero at infinite separation. The force varies with inverse square distance, but energy varies with inverse distance. They are not interchangeable.

Why does energy fall off more slowly than force?

The change in potential energy is minus the work done by the force. To find U(r), integrate −Fr dr from infinity to r. Because the integral of 1/r² is −1/r, the result is proportional to 1/r. For opposite charges, q₁q₂ is negative, and bringing them closer lowers potential energy. For equal signs, bringing them closer requires work and increases potential energy. The equations are idealised: at very short distances electron-cloud overlap, specific hydration and finite size become important. We will introduce short-range repulsion later.

For biochemical calculations, it is often convenient to express charges as z times the elementary charge e, where e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and z may be a signed integer or a model partial charge. Multiplying the pair energy by Avogadro's constant and converting distance into nanometres yields a practical molar form. The numerical coefficient is approximately 138.94 kJ mol⁻¹ nm, obtained from NAe²/(4πε₀) and the unit conversions. It applies to a pair of charges in the assumed continuum, not to the total binding free energy of a protein.

U (kJ mol⁻¹) ≈ 138.94 z₁z₂/(εr rnm).

Worked example: a monovalent charge pair

For z₁ = +1, z₂ = −1, and r = 0.40 nm, U in vacuum is −138.94/0.40 = −347.35 kJ mol⁻¹. In bulk water approximated by εr = 78, U = −138.94/(78 × 0.40) ≈ −4.45 kJ mol⁻¹. In an illustrative uniform medium with εr = 4 it is −86.84 kJ mol⁻¹. These are continuum pair energies only. The low-dielectric value does not establish that moving charged groups into a protein core is favourable, because the cost of desolvating those charges has not been included.

If we double r, the magnitude of the force becomes one-quarter of its former value, but the magnitude of potential energy becomes one-half. This difference frequently appears in exam reasoning. Another common mistake is to confuse a negative potential energy with a negative force in every geometry. The force must be determined from the gradient of the potential energy; our sign convention is specifically radial for a pair of charges.

Electrostatic interactions also occur between an ion and a polar neutral molecule, and between molecular dipoles. A positive ion near water attracts the negatively polarised oxygen region. A fixed ion–dipole interaction has an orientation-dependent energy scaling approximately with 1/r² in the point-dipole limit. A fixed dipole–dipole interaction instead scales with 1/r³ and may be attractive or repulsive depending on orientation. These ideal laws describe separated small charge distributions; rotating and solvated molecules require statistical averaging and additional interactions. The following section explains why water is particularly effective at changing the strength of these forces.

7. Dielectric screening, ionic strength and salt bridges

Two distinct screening mechanisms

Imagine placing two opposite charges in water. The field generated by each charge tends to orient surrounding water dipoles in preferred directions. The resulting polarisation modifies the electric field and reduces the electrostatic interaction predicted between the original charges. Relative permittivity is a macroscopic description of this response. It is dimensionless and is close to 78 for bulk liquid water at about 25 °C under static-field conditions, compared with 1 for vacuum. It depends on temperature, frequency of the applied field and composition. This is dielectric screening. It occurs even in water that contains no deliberately added salt.

The environment within a protein or membrane is not a homogeneous block of water. A nonpolar interior generally has less ability to orient polar groups than bulk water does, so a small relative permittivity is sometimes used for simplified models. However, a protein contains peptide dipoles, polar side chains, bound waters and flexible groups. It does not possess one universal microscopic dielectric constant. A surface salt bridge, a buried ion pair and a charge adjacent to a membrane each experience different local environments. The values 4 and 78 in the previous calculation should therefore be interpreted as model assumptions, not measured constants of every protein and water contact.

A second form of screening occurs when mobile ions accumulate preferentially near opposite charges and are depleted near like charges. A negatively charged macromolecule attracts counterions such as Na⁺ and repels co-ions such as Cl⁻. Their average redistribution forms an ionic atmosphere that attenuates the electrostatic potential at a distance. Dielectric screening comes from the solvent's polarisation; ionic screening comes from the redistribution of mobile charges. They occur together in many biological fluids, but increasing salt mainly changes the second mechanism rather than simply making water more polar.

I = ½ Σ cᵢzᵢ²; λD ≈ 0.304/√I nm (water at 25 °C, dilute-solution approximation).

I is ionic strength in mol L⁻¹, cᵢ is the molar concentration of ionic species i and zᵢ is its signed charge number. Neutral molecules do not enter this sum, and the charge is squared, so multivalent ions have a particularly strong influence. The Debye length λD describes a characteristic distance over which the electrostatic potential is attenuated in a dilute electrolyte. The numerical form above assumes bulk water near 25 °C and ionic strength entered in mol L⁻¹. It is derived from linearised electrostatic screening theory; it becomes less reliable close to strongly charged surfaces, for concentrated salts, ion-specific binding, and heterogeneous environments.

Uscreened(r) ≈ [q₁q₂/(4πε₀εrr)] exp(−r/λD).

This expression models a screened point-charge pair in a dilute uniform electrolyte. At r = λD the screened energy is approximately 37% of the corresponding unscreened continuum energy. At a distance much smaller than λD, the exponential factor approaches one; at much larger separation it becomes very small. Thus higher ionic strength shortens the effective range of both attraction and repulsion. The model should not be treated as an exact description of contact ion pairs inside a folded protein.

Worked example: comparing electrolytes

For 0.10 M NaCl, I = ½(0.10 × 1² + 0.10 × 1²) = 0.10 M and λD ≈ 0.96 nm. For 0.10 M CaCl₂, assuming complete dissociation, [Ca²⁺] = 0.10 M and [Cl⁻] = 0.20 M, so I = ½(0.10 × 4 + 0.20 × 1) = 0.30 M and λD ≈ 0.56 nm. For an ideal pair at r = 1.0 nm the exponential factors are about 0.35 and 0.17 respectively. The second solution has stronger ionic screening in this model; specific Ca²⁺ binding may create additional effects not represented by the formula.

Salt bridges in proteins and complexes

A salt bridge is a favourable contact between oppositely charged groups, such as a lysine ammonium group and an aspartate or glutamate carboxylate group. Arginine's guanidinium group can form charge-assisted hydrogen bonds to carboxylates as well. In an exposed salt bridge the groups may retain some hydration; in a buried bridge they may lose much of it. A salt bridge often combines electrostatic attraction with directional hydrogen bonds and steric constraints, so these labels are complementary descriptions rather than fully independent energy terms.

One might expect every salt bridge to stabilise a protein because opposite charges attract. The correct comparison is subtler. The charged groups in the unfolded or separated state may be favourably hydrated. Forming a bridge in the folded state could compensate for some or all of the desolvation cost. A well-oriented pair can contribute to stability or specificity, whereas burying an unpaired charged group is often costly. Rearrangement of nearby residues, altered protonation and loss of side-chain freedom can change the overall result. A mutation that removes a charged group therefore does not necessarily destabilise a protein.

These principles give useful experimental predictions. When increased salt weakens the association between a positively charged protein surface and negatively charged DNA, it supports the participation of electrostatic attraction. But salt can also stabilise a DNA double helix by reducing repulsion between its negatively charged backbones. Thus the same increase in ionic strength can weaken one association and strengthen another. The sign of the interaction being screened and the other interactions in the system must both be considered.

In the next chapter we will study pH and acid–base equilibria, which determine whether particular side chains are charged at a given pH. For now, remember that salt screening changes the effect of existing charges, while changing pH can change the charge states themselves. These are different mechanisms. An experiment that changes pH and ionic strength simultaneously cannot isolate one of them without appropriate controls.

8. Hydrogen bonding: donors, acceptors, geometry and specificity

Electrostatics explains much of the attraction between polar groups, but a hydrogen bond is more geometrically selective than an attraction between spherical point charges. We write it as D–H···A, where D is the donor atom covalently bonded to hydrogen and A is the acceptor atom. In biological molecules D and A are most commonly oxygen or nitrogen, although other atoms and weaker donor groups can participate in suitable environments. The hydrogen-rich end of the polar covalent bond approaches an electron-rich region of the acceptor. Electrostatic attraction, polarisation and some orbital interactions contribute; describing the bond solely as a full transfer of hydrogen or an ordinary covalent bond is incorrect.

Learning to identify donors and acceptors

Do not classify a functional group by its element alone. A donor needs a suitably polar bonded hydrogen; an acceptor needs an available electron-rich site. A carbonyl oxygen accepts because its electron density can interact with a donor hydrogen. It does not donate because it has no directly bonded hydrogen. An alcohol hydroxyl group can both donate through its O–H bond and accept through oxygen. A protonated amine such as R–NH₃⁺ donates but normally cannot accept through nitrogen because that nitrogen no longer has an available lone pair. An unprotonated primary amine can generally act as both donor and acceptor.

The peptide group is a particularly important exception to the rule that nitrogen often accepts. The nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the adjacent carbonyl system by resonance, giving the C–N bond partial double-bond character. The backbone amide nitrogen is not a normal hydrogen-bond acceptor. If it carries hydrogen it can donate; the backbone carbonyl oxygen accepts. The proline residue within a peptide lacks a backbone amide hydrogen on its substituted nitrogen and consequently cannot donate that usual backbone hydrogen bond. This detail affects secondary-structure patterns, although proline can still occur in turns and particular helix positions.

Distance and directionality

For many conventional O/N hydrogen bonds, the donor–acceptor heavy-atom separation is roughly 0.27–0.32 nm, with the hydrogen–acceptor separation often near 0.17–0.22 nm. These are representative distances, not universal criteria. The D–H···A angle frequently approaches 180°, which favours alignment between the donor bond and acceptor region. Bent bonds can still form; their energetic preference depends on the functional groups and local constraints. In a folded macromolecule there may be a trade-off between an ideal hydrogen-bond angle and the best overall geometry of the surrounding structure.

Hydrogen bonds can be intermolecular, joining two molecules, or intramolecular, connecting distant groups of the same molecule. An intramolecular hydrogen bond can stabilise a particular conformation and sometimes reduce exposure of polar groups to solvent. A water-mediated hydrogen bond connects two groups through one or more waters; it can satisfy a pair of sites that would not align for direct contact. Crystallographic models often reveal such water oxygen sites, although ordinary X-ray structures do not necessarily locate every hydrogen precisely. A structural hydrogen bond assigned from geometry is a plausible contact, not a direct calorimetric measurement of its strength.

Why hydrogen bonds do not all have one strength

Typical conventional hydrogen bonds between small molecules can have interaction energies of several to a few tens of kJ mol⁻¹, depending on environment, geometry and charge. This range is not a universal value for their contribution to aqueous folding or binding free energy. Charge-assisted hydrogen bonds can be stronger, and some weak contacts may have much smaller energies. Even when a direct hydrogen bond is favourable, forming it in water may require breaking donor–water and acceptor–water contacts. The resulting free-energy change can be much smaller than the isolated interaction energy.

To make this reasoning concrete, consider a hypothetical donor and acceptor that are separated and hydrated. Imagine that direct association gives an enthalpic contact of −15 kJ mol⁻¹, while removing their previous water contacts and reorganising the solvent costs +12 kJ mol⁻¹ in enthalpy. The net change in enthalpy in this simplified accounting is −3 kJ mol⁻¹ rather than −15. Entropy changes, including solvent release and restricted molecular motion, still need to be included to predict association. The values are illustrative, not fixed properties of hydrogen bonds. In a real system these terms interact and cannot always be measured separately.

Hydrogen bonds in proteins and nucleic acids

A polypeptide contains repeated carbonyl acceptors and amide donors. In an α helix, the carbonyl of residue i commonly accepts a hydrogen bond from the backbone N–H of residue i + 4. In a β sheet, hydrogen bonds connect neighbouring extended strands, whether the strands run parallel or antiparallel. These recurring contacts help satisfy the polar backbone when it is less accessible to water. Individual side chains may also form hydrogen bonds to other residues or solvent. The complete rules of secondary structure belong to 1.C.02, but the underlying principle is the directional complementarity of donors and acceptors.

Watson–Crick A–T pairs have two conventional interbase hydrogen bonds, and G–C pairs have three. In RNA the analogous A–U pair ordinarily has two. The arrangement of donor and acceptor sites helps complementary bases recognise each other. This is one reason hydrogen bonds contribute to the specificity of nucleic acid pairing. Yet the double helix also contains stacked aromatic bases, negatively charged backbones, associated counterions and structured hydration. A G–C-rich duplex often has higher melting temperature under comparable conditions, but base sequence, nearest-neighbour stacking, length, strand concentration and salt also matter. Counting hydrogen bonds alone does not calculate melting temperature or overall stability.

Evidence, perturbation and exam reasoning

Infrared spectroscopy provides evidence about changes in bond vibrations and their environment. A change in an O–H or N–H stretching band upon association can support hydrogen bonding, but other structural changes may also shift bands. An H/D exchange experiment measures how readily certain hydrogens exchange with deuterium from solvent. In proteins, backbone amide exchange can become slow when a site is protected by stable structure, hydrogen bonding or limited access to solvent. A slow rate is evidence of protection, not by itself proof that a particular donor–acceptor pair has a given bond energy.

A related strategy is to replace a donor or acceptor with a chemically similar group while monitoring binding or folding. If an otherwise similar substitution removes a donor and reduces affinity, it supports a role for that site. But the mutation may also alter hydration, shape or charge, so a strong conclusion requires comparison with controls and structural evidence. NET questions often test this logic by giving a mutation, a structural observation and a binding measurement, then asking which interpretation follows without overclaiming. The same style of reasoning applies to every stabilising interaction in this chapter.

9. Van der Waals interactions: permanent and fluctuating dipoles

Not all attractions require net charge or hydrogen-bond donors. Even two neutral nonpolar atoms, such as neighbouring noble-gas atoms, attract weakly at appropriate separation. The attraction becomes understandable when we remember that electron density is not a frozen rigid shell. The electrons respond to other charges and undergo quantum fluctuations. Traditionally, the van der Waals attractions are divided into interactions of permanent dipoles, permanent dipoles with induced dipoles, and fluctuating dipoles. The term is also used in molecular mechanics for the combined short-range dispersion and repulsion potential, so always inspect the definition used in a question.

Keesom: permanent dipole with permanent dipole

Two molecules with permanent dipoles can orient positive regions toward negative regions. A fixed head-to-tail configuration can be attractive, while another orientation may be repulsive. The interaction of fixed ideal dipoles varies as 1/r³ multiplied by an angular factor. In a freely rotating pair at thermal equilibrium, favourable orientations occur with slightly higher probability. Under the weak-interaction, freely rotating approximation, the orientationally averaged interaction energy has an attractive 1/r⁶ dependence and depends inversely on temperature. These are different statements about different conditions, not inconsistent distance laws. In a protein, dipoles constrained by the backbone may not rotate freely, so the fixed-geometry description may be more appropriate.

Debye: permanent dipole with induced dipole

When a polar molecule approaches a neutral but polarisable molecule, its electric field can distort the neighbour's electron density. This creates an induced dipole. Polarisability describes how readily the electron distribution is distorted by an electric field. Larger and more diffuse electron clouds are often more polarisable than compact clouds, though shape and electronic structure matter. In the ideal point-dipole approximation the field decreases as 1/r³, and the energy associated with inducing a dipole is proportional to the square of that field. The resulting induction attraction has a 1/r⁶ dependence. It can occur between a polar group and an aromatic or hydrocarbon surface without a conventional hydrogen bond.

London dispersion: fluctuating dipole with induced dipole

Even a molecule with no permanent dipole has quantum fluctuations in its electron density. Correlated fluctuations in a neighbouring molecule lower the energy of the pair at appropriate distances. This London dispersion interaction exists between all atoms and molecules, including polar and charged groups. It is not restricted to hydrocarbons, although it is especially important for understanding attractions between nonpolar surfaces. For two separated atoms in the ordinary nonretarded regime, the leading attractive energy is commonly written −C₆/r⁶, where C₆ is positive and depends on the interacting partners. If r doubles, this ideal attraction falls to one sixty-fourth of its initial magnitude.

A standard observation supporting this principle is the rise in boiling points from fluorine and chlorine to bromine and iodine. These elemental diatomic molecules are nonpolar, yet they condense at different temperatures. Increasing electronic polarisability strengthens dispersion in the heavier species. Molecular surface geometry also matters: extended hydrocarbons may pack and contact neighbours differently from compact branched isomers, contributing to differences in their physical properties. These are collective condensed-phase observations; they do not imply that each atom pair has exactly the same interaction energy.

10. Short-range repulsion, molecular packing and Lennard–Jones potential

If attraction simply increased indefinitely as atoms approached, matter would collapse into arbitrarily dense arrangements. Instead, approaching atoms eventually encounter a very steep rise in energy as their electron clouds overlap. The Pauli exclusion principle restricts the allowed electronic states of identical electrons, and the reorganisation required at close approach produces strong exchange repulsion. Other electrostatic terms are also present. In a simple molecular mechanics model, short-range repulsion is represented by a steep empirical term. A widely used illustrative potential combines it with the attractive London dispersion term.

U(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶].

Here r is the distance between two sites, σ is a length parameter at which U crosses zero, and ε is a positive energy scale representing the depth of the potential well. This ε is unrelated to relative permittivity εr used for electrostatics. If ε is specified in kJ mol⁻¹, U is a molar pair energy; r and σ must use the same length units. The positive inverse twelfth-power term gives a steep repulsive wall, while the negative inverse sixth-power term describes dispersion. The power 12 is a convenient empirical approximation, not a fundamental law of Pauli repulsion.

Deriving the most favourable distance

At r = σ, both powers equal one and the potential is zero. This is not the equilibrium separation. To locate the minimum, differentiate U with respect to r and set the derivative equal to zero. The derivative is 4ε[−12σ¹²/r¹³ + 6σ⁶/r⁷]. Rearranging the zero derivative gives r⁶ = 2σ⁶; therefore rmin = 21/6σ ≈ 1.122σ. Substituting this distance yields (σ/r)⁶ = 1/2 and (σ/r)¹² = 1/4, so the energy is 4ε(1/4 − 1/2) = −ε. The force is minus the derivative; it vanishes at the potential minimum. At greater separation the potential approaches zero from below, while at smaller separation the repulsive term rapidly dominates.

Worked example: packing distance

Suppose σ = 0.32 nm and ε = 0.80 kJ mol⁻¹. The minimum occurs at 1.122 × 0.32 ≈ 0.359 nm, with U = −0.80 kJ mol⁻¹. At r = 2σ, U = 3.20[(1/2)¹² − (1/2)⁶] ≈ −0.0492 kJ mol⁻¹. At r = 0.90σ, the same formula gives about +5.31 kJ mol⁻¹. The large positive value at the shorter distance illustrates why forcing atoms into an undersized cavity can be destabilising even though dispersion attraction increases as separation falls.

Van der Waals radii, steric clashes and biological packing

A van der Waals radius is a conventional measure related to the closest typical nonbonded contact of an atom. It is not the same as a covalent radius. The sum of two van der Waals radii provides a useful approximate contact distance for those atoms, but exact preferred distances depend on the partners and their environment. A distance substantially shorter than expected can indicate a steric clash, an erroneous structural model, or a special contact not captured by a simple radius rule. It is not appropriate to apply the nonbonded radius rule to atoms directly joined by covalent bonds.

A folded protein usually contains an interior in which many nonpolar side chains pack against each other. Replacing a large hydrophobic residue with a much smaller one may create a cavity, sacrificing favourable dispersion contacts and altering the amount of buried nonpolar surface. Replacing a small residue with an oversized one may produce repulsive strain or force local rearrangement. Either mutation can destabilise the fold, even when both amino acids are hydrophobic. This is why the hydrophobic effect alone cannot select every side-chain position of a protein's native structure.

The same balance applies to molecular recognition. An enzyme pocket that is geometrically complementary to a ligand can make many close favourable contacts without severe overlap. A ligand with a slightly different shape may lose these interactions or encounter steric repulsion. But good packing still does not prove high affinity: water displacement, charges, hydrogen bonding and conformational changes must be included. The Lennard–Jones equation explains one contribution to an energy model, not the complete free energy of a solvated complex.

11. The hydrophobic effect: why nonpolar surfaces associate in water

We have now considered forces that can be described by attractions or repulsions between particular atomic groups. The hydrophobic effect is different in an important way. It describes the tendency of nonpolar surfaces to reduce exposure to an aqueous environment. It emerges from the combined behaviour of solute and solvent rather than requiring a new special chemical bond between hydrocarbon groups. The same nonpolar molecules may be readily soluble in a nonpolar solvent. To understand why the association is particularly favoured in water, we must follow the water molecules as well as the solute.

Hydrating a nonpolar molecule

Consider transferring a small hydrocarbon molecule into water. Space must be available for it, and the surrounding water must reorganise. A small nonpolar solute may allow adjacent water molecules to preserve many water–water hydrogen bonds by changing their orientations around its surface. This rearrangement changes the statistical distribution of accessible solvent configurations. It is often taught as an ordered cage around the hydrophobe. That picture captures the possibility of reduced orientational freedom, but a real hydration shell is mobile, not a rigid clathrate crystal in ordinary liquid water. The solute also interacts attractively with surrounding molecules through dispersion. Both the cost of creating a cavity and the favourable solute–water attractions contribute to hydration free energy.

Now imagine two hydrophobic groups initially separated in water. Each presents its own solvent-accessible surface. If they approach closely, some surface becomes inaccessible to water, and water previously associated with that region can return to the bulk. For small nonpolar groups near room temperature, the gain in available solvent configurations often contributes favourably to entropy. The process may also change enthalpy through solvent contacts and direct solute packing. If the sum of these changes outweighs the loss of translational or conformational freedom of the solutes, association becomes thermodynamically favourable.

Free energy, entropy and enthalpy

At constant temperature and pressure ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. If association releases constrained water, the solvent entropy may increase, giving a negative contribution −TΔSwater. However, the associating solutes may lose freedom of movement, giving an unfavourable entropy contribution. Likewise, attractive dispersion between solutes may lower enthalpy while breaking solute–water contacts may raise it. It is the total ΔG, for the specified association under defined conditions, that predicts thermodynamic preference. Entropy is not just visible disorder; it concerns the number and probabilities of accessible microscopic configurations.

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; ΔStotal system = ΔSsolute + ΔSsolvent (conceptual decomposition).

Worked example: solvent entropy can overcome solute ordering

Assume that association at 298 K has ΔH = +5.0 kJ mol⁻¹, solvent entropy change +60 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and solute entropy change −25 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Net ΔS = +35 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.035 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Then ΔG = 5.0 − 298 × 0.035 = −5.43 kJ mol⁻¹. Association is favourable under the stated thermodynamic assumptions despite being endothermic. The favourable entropy term includes water, not just the hydrophobic groups. These are illustrative data for a defined process; the numbers are not universal parameters.

Size, temperature and heat capacity

The hydration of a small solute is not identical to the hydration of a large extended nonpolar surface. Water can reorganise around a small excluded volume without necessarily losing many hydrogen bonds, while extended interfaces can create a more pronounced loss or distortion of neighbouring water contacts. For large surfaces, the free-energy cost may scale approximately with exposed area under suitable conditions. For small solutes this simple surface-area rule need not be quantitatively valid. Curvature, shape, chemistry and temperature change the hydration response. Thus a hydrocarbon side chain is not surrounded by an identical number of rigidly ordered waters in every protein.

Hydrophobic association also does not have one universal enthalpy or entropy signature. Near room temperature, many small nonpolar hydration processes have an unfavourable entropy contribution, but that contribution changes with temperature. Hydration of nonpolar solutes often has a positive heat-capacity change, reflecting the temperature dependence of their interaction with water. Burial of nonpolar area in protein folding can contribute to a negative folding heat-capacity change. At constant pressure ΔCp = d(ΔH)/dT; if ΔCp is approximately constant over a narrow interval, ΔH(T₂) ≈ ΔH(T₁) + ΔCp(T₂ − T₁). Here heat capacity is molar and temperatures are absolute or temperature differences in kelvin. This relationship explains why the balance of enthalpy and entropy can vary with temperature instead of remaining fixed.

Some proteins can unfold at sufficiently low as well as high temperatures under appropriate solution conditions. This cold denaturation is consistent with a temperature-dependent balance of interactions and hydration, but it is not a universal consequence of putting a protein into a refrigerator. The exact transition depends on the full stability curve, composition and experimental conditions. We reserve the quantitative treatment of protein denaturation for 1.C.04. For present purposes, the key caution is that saying “the hydrophobic effect is always driven by entropy” or “it always becomes stronger when temperature rises” is too broad.

Evidence and practical interpretation

The relatively low aqueous solubility of many hydrocarbons and their preference for nonpolar phases provide experimental evidence that their exposure to water can be unfavourable. Partitioning a molecule between water and an organic phase measures its distribution under equilibrium conditions. A high preference for the organic phase supports lipophilic behaviour, although it does not directly measure the free energy of a particular protein folding event. Calorimetry can measure heat absorbed or released during transfer or association, and temperature-dependent equilibria can provide thermodynamic information. By considering both measurements, one can distinguish an entropy-favoured process from a purely enthalpic explanation.

When two oil droplets merge, the total area contacting water usually decreases. For two equal spheres of radius r, the combined volume is 2(4πr³/3). A single spherical droplet of the same volume has radius 21/3r and area 4π22/3r² instead of 8πr². The final-to-initial area ratio is 2−1/3 ≈ 0.794, a reduction of about 20.6%. At positive interfacial tension, reducing area is favourable if other contributions are held fixed. This macroscopic analogy is useful for extended interfaces, but it should not be mistaken for an exact molecular model of two individual amino acid side chains.

The distinction between hydrophobic effect and van der Waals attraction is fundamental. Two hydrocarbon groups attract through dispersion even without water. Their additional preference for association that depends on the aqueous environment is a hydrophobic effect. In a folded protein, both can cooperate: burial reduces solvent-exposed nonpolar area, and close packing makes many direct dispersion contacts. Calling all of this a “hydrophobic bond” can obscure the physical distinction and lead to wrong predictions when the solvent changes.

12. Amphipathic molecules, micelles and lipid bilayers

An amphipathic molecule has both a region that interacts favourably with water and a region that has a relatively unfavourable exposure to water. A fatty-acid salt, a detergent or a phospholipid can therefore respond to water by arranging its molecules so that polar or charged head groups contact the solvent while hydrocarbon tails are collectively shielded. The structures are not covalently joined into a single giant molecule. Their organisation emerges from molecular properties and the surrounding conditions. This is one of the clearest biological demonstrations of the hydrophobic effect.

From dispersed molecules to micelles

When a typical soluble surfactant is present at very low concentration, molecules may be mostly dispersed, with some adsorption at interfaces. As the concentration rises, aggregates become appreciably populated. The critical micelle concentration (CMC) is an operational concentration range around which micellisation becomes prominent. Above it, added surfactant tends mainly to enter aggregates and the free monomer concentration often changes relatively slowly. Micelles are dynamic; molecules can exchange between an aggregate and the solution. They do not appear as an absolute all-or-none transition at a single exact concentration in every system.

A spherical micelle contains hydrated heads on its exterior and a nonpolar interior formed by its tails. It reduces exposure of the hydrocarbon region but must also accommodate head-group crowding and tail packing. A longer tail often lowers the CMC within a comparable surfactant series because avoiding its aqueous exposure becomes more favourable. Charged heads repel one another; adding suitable electrolyte can reduce this repulsion and lower the CMC for many ionic surfactants. The outcome depends on counterion binding, temperature, molecular architecture and the kind of surfactant. Do not extrapolate the rule to all amphipathic molecules indiscriminately.

Why different amphiphiles form different aggregates

Many single-chain detergents have a head group that occupies a large area relative to the tail and can pack favourably into curved micelles. Many double-chain phospholipids have a roughly cylindrical shape that fits well into bilayers. In a bilayer two leaflets align tails inward, while heads remain hydrated on both sides. A free bilayer edge exposes some of the hydrophobic interior to water, so closing into a vesicle can remove that edge at the cost of bending the membrane. Molecular geometry, electrostatics, temperature and concentration determine which structure is preferred. There are exceptions, including nonspherical micelles and nonbilayer lipid phases. We will study lipid chemistry and membranes more completely in 1.B.02 and 2.A.02.

To see how the CMC can be inferred, imagine a plot of surface tension against logarithmic surfactant concentration. At first the surface tension falls as surfactant accumulates at the water–air interface. After micelles become appreciably populated, the free monomer activity changes more slowly and the surface tension may show a clear change in slope. Conductivity can also change slope for ionic surfactants because free ions and aggregates have different mobilities and some counterions may associate with the micelle. These methods identify an operational CMC, not a permanent new covalent species. A practice question later asks you to infer the CMC from a small set of such measurements.

Detergents can solubilise some membrane proteins by coating their exposed nonpolar regions and surrounding them with a water-compatible aggregate. This process competes with lipid–protein and lipid–lipid contacts. A strongly denaturing detergent such as SDS can also alter protein conformation; a milder detergent may preserve many native contacts. These observations connect molecular interactions directly to laboratory methods without requiring detailed purification protocols. The central lesson is that self-assembly and disassembly follow the balance of several reversible interactions in a particular solvent.

13. Cooperative stabilisation of proteins, nucleic acids and complexes

The four major stabilising mechanisms are now familiar, but an actual cell does not use them one at a time. In an aqueous globular protein, nonpolar residues often become less exposed during folding, the interior gains close dispersion contacts, polar backbone groups form hydrogen bonds and some charged groups form salt bridges. At the surface, favourable water contacts can help maintain solubility. An unfolded chain has more conformational freedom; folding typically costs some chain entropy. A stable folded state is the net result of these opposing factors, not the state that simply has the largest number of attractive atom pairs.

Membrane proteins demonstrate why the environment matters. A hydrophobic side chain on a membrane-spanning protein may face lipid tails rather than water, whereas residues lining an aqueous channel may be polar. A distribution of hydrophobic groups that would be unfavourable on the surface of a soluble protein can be appropriate in a lipid environment. Some soluble proteins also expose hydrophobic patches for ligand binding or assembly. The rule that “hydrophobic residues are always inside and hydrophilic residues are always outside” is only a useful first approximation for many soluble globular proteins, not a universal law.

Specificity, affinity and cooperativity

A protein and ligand may associate through favourable contacts in several places at once. Specificity describes preference for one partner or orientation over alternatives. Affinity describes the strength of an equilibrium association under defined conditions. Directional hydrogen bonds, matching charge distributions and precise packing can provide specificity, while hydrophobic burial and many other contacts can contribute to affinity. A molecule that binds tightly to many different partners has high affinity in those cases but limited specificity. Water-mediated contacts and changes of conformation can also make a major difference to recognition.

Cooperation in this broad structural sense means that one interaction helps make other contacts possible: burying nonpolar residues can bring particular groups into position for hydrogen bonding and packing. Breaking one contact can indirectly disrupt others. This does not mean every protein must show formal cooperative ligand binding, which has a specific thermodynamic meaning and is treated in later protein and enzyme chapters. Nor does it mean that every pair interaction is numerically additive. If two contacts share a group or change its hydration, their combined effects need not equal the sum of their isolated effects.

For P + L ⇌ PL, Kd = [P][L]/[PL]; ΔG°bind = RT ln(Kd/c°).

P is unbound protein, L is free ligand and PL is the complex. For an ideal dilute 1:1 equilibrium the dissociation constant Kd is a concentration; the standard concentration c° = 1 mol L⁻¹ makes the logarithm dimensionless. R is the molar gas constant and T is absolute temperature. The relation follows from the general thermodynamic identity ΔG° = −RT ln K for a dimensionless association equilibrium constant K = c°/Kd. We will derive and generalise equilibrium relations in 1.A.05. A smaller Kd means higher affinity, and a more negative standard binding free energy under the same standard-state convention.

Worked example: affinity change after a mutation

A wild-type protein binds at Kd = 10 nM; a mutant binds at 1 μM at 298 K. The ratio is 100. ΔΔG° = RT ln(Kd,mut/Kd,WT) = 2.478 × ln(100) ≈ +11.41 kJ mol⁻¹. The mutant is less favourable for binding by this free-energy difference. The number is not the isolated energy of a lost hydrogen bond, because mutation can also change folding, packing, desolvation or interactions with other groups.

DNA, RNA and reversible assembly

Nucleic acids illustrate another balance of molecular forces. Their phosphate backbones repel one another, but a complementary duplex gains directional base-pair hydrogen bonds, stacking interactions and favourable changes in solvation. Increasing salt often stabilises the duplex by reducing phosphate–phosphate repulsion. Heating can shift the equilibrium toward separated strands without breaking the covalent phosphodiester backbone. RNA adds additional folding possibilities, including intramolecular pairing and tertiary contacts. Mg²⁺ can help stabilise compact RNA by screening charge and sometimes by specific coordination. These two roles of Mg²⁺ are not identical: a diffuse ionic atmosphere differs from a metal ion occupying a particular structural site.

Biological complexes must be stable enough to persist when needed and sufficiently reversible to permit function. For instance, a complementary DNA duplex can be separated during replication through energy-dependent processes, while receptor–ligand complexes form and dissociate as concentrations and conditions change. Noncovalent interactions make this flexibility possible. Covalent disulfide bonds can further stabilise certain proteins, particularly in suitable oxidising environments; they are not a fifth noncovalent force and should not be confused with salt bridges or hydrogen bonds.

What changes when the environment changes?

Increasing salt may screen charge attractions or repulsions; changing solvent polarity can modify electrostatic attraction, hydration and nonpolar association; temperature changes thermal motion and the thermodynamic balance; changing pH alters protonation; and a denaturant may favour exposure of groups through interactions with the solvent. No single universal ranking of the four stabilising interactions can predict every case. If a question asks which is strongest, identify whether it concerns the energy of an isolated pair, the interaction in water, a collective interface, or the free-energy contribution to folding. A rigorous answer must specify the context.

Show answer and explanation

Correct: C. I(X) = ½(0.15+0.15) = 0.15 M. I(Y) = ½(0.05×4+0.10) = 0.15 M. C: both give λD ≈ 0.785 nm. A ignores charge squared and chloride stoichiometry. B contradicts equal I. D ignores mobile divalent ions; specific binding can differ despite this model equality.

C2. A pair of charges +1 and −1 is separated by 0.50 nm. Compare continuum medium A (εr = 80) with B (εr = 5); ignore ionic screening. Which conclusion is valid?

  1. U is −3.47 kJ mol⁻¹ in A and −55.58 in B; transfer free energy is not determined.
  2. B must favour complete burial of both ions by exactly 52.1 kJ mol⁻¹.
  3. U is positive in both media.
  4. The attractive energy in A is 16 times greater in magnitude.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: A. A follows −138.94/(εr×0.50). B improperly equates the pair-energy difference with a full desolvation/transfer free energy. C has the wrong sign for opposite charges. D reverses the magnitude ratio.

C3. In a hypothetical association, ΔH = +12 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = +50 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 300 K. Which inference follows?

  1. ΔG = +27 kJ mol⁻¹; favourable.
  2. ΔG = −3 kJ mol⁻¹; favourable under the specified conditions.
  3. ΔG = +3 kJ mol⁻¹; favourable.
  4. An endothermic process cannot be favourable.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. TΔS = 300×0.050 = 15 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔG = 12−15 = −3. B is correct. A adds rather than subtracts TΔS. C reverses the sign and criterion. D neglects the entropy contribution.

C4. An equilibrium assay at 298 K gives Kd of 20 nM for wild type and 200 nM for a mutant lacking a donor group. Both are folded. Which is best supported?

  1. The mutation changes binding free energy by about +5.7 kJ mol⁻¹.
  2. The lost isolated hydrogen bond is exactly 5.7 kJ mol⁻¹.
  3. The mutant is tenfold higher affinity.
  4. The mutation changes the standard free energy by −5.7 kJ mol⁻¹.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: A. A follows RT ln(10) ≈ +5.71 kJ mol⁻¹. B overinterprets the net binding change despite similar folding. C confuses larger Kd with stronger binding. D reverses the ratio.

C5. A DNA-binding protein gives Kd = 10 nM at 50 mM NaCl and 500 nM at 300 mM. A separate complementary DNA duplex becomes more thermally stable as salt rises. Which explanation can account for both?

  1. Salt universally destroys hydrogen bonds.
  2. Salt strengthens every opposite-charge attraction.
  3. Ionic screening reduces protein–DNA attraction and reduces backbone–backbone repulsion.
  4. The duplex must contain new covalent interstrand bonds.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: C. A cannot explain the distinct trends. B is opposite to the normal screening model. C accounts for both by considering the sign of the screened interactions. D is unnecessary; duplex stability can change without new covalent bonds. These measurements are consistent with, not exclusive proof of, screening.

C6. A protein has a buried leucine replaced either by alanine (smaller) or tryptophan (larger). Both mutants are less stable despite remaining hydrophobic at that position. What is the most defensible explanation?

  1. Hydrophobicity alone determines the native fold.
  2. Alanine may create a cavity; tryptophan may create steric strain.
  3. Both mutations necessarily remove an ionic bond.
  4. Dispersion is absent from the folded state.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. A cannot account for geometry-dependent stability. B explains lost packing or excessive overlap, though structural data would be needed to confirm these mechanisms. C is not supported by neutral side chains. D contradicts close-contact dispersion.

C7. A surfactant solution gives surface tension (mN m⁻¹) at 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 mM of 69, 54, 43, 34, 33 and 33 respectively. What is the approximate CMC from these illustrative data?

  1. 0.1 mM.
  2. 0.5 mM.
  3. Around 2 mM.
  4. Exactly 10 mM.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: C. The curve falls strongly until about 2 mM, then nearly plateaus. C identifies the approximate slope change. A and B are still in the falling region. D confuses the last data point with the onset of micellisation; the CMC is not precisely established by these sparse values.

C8. For a 12–6 potential with σ = 0.40 nm and ε = 2.0 kJ mol⁻¹, which pair of statements is correct?

  1. Minimum at 0.40 nm, U = 0.
  2. Minimum near 0.449 nm, U = −2.0 kJ mol⁻¹.
  3. Minimum at 0.80 nm, U = −2.0 kJ mol⁻¹.
  4. Force is maximally attractive at the minimum.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. B follows rmin = 2^(1/6)σ ≈ 0.449 nm and Umin = −ε. A is the zero crossing, C miscalculates the separation, D ignores that the energy gradient and hence force vanish at the minimum.

C9. A backbone amide exchanges slowly with solvent deuterium in the native protein but rapidly after unfolding. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. It proves the exact identity and energy of its hydrogen-bond acceptor.
  2. It is consistent with protection in the native structure, including hydrogen bonding or limited solvent accessibility.
  3. The amide group has become a covalent disulfide.
  4. Every amide in the native protein must exchange at the same rate.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. A exceeds what exchange data establish. B correctly recognises protection without claiming a unique cause. C confuses unrelated chemical groups. D is false because local structure and accessibility vary.

C10. For a defined association at 298 K, ΔH = −20 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔG = −8 kJ mol⁻¹. Which inference follows?

  1. Entropy is favourable by +40 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  2. ΔS ≈ −40 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; favourable enthalpy outweighs the entropy penalty.
  3. ΔS = 0 because association is favourable.
  4. The process cannot involve nonpolar surfaces.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. ΔS = (ΔH−ΔG)/T = −12/298 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ ≈ −40 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. B is correct. A reverses the sign. C ignores enthalpy–entropy compensation. D incorrectly assumes every nonpolar association must have favourable net entropy.

C11. A protein–ligand association has measured Kd values at 298 K: wild type, 10 nM; mutant A, 100 nM; mutant B, 100 nM; double mutant AB, 100 nM. All proteins are folded. If mutations acted independently on standard binding free energy, what Kd would be expected for AB, and what do the observed data suggest?

  1. 100 nM; independent effects are proved.
  2. 1000 nM; the observed effects are nonadditive.
  3. 20 nM; the effects add directly as concentrations.
  4. 1 nM; the effects improve binding.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. Each single mutation costs RT ln(10). If the two costs were independent, their sum would be RT ln(100), predicting Kd = 1000 nM. The observed 100 nM corresponds to just RT ln(10). B is correct: the free-energy effects are nonadditive, consistent with a shared or coupled interaction network; it does not prove a direct atomic contact. A ignores the second expected cost, C adds concentration rather than free energy, and D reverses the affinity change.

C12. An ideal opposite-charge pair at 1.0 nm in dilute aqueous electrolyte has the same dielectric constant in two solutions. Ionic strength increases from 0.01 M to 0.09 M. Using λD = 0.304/√I nm and the screened Coulomb model, approximately what fraction of the original attractive energy magnitude remains?

  1. One third exactly, because ionic strength triples.
  2. About 0.52.
  3. Nine times the original magnitude.
  4. The same, because permittivity is unchanged.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. The two screening lengths are 3.04 nm and 1.013 nm. The ratio at fixed distance is exp(−1/1.013 + 1/3.04) = exp(−0.658) ≈ 0.52. B is correct. A ignores the exponential and the ninefold increase in I, C reverses the trend, and D ignores ionic screening even when dielectric response is held constant.

C13. A mutant replaces a neutral polar donor in a ligand pocket with an isosteric nonpolar group. At 298 K the binding ΔH becomes 8 kJ mol⁻¹ less favourable, but −TΔS becomes 10 kJ mol⁻¹ more favourable. The native fold is unchanged within measurement resolution. Which result follows?

  1. Binding is weaker by 18 kJ mol⁻¹.
  2. Binding is stronger by 2 kJ mol⁻¹ in standard free energy; solvent release is one possible explanation.
  3. Binding is weaker by 8 kJ mol⁻¹; entropy is irrelevant.
  4. The original donor had no interaction with the ligand.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. ΔΔG = +8 − 10 = −2 kJ mol⁻¹, so B is correct. A adds magnitudes without signs, C neglects the favourable entropy term, and D makes an unjustified mechanistic inference. Removing the donor may weaken direct enthalpic contacts while changing solvent or conformational entropy.

C14. Two proteins of identical charge and similar solvent-accessible nonpolar area have different folded stabilities. Structural data show that protein X has fewer unsatisfied buried backbone carbonyl groups and fewer steric clashes than protein Y. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. Equal charge and nonpolar area require equal free energies.
  2. X can be more stable because hydrogen-bond satisfaction and packing also contribute.
  3. Y must be more stable because unsatisfied carbonyls are hydrophobic.
  4. The observations determine the precise unfolding temperature without further data.
Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. B correctly recognises that charge and buried surface do not specify all interactions or entropy changes. A assumes an incomplete model, C misclassifies polar carbonyls, and D overclaims quantitative predictive power. The structural observations suggest mechanisms, not an exact melting temperature.

15. Consolidation and the next learning step

Our journey began with the polarity of a single water molecule. Its bent shape produces a net dipole, and its hydrogen-bonding capacity produces a dynamic network. That network gives rise to distinctive thermal and interfacial properties and determines how water interacts with ions, polar groups and nonpolar surfaces. In turn, the aqueous environment modifies electrostatic energies, competes for hydrogen bonds and makes hydrophobic association possible. The same underlying chemistry operates throughout proteins, nucleic acids, membranes and molecular complexes.

When solving a problem, first identify the initial and final states and the environmental conditions. Ask which charges exist, whether they are screened, and which polar groups have available donors and acceptors. Examine whether the separation of interacting atoms is physically reasonable. Consider nonpolar area exposed to water, and include the energetic and entropic consequences of changing solvent contacts. Finally distinguish pair potential energy, enthalpy change and total free-energy change. This framework is more dependable than memorising a single list of bond strengths.

Misconceptions to eliminate before moving on

Polar bonds do not guarantee a polar molecule. Liquid water is not a static ice lattice. A hydrogen bond is not an ordinary covalent bond. Water's dielectric response is not identical to ionic screening. Zero Lennard–Jones energy is not zero force. Hydrophobic association is not a covalent bond and does not always have favourable total entropy. The number of DNA hydrogen bonds alone does not determine melting temperature. A measured mutation-induced change in affinity is not automatically the energy of one lost contact.

We have treated water as a molecular environment and learned why dissolved charges and molecular surfaces behave differently within it. In Node 1.A.03, Biological Solutions, pH and Buffer Systems, we will quantify concentrations and osmotic effects, then learn how protonation and buffers control the charge states that are so important for electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions. The full development of chemical equilibrium and Gibbs energy will follow in 1.A.05. Before proceeding, try explaining in your own words why salt can weaken a protein–DNA association and yet stabilise a DNA duplex: a correct explanation integrates the sign of electrostatic interactions, ionic screening and the other stabilising forces.
