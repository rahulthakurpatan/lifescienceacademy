1. Why water movement needs a physical explanation

An intact seedling can absorb water through its roots even though there is no central water pump, and a mature tree can transport water over distances that are enormous in comparison with the size of a single plant cell. A cut leaf loses its rigidity when it dries, yet a mildly wilted leaf may recover after the plant is watered. A seed placed in moist surroundings gains water before the embryo has developed an active root system. These observations involve different structures, but they can be understood with a common principle: the energetic state of water differs from one location to another. Water moves spontaneously when there is a suitable pathway and an energetic difference that favors movement. Understanding this principle makes it possible to predict outcomes even in a plant that has never been studied experimentally.

Water has several functions in plants that must be considered together. It is a solvent for inorganic ions and organic compounds, a reactant in photosynthetic chemistry, and a component of the hydrated environment required by proteins and membranes. It also contributes to the mechanical behavior of tissues. The pressure of water-containing cellular contents against a cell wall helps a herbaceous shoot maintain its shape. A young growing cell needs an adequate supply of water as its wall expands. In leaves, evaporation consumes energy and can reduce tissue temperature. These roles create demands on a single transport system: the root must acquire resources, the vascular system must distribute them, and leaf surfaces must exchange gases without losing water at an unsustainable rate.

To explain transport, it is necessary to distinguish three questions. First, which direction is thermodynamically favored? Second, how much water moves per unit time? Third, how does the movement change the tissue itself? The first question requires an energetic description; the second requires knowledge of permeability or hydraulic conductance; the third requires knowledge of cell volume, wall mechanics and solute behavior. A correct answer to the first question alone does not guarantee a large rate of flow. A dry soil may have a low potential and also very poor hydraulic continuity. A membrane can have a large driving force but a low permeability. A cell can take up only a small amount of water before a substantial pressure difference develops. Keeping these distinctions explicit is the foundation of plant water relations.

2. Chemical potential and the water-potential reference

Imagine two reservoirs at the same temperature containing water that is able to exchange between them. Molecules move thermally in both directions even when nothing visible changes. Net movement appears if the state of water differs sufficiently between the reservoirs. It is not meaningful to say that a particular molecule possesses a fixed urge to move toward a root; the direction of spontaneous net movement is a statistical consequence of the difference in the thermodynamic state of the two systems. The appropriate quantity for describing this difference is the chemical potential of water, written μw. It is the change in Gibbs free energy associated with adding a small amount of water while holding the relevant thermodynamic variables fixed. Its SI unit is joules per mole.

Plant physiologists commonly express this difference in pressure units by dividing by the partial molar volume of water, V̄w. Relative to a reference state, Ψw = (μw − μw,ref)/V̄w. The units are (J mol⁻¹)/(m³ mol⁻¹), which simplifies to J m⁻³. A joule is a newton metre, so J m⁻³ equals N m⁻², or a pascal. Thus water potential is an energy density and has the same dimensions as pressure. The use of megapascals is a practical choice because plant water relations commonly involve differences of a fraction of a megapascal or several megapascals. A value of −0.7 MPa is −700,000 Pa; it is not a concentration of solute or a count of water molecules.

The reference is pure water at the specified temperature, standard reference pressure and reference elevation. In everyday cell calculations, pure water at atmospheric pressure at the same height is assigned zero. A negative value signifies a lower chemical potential per unit volume than that reference, not a negative absolute amount of energy. A positive pressure can raise the water potential above the chosen reference. Two locations with negative values should be compared algebraically: −0.2 MPa is higher than −0.8 MPa. A passive pathway joining these locations can support net movement toward the latter, provided the total potentials include the relevant physical terms and the conditions allow the water to pass.

Water potential is an intensive quantity. Doubling the size of a reservoir without changing its composition, pressure, temperature or elevation does not double its water potential. Its total water content and the amount available for transfer can change greatly while the potential remains the same. Consequently a plant tissue can contain much water but have a low water potential, and two tissues with equal water potential can differ in water content. When an exam question presents percentages of water, the percentages alone are usually insufficient to establish the direction of transfer. The state and availability of the water matter.

3. Four contributions to water potential

An operational model separates the contributions of dissolved solutes, hydrostatic pressure, elevation and interactions with a solid matrix. In a convenient bookkeeping convention, Ψw = Ψs + Ψp + Ψg + Ψm. The four terms are not four different kinds of water. They account for physical influences on the same water. A plant cell in a liquid at a common height is often described by Ψw = Ψs + Ψp, because gravity differences over a cell are minute and additional matric effects are either negligible for the chosen calculation or included in its convention. Soil physicists often represent capillary and adsorptive effects with a combined matric term; these must not also be counted as separate pressure effects if that would duplicate the same physical contribution.

The osmotic or solute potential, Ψs, expresses the lowering of water activity by dissolved substances. For an ordinary aqueous solution it is zero for pure water and negative when solutes lower water activity. Adding an impermeant solute to a reservoir at constant pressure and temperature lowers its potential. This is why the vacuolar solution of a living root cell can favor uptake from dilute surroundings, but only if the total potential inside is lower. Once the cell gains water, the volume and pressure of the cell change. A calculation that treats solute potential as the only term misses this feedback.

The pressure potential, Ψp, is defined relative to the chosen pressure reference. If a rigid vessel of solution is connected to a piston and pressure is applied, the potential of the water increases. In a living plant cell, the contents press against the cell wall while the wall exerts an opposing force; the resulting positive hydrostatic pressure contributes positively to water potential. In a transpiring xylem conduit, the water may be under tension, corresponding to a negative gauge pressure. This does not imply that water molecules have acquired negative electric charge. Nor does it imply that the xylem cell has an unusually concentrated cytoplasm; mature conducting vessel elements are not living protoplasts.

Gravity contributes Ψg = ρwgz, where ρw is the density of water, g is gravitational acceleration and z is height above the reference. At 1000 kg m⁻³ and 9.81 m s⁻², the gradient is about 9810 Pa per metre or 0.00981 MPa per metre. At a height of 20 m the contribution is approximately +0.196 MPa. Water can move upward when other contributions decrease with height enough to overcome this increase. Without taking gravity into account, a tall-plant problem can produce the wrong answer even when osmotic and pressure terms have been calculated perfectly.

Matric effects arise because water interacts with surfaces and pores. Water adheres to hydrophilic soil particles and cell-wall polymers. Curved air–water interfaces in unsaturated pores create capillary effects, and strongly adsorbed water has reduced availability. As a moist soil dries, water may remain in thin films and small pores even after the larger pores empty. Such water can be held at a very low potential. Two soils with the same gravimetric water content can therefore differ substantially in the availability of water to roots. In a saline soil, dissolved salts additionally lower the osmotic contribution; treating both effects as one ideal solute concentration loses the distinction between drying and salinity.

4. Osmotic potential, activity and ideal-solution calculation

For water in a solution at the reference pressure, the chemical potential contribution due to composition is RT ln aw, where R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, and aw is water activity. Dividing by the partial molar volume gives Ψs = RT ln aw/V̄w. Pure water has activity one under the chosen reference; ln(1) is zero. When activity is less than one, its natural logarithm is negative. The logarithmic expression also reveals why a high concentration solution cannot necessarily be described accurately by a simple linear equation. Interactions between ions and other dissolved particles can change the relation between composition and water activity.

In a sufficiently dilute ideal solution, the activity of water is approximately its mole fraction. If the amount of dissolved particles is small relative to the amount of water, ln aw is approximately minus the solute-particle mole fraction. Since the volume of water is approximately the number of moles of water multiplied by its molar volume, substitution gives Ψs ≈ −RTCosm. This reasoning connects the familiar van’t Hoff equation to the energetic definition instead of presenting it as an arbitrary memorization rule. The corresponding ideal osmotic pressure Π is positive: Π ≈ RTCosm. At the same reference pressure, Ψs is approximately −Π. The sign difference is essential because the pressure that would prevent osmosis opposes the lowering of potential due to solute.

When concentration is measured in mol L⁻¹, use R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 25 °C, T is 298.15 K, not 25. For a 0.20 mol L⁻¹ ideal sucrose solution, the solute remains one dissolved molecular particle per formula unit, so Ψs ≈ −0.008314 × 298.15 × 0.20 = −0.496 MPa. For an ideal 0.10 mol L⁻¹ NaCl solution with complete dissociation, the particle concentration is 0.20 osmol L⁻¹ and the estimated value is again −0.496 MPa. A real salt solution can depart from complete ideal behavior. The calculation is useful for testing the physical principle and comparing solutions, but does not replace direct measurements in concentrated mixtures.

A 0.10 mol L⁻¹ calcium chloride solution would yield an ideal particle concentration of 0.30 osmol L⁻¹ if dissociation were complete: one calcium ion and two chloride ions per formula unit. Its estimated Ψs at 298 K would be about −0.743 MPa. Equal molar concentrations are not necessarily equal osmotic concentrations. Conversely, two solutions can have equal ideal osmotic concentrations and different physiological effects because an ion may enter a cell, alter membrane voltage, or interfere with enzyme function. In osmosis experiments it is not sufficient to calculate particle concentration; the permeability of the membrane to each solute and the time allowed for equilibration are also important.

5. Osmosis and membrane selectivity

Osmosis is the net movement of water through a selectively permeable barrier in response to a difference in its chemical potential. A selectively permeable membrane allows water to cross but may restrict one or more solutes. Consider pure water separated from a sucrose solution by a membrane impermeable to sucrose but permeable to water. With equal hydrostatic pressure and elevation, the sucrose solution has lower water potential. Water enters that compartment. As the compartment fills, the pressure may rise and the sucrose is diluted; these changes increase its total potential. Net transfer stops when the total water potentials become equal. The individual molecules continue to exchange, so equilibrium is dynamic rather than a cessation of thermal motion.

If a membrane is also permeable to the solute, the solute concentration difference may dissipate. The direction and duration of the associated volume changes depend on relative permeabilities, time and any additional solute gradients. A solution described as hyperosmotic contains more osmotically active particles per unit volume than the reference solution. A solution described as hypertonic tends to cause a sustained reduction in cell volume under the specified membrane conditions. These descriptions coincide for ideal nonpenetrating solutes but are not interchangeable for all solutes. Plant cell walls introduce an additional complication because the final volume and pressure cannot be inferred from solution osmolality alone.

Water can cross a lipid membrane directly and through aquaporins. Aquaporins facilitate water movement but do not hydrolyze ATP to transport each water molecule against its chemical potential gradient. Increasing aquaporin permeability makes the approach to equilibrium faster without reversing the thermodynamic direction of movement. Metabolism can nevertheless influence water movement indirectly by changing ion concentrations, solute accumulation or channel regulation. This is a crucial conceptual distinction between passive water movement and energy-dependent maintenance of the conditions that allow water to move.

Pressure can reverse the direction favored by a solute gradient. Suppose compartment A is pure water at gauge pressure zero and compartment B contains a solution with Ψs = −0.5 MPa. At zero applied pressure, water flows from A to B. If a piston raises Ψp in B to +0.5 MPa while composition stays constant, the potentials are equal. If the pressure is raised to +0.7 MPa, B has a total potential of +0.2 MPa and net water movement is favored from B to A. This example explains why comparing solute concentration without checking pressure can lead to an incorrect answer.

6. The plant cell as an osmotic and mechanical system

A typical mature plant cell has a large central vacuole surrounded by the tonoplast, a relatively thin cytoplasmic layer, the plasma membrane and an extracellular wall. The plasma membrane separates the cell's living contents from the wall water. The tonoplast separates the vacuolar solution from the cytosol. The wall is a hydrated polymer network and is not equivalent to the selectively permeable plasma membrane. When water enters a cell, vacuolar volume can increase and the protoplast presses outward against the wall. The wall stretches to an extent determined by its mechanical properties and pushes back. This opposition creates turgor pressure and changes the energetic condition that initially favored water entry.

Consider a cell whose initial Ψs is −0.6 MPa and Ψp is +0.3 MPa. Neglect gravity and additional matrix effects, so its Ψw is −0.3 MPa. If the external medium has Ψw = −0.2 MPa, the initial net flow is inward; if the medium has Ψw = −0.5 MPa, initial net flow is outward. Notice that both external values are negative, but the direction differs. Inward movement dilutes intracellular solutes and tends to raise pressure. Outward movement concentrates retained solutes and tends to lower pressure. A final value of turgor cannot be calculated from these initial numbers alone because the cell's solute content, volume and pressure–volume relationship are required.

A turgid cell possesses positive hydrostatic pressure and its protoplast is pressed against the wall. A flaccid cell has little or no positive turgor. The absence of substantial turgor does not necessarily mean that the protoplast is already visibly detached from the wall. As the external solution becomes sufficiently concentrated in a nonpenetrating solute, the protoplast shrinks and may withdraw from part of the wall, a process called plasmolysis. The beginning of separation is termed incipient plasmolysis. In the idealized elementary analysis it occurs when turgor has fallen to zero and the cell and surrounding medium are at osmotic equilibrium. Adhesion, wall flexibility and variation among cells can complicate this simple identification.

Plasmolysis should not be confused with the shrinkage of the entire wall or with the rupture of a membrane. A plasmolyzed cell may retain an intact plasma membrane and may recover when returned to a sufficiently dilute solution. Recovery is called deplasmolysis. Severe dehydration, solute toxicity or prolonged treatment can damage cells, and reversibility is not guaranteed. An animal cell lacks the comparable rigid wall, so responses to dilute surroundings differ: excessive water entry can lyse an animal cell, whereas a normal plant wall usually prevents unlimited expansion by developing opposing mechanical pressure.

In a growing organ the wall may yield irreversibly when pressure and wall extensibility are sufficient. Water can then enter to fill the newly available volume. A high turgor pressure does not by itself prove that growth is rapid, because a stiff nonyielding wall can resist expansion. Similarly, a tissue may maintain positive turgor while growing slowly because its biochemical regulation of wall loosening is restricted. The mechanical interpretation is important when examining experiments on cell growth, even though the detailed regulation of wall extensibility belongs to other parts of the curriculum.

7. Water potential across the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum

Water normally enters roots from the surrounding soil, travels across the root, passes into xylem, and is distributed to leaves. Some water is used in metabolism and expansion, but much of the absorbed water in a transpiring plant is eventually lost as vapor. The pathway contains both living cellular regions and conducting spaces that do not require a selectively permeable membrane at every step. Water transfer across membranes is described by osmosis when a membrane separates solutions with different potentials. Movement through xylem conduits is largely collective hydraulic flow. Both processes obey energetic principles, but they are not identical mechanisms.

As an illustrative same-height profile, soil might have Ψw = −0.10 MPa, root tissues −0.25 MPa, stem xylem −0.45 MPa and evaporating leaf tissues −0.85 MPa. This series predicts a tendency for passive water movement from soil toward leaves, provided the pathways are continuous and their resistance is finite. It does not establish an actual flux without conductance measurements. Nor does every adjacent cell have exactly these values. The values are a simplified teaching representation of a spatial gradient, not a published measurement of a particular species.

Evaporation into air with a water vapor pressure below saturation creates a strong energetic sink for water. For vapor near equilibrium with liquid water at the same temperature, Ψair ≈ (RT/V̄w) ln RH, with relative humidity RH expressed as a fraction and not a percentage. At 298 K, RT/V̄w is approximately 138 MPa using V̄w ≈ 18 × 10⁻⁶ m³ mol⁻¹. At 90% relative humidity, ln(0.90) is approximately −0.105, giving roughly −14.5 MPa. This large negative value does not mean that the liquid in a leaf must attain −14.5 MPa. The system is not at uniform equilibrium during transpiration; evaporation and diffusion occur across interfaces and resistances. The air and leaf may also have different temperatures.

When the soil dries, its matrix contribution becomes more negative and water pathways between soil particles may lose continuity. A root can then experience a lower external potential and lower soil hydraulic conductivity at the same time. Salinity can lower the external osmotic potential even when soil visibly contains water. If a plant changes intracellular compatible solute concentrations, it may help maintain a favorable gradient and positive turgor under moderate stress. These responses do not eliminate the need for water and cannot make arbitrary external conditions survivable. The molecular signaling and stress adaptations are treated in later chapters; the physical interpretation here explains why they are necessary.

Gravity must be included in comparing positions in tall plants. Suppose root xylem at the reference height has Ψs + Ψp = −0.30 MPa, and xylem 30 m above it has Ψs + Ψp = −0.80 MPa. The upper position has Ψg approximately +0.294 MPa, so its total potential is −0.506 MPa. The lower position is still higher in total potential, allowing upward flow. If the upper pressure-plus-solute value were instead −0.50 MPa, its total would be −0.206 MPa, which would not favor upward passive flow under those assumptions. A height difference therefore cannot simply be ignored in an ascent-of-sap calculation.

8. Driving force, hydraulic conductance and equilibration

For a simple pathway and modest gradients, water flow may be modeled as Q = K(ΨA − ΨB), where Q is volume transported per unit time from A toward B and K is hydraulic conductance. If Q is expressed in mL min⁻¹ and potential in MPa, K is measured in mL min⁻¹ MPa⁻¹. Hydraulic resistance is the reciprocal, R = 1/K, when the linear model is applicable. The positive sign of Q in the chosen direction means the potential at A is higher than that at B. A negative calculated Q means the actual flow is in the opposite direction. In living plants, conductance can itself vary with tissue hydration, temperature, channel regulation and structural changes, so the linear expression is an approximation rather than a universal constant.

Consider two pathways in series between soil at −0.20 MPa and a leaf at −0.80 MPa, with resistances of 2 and 4 MPa min mL⁻¹ respectively. Under steady flow, with no additional side branches, storage or local source, the same volume per minute passes each pathway. The total resistance is 6 MPa min mL⁻¹ and the flow is 0.60/6 = 0.10 mL min⁻¹. The potential loss across the first pathway is 0.20 MPa, while the loss across the second is 0.40 MPa. The intermediate location has a potential of −0.40 MPa. Increasing the resistance of one section decreases the overall flow and redistributes the potential losses.

A large difference in potential is not sufficient to establish a large flow. When a membrane has very low water permeability or a conduit becomes blocked, the gradient can persist while the flow is small. In contrast, a high-conductance connection can transport considerable water with a relatively small potential difference. This distinction also explains why measurements of tissue water potential should not be treated as direct measurements of transpiration or uptake rate. The difference in potential describes the driving force, whereas conductance and the structure of the transport network determine the rate.

At equilibrium in a closed, stable, isothermal system with a water-permeable connection and no net driving force, potentials equilibrate. A growing or transpiring plant is generally an open system. Its leaves lose water and its roots receive new water; metabolism can alter solute concentrations, and tissues can store or release water as conditions change. A steady flow can persist while potentials remain unequal. The word steady refers to a constant macroscopic rate or state over an interval, not to thermodynamic equilibrium.

9. Experimental approaches and what they establish

Classical osmosis experiments use a selectively permeable membrane separating compartments of known composition. When one side contains a retained solute, the liquid level on that side may rise. The increasing height produces hydrostatic pressure that counteracts the osmotic tendency. An appropriate control uses equal solute compositions and equal initial pressures. To interpret the observation, one must consider membrane selectivity, any leakage, evaporation and the relative size of the compartments. A rise in liquid level supports net transfer of water; it does not prove that the membrane completely excludes every type of solute.

Incipient plasmolysis can be used to estimate the osmotic potential of cells. Prepare a series of known external concentrations of a suitable nonpenetrating osmotic solute, expose comparable epidermal samples for a standardized interval, and observe the onset of protoplast separation by microscopy. A population of cells will often show a range of thresholds. An operational endpoint may be the concentration at which half of the examined cells show incipient plasmolysis. If turgor is approximately zero and equilibrium has been achieved, the cell's osmotic potential at that state is approximately equal to that of the surrounding solution. At 298 K, a threshold of 0.30 mol L⁻¹ sucrose corresponds to roughly −0.743 MPa. It does not directly measure the cell's original osmotic potential before any water was lost.

A tissue mass-change experiment uses pieces of comparable plant tissue placed in solutions of different osmotic potentials. The percentage change in mass is 100(mfinal − minitial)/minitial. With a carefully standardized procedure, a plot of mass change against external concentration can provide an estimate of the solution concentration that produces no net mass change. Suppose illustrative samples exhibit +6%, +2%, −2% and −5% mass changes in 0.10, 0.20, 0.30 and 0.40 mol L⁻¹ sucrose. Linear interpolation near zero suggests an external concentration of 0.25 mol L⁻¹, giving approximately −0.62 MPa at 298 K. This is an approximate operational estimate of tissue water potential under the experimental conditions, not a direct determination of each cell's turgor or solute concentration.

Interpretation requires control of tissue size, exposed surface area, incubation time, temperature, blotting before weighing and solution volume. Damaged cells may leak solutes, and a long incubation may permit solute uptake or metabolism. Cell walls can retain external water that contributes to measured mass without entering the protoplast. Variation in the proportions of vascular, epidermal and storage cells creates an additional reason not to equate a tissue-level result with a uniform cellular value. Replication and inspection of the response near the zero crossing are more informative than selecting a single apparent isotonic concentration without uncertainty.

The pressure chamber method, associated with Scholander and colleagues, addresses water status in excised plant organs. A leaf or shoot is placed inside a chamber while its cut end remains accessible outside. Increasing gas pressure can force sap back toward the cut surface. The balance pressure at the appearance of sap is related to the negative pressure of the xylem before excision. Under appropriate equilibration conditions, and when xylem sap osmotic effects are small or accounted for, the negative of the balance pressure approximates tissue water potential. A chamber reading of +1.2 MPa is therefore not automatically evidence of a positive leaf water potential of +1.2 MPa. It is the externally applied balancing pressure that has been measured.

The pressure chamber and the incipient plasmolysis approach answer different questions. The former concerns a hydraulic balance in an excised organ; the latter uses a cellular morphological endpoint. Psychrometric approaches infer potential from vapor equilibrium, which is sensitive to temperature control. A cell pressure probe can assess hydrostatic pressure in a suitable individual cell. Combining methods may help distinguish low turgor from a low osmotic potential. For example, if a cell has Ψw = −0.7 MPa and independently measured Ψs = −1.1 MPa under compatible conditions, then Ψp = +0.4 MPa when other terms are negligible. A single total potential measurement would not reveal this decomposition.

10. Water relations during volume change and growth

To analyze a living cell more deeply, distinguish the amount of intracellular osmotically active solute from its concentration. In a short experiment with an impermeant intracellular solute and negligible metabolism, its amount n may be approximately fixed. If the aqueous solution volume is V, the concentration is n/V. The ideal osmotic pressure therefore increases when V decreases. A cell that loses ten percent of its solution volume has a concentration factor of 1/0.90, approximately 1.11, rather than an increase of exactly ten percent. This reciprocal dependence matters in quantitative interpretations of osmotic shrinkage, because the original osmotic potential cannot simply be assumed to remain constant as water leaves.

Consider a hypothetical cell initially containing 1.0 nanolitre of solution with an ideal osmotic concentration of 0.40 osmol L⁻¹. At 298 K its solute potential is approximately −0.99 MPa. If water loss reduces the solution volume to 0.80 nanolitre without net solute exchange, the osmotic concentration becomes 0.50 osmol L⁻¹. Its new ideal solute potential is about −1.24 MPa. If positive turgor was initially +0.60 MPa, the initial total potential would have been −0.39 MPa. If the cell is now at zero turgor, its new total potential is −1.24 MPa. This example is internally consistent only if the wall and membrane permit the assumed changes; it is not intended as a measurement of a specific cell.

The pressure term can change sharply during a relatively modest volume change. A stiff wall provides large opposition to expansion. With other quantities fixed, an increase in volume of one percent can raise pressure appreciably in a stiff-walled cell, whereas a compliant wall may undergo a larger deformation for the same pressure increment. The local bulk elastic modulus, often written ε, can be expressed approximately as V(dP/dV) for a specified reversible volume change and defined tissue compartment. It has units of pressure. For small reversible changes, ΔP ≈ εΔV/V. If ε is 10 MPa, a fractional volume increase of 0.01 gives an approximate pressure increase of 0.10 MPa. Real walls have nonlinear and time-dependent behavior, so the linear estimate should not be extrapolated over large changes.

Elastic deformation and irreversible growth are not the same process. A hydrated wall may expand elastically while pressure rises and then contract as pressure falls. An actively growing cell can instead increase its relaxed wall dimensions. The wall then has a new configuration even after pressure is released. This distinction explains why supplying water to a mature, nonexpanding tissue may restore turgor without substantially increasing its permanent dimensions, while supplying water to an expanding region can support growth. Water availability is a necessary condition for such expansion, but the capacity of the wall to yield is an independent control.

A simple thought experiment separates these factors. Suppose two cells have identical solute potential, identical initial positive turgor and identical membrane water permeability. Both are moved into a bath of higher water potential. Their initial water fluxes may be similar. If one wall is stiff and the other relatively compliant, the first cell develops a larger pressure rise for a given small influx. Its potential consequently approaches that of the bath more rapidly in terms of the volume transferred. The compliant cell can undergo a larger volume change before a comparable counterpressure develops. The final states cannot be inferred by comparing initial osmotic potentials alone.

Likewise, two cells can begin at the same total water potential but have distinct turgor pressures. Cell A might have Ψs = −1.0 MPa and Ψp = +0.5 MPa. Cell B might have Ψs = −0.7 MPa and Ψp = +0.2 MPa. Their total potentials are both −0.5 MPa, and a permeable connection would not initially support net water transfer. The difference in their pressure components does not by itself establish water movement across a membrane separating solutions with different compositions. This example is valuable because it prevents the mistaken application of a bulk-pressure rule to an osmotic membrane problem.

Water storage also complicates the relation between flow and tissue potential. If water enters a cell or an organ faster than it leaves, water is accumulating. If it leaves faster than it enters, storage is being depleted. In an intact plant, a change in transpiration can initially be met partly by stored water rather than by an instantaneous equal change in root uptake. This temporary buffering is possible because tissues have hydraulic capacitance, a change in stored water associated with a change in water potential. The sign, magnitude and time course depend on the tissues and conditions. A steady-state series-resistance calculation should not be used to interpret the first seconds of a sudden disturbance without checking whether storage is significant.

11. Water availability in porous media and hydrated tissues

Soil water relations can be understood by examining the physical configuration of water around particles. In saturated soil, many pores are occupied by water. After free drainage under gravity, large pores tend to lose water first, while smaller pores and films adjacent to solid surfaces retain more water. In an unsaturated soil, the interface between air and water may be curved. Surface tension and wetting properties determine the pressure difference across that interface. In a simplified cylindrical wetting pore of radius r, the capillary pressure difference has magnitude approximately 2γ cos θ/r, where γ is surface tension and θ is the contact angle measured through the liquid. A smaller wetting pore can therefore hold water under a larger negative pressure relative to the adjacent gas phase.

For pure water with γ approximately 0.072 N m⁻¹ near room temperature and an ideal perfectly wetting pore of radius 10 micrometres, the magnitude 2γ/r is about 14,400 Pa, or 0.0144 MPa. If the pore radius decreases to 0.1 micrometre, the ideal magnitude is about 1.44 MPa. Actual soil pores are not straight uniform cylinders; interfaces change shape, surfaces have diverse wetting properties, and adsorption becomes increasingly important in thin films. These calculations demonstrate why decreasing pore dimensions can strongly increase retention, rather than providing an exact prediction of the water potential of an agricultural soil.

Water retention and water supply are distinct. A soil with fine particles may retain a high proportion of water compared with a coarse soil at a given stage of drying, but a significant fraction may be difficult for roots to acquire at very negative matric potentials. Water moves through connected pathways toward roots, and these pathways can become discontinuous as drying proceeds. The potential of water immediately adjacent to an absorbing root may then be lower than that in more distant soil. A bulk soil sample can therefore conceal local depletion. This is one reason why the transport pathway and water content measurements must be interpreted together.

Salinity changes a different contribution to water potential. If dissolved salt increases in soil solution while water content and capillary configuration are held fixed, the osmotic potential tends to become more negative. The reduction in water activity can reduce or reverse the gradient that favors root uptake. Drying can intensify salinity locally by concentrating retained solutes as water is removed. Consequently osmotic and matric limitations may occur simultaneously. The relative importance of each cannot be inferred merely from the appearance of the soil. If a question describes a visibly moist saline soil and a wilted plant, the water-potential framework supplies a physical explanation without requiring the assumption that the soil lacks water altogether.

Imbibition is another situation in which water uptake can be driven by hydration of a matrix. Dry seeds contain hydrophilic macromolecules and cellular structures that interact strongly with water. During initial hydration, water moves into such material even before a fully functional root transport system exists. Swelling can generate substantial mechanical stresses when the material is confined. This process does not require a selectively permeable membrane in the same sense as a classical osmometer, although living seed tissues also undergo membrane-dependent water and solute exchanges during germination. The distinction matters when explaining why an initially dry seed, a cell in a sucrose solution and a transpiring xylem column can all acquire water through different physical pathways.

The physical effects of matrix hydration are also relevant to cell walls. A hydrated wall can permit diffusion and bulk water movement through its porous network. The wall is outside the plasma membrane, so water entering wall spaces has not necessarily entered the living cytoplasm. When a tissue is removed from a solution and weighed, some water may remain in its wall network or on its surface. This can contribute to the observed mass change without representing an increase in the volume of the intracellular solution. The distinction between apoplastic water and symplastic water therefore has an immediate experimental consequence, before more detailed root transport pathways are introduced in the subsequent nodes.

12. Designing and interpreting measurements of water status

A good experiment begins by identifying the physical quantity that is actually measured. A balance measures mass, a microscope detects changes in shape, a pressure chamber measures applied gas pressure at an endpoint, and a psychrometric system measures a property of vapor in relation to saturation. Each observation becomes an estimate of a water-relation variable only through a model. When the assumptions of the model fail, a precisely measured observation may yield an inaccurate physiological estimate. Exam questions often present two methods that appear to give different answers; the task is to identify whether the methods actually address the same variable, state and biological compartment.

Consider an experiment comparing epidermal cells from two plants, X and Y, grown under different watering regimes. Samples are incubated in a series of sucrose solutions and assessed for incipient plasmolysis. Suppose X shows a median endpoint at 0.25 M and Y at 0.40 M, at the same temperature. Ideal calculations give osmotic potentials at the endpoint of about −0.62 and −0.99 MPa. The observation is consistent with a higher concentration of retained osmotic particles in Y at incipient separation. It does not, by itself, establish that Y has greater initial turgor, lower initial total potential, a higher hydraulic conductance, or a superior capacity to survive dehydration. To distinguish those possibilities, additional measurements of cell volume, pressure, total potential and physiological performance would be needed.

If an investigator estimates the osmotic potential at full turgor from the plasmolysis endpoint without accounting for shrinkage, the value may be too negative. The reason is that intracellular particles become concentrated as water is lost before the endpoint. If the solution volume at incipient plasmolysis is ninety percent of the solution volume in the initial state and no solute was lost, the initial concentration would be approximately ninety percent of the endpoint concentration. Under ideal conditions the initial osmotic potential would have ninety percent of the endpoint magnitude. This correction does not resolve all complications, but shows why a direct numerical identification of the two values is unjustified.

In a tissue mass-change experiment, a zero mass change can be interpreted as approximate equality of external and initial tissue water potentials only if the main mass transfer is water and other changes are small. Suppose a tissue loses intracellular solute into the medium but simultaneously absorbs water. The balance may show zero net mass change even though water entered. Likewise, surface water may mask a decrease in tissue water. An appropriate design includes consistent blotting, comparable tissue dimensions and careful control of incubation time. Measuring changes at several intervals can help reveal whether an initial osmotic response is followed by slower processes such as solute entry or metabolic adjustment.

A graph of mass change against external solute concentration is often curved over a broad range. It may nevertheless be reasonable to interpolate locally between points close to the zero crossing. If two measurements bracket zero, a first approximation is Czero = C1 + (0 − y1)(C2 − C1)/(y2 − y1), where y is the mass change. For an illustrative case, y1 = +3% at C1 = 0.20 M and y2 = −1% at C2 = 0.30 M. The estimate is 0.20 + (−3)(0.10)/(−4) = 0.275 M. At 298 K the ideal sucrose potential is about −0.681 MPa. Replicate values and uncertainty near the intercept are preferable to reporting this estimate to excessive precision.

An experimental control is not simply a sample without treatment; it is a condition that helps isolate a proposed cause. For a membrane experiment testing the effect of solute concentration on water transfer, equal solution compositions and initial pressures provide a useful negative control. A membrane with a known water-permeable, solute-restricting behavior can help establish that the assay detects osmosis. To distinguish permeability effects from osmotic effects, maintain the same initial potential difference when comparing membranes, or independently measure the potential on each side. To distinguish changes caused by evaporation from transfer through the membrane, use a closed system or appropriate evaporation controls. Without these controls, a change in liquid level cannot be uniquely attributed to osmosis.

Pressure–volume analysis provides a further conceptual link between water potential, osmotic concentration and tissue hydration. In a simplified model, a tissue with positive turgor has total potential equal to osmotic potential plus pressure. As water is removed, the osmotic contribution becomes more negative and the positive pressure contribution declines. At the turgor loss point the pressure contribution reaches approximately zero. In the region where turgor is absent, changes in the potential predominantly reflect concentration of retained solute under the stated assumptions. A plot of inverse water potential against a measure of water loss can reveal an approximately linear region after turgor loss, whereas the turgid portion departs from that relation because pressure also contributes. Estimation of osmotic potential and tissue water fractions requires careful definitions and appropriate fitting rather than visual selection of an arbitrary bend.

Relative water content, RWC, is often calculated as 100(FW − DW)/(TW − DW), where FW is fresh mass, DW is dry mass and TW is the mass after a defined full hydration procedure. For illustrative values FW = 0.72 g, TW = 0.90 g and DW = 0.18 g, RWC equals 100(0.54)/(0.72) = 75%. The ratio estimates how much water the sample contains relative to its operationally determined hydrated state. It is not water potential, and a percentage of 75 does not correspond to a universal potential or turgor. Tissues with different solute contents and elastic properties may have different potentials at the same RWC. Full hydration can also be difficult to define in damaged or osmotically adjusted tissue.

Combining independent observations helps identify a mechanism more reliably. Suppose one treatment produces lower total leaf potential while relative water content is only slightly reduced, and a second produces a much larger decline in water content at a similar potential. Differences in osmotic concentration or tissue pressure–volume relations could explain the contrasting responses. The two observations suggest hypotheses; they do not determine which explanation is correct. Direct estimates of osmotic potential or pressure would strengthen the inference. This habit of separating the observation, model, possible mechanism and further test is fundamental to CSIR-NET analytical questions.

Misconceptions and examination reasoning

  1. Water always flows toward higher solute concentration. Pressure, gravity and matrix effects can offset the solute term. Compare total water potentials before deciding direction.
  2. A negative number of greater absolute magnitude is a higher potential. −0.9 MPa is lower than −0.2 MPa. Order values on an ordinary number line.
  3. Water potential measures water content. It is an intensive thermodynamic quantity. Do not infer direction from mass or percentage water alone.
  4. Solute potential and osmotic pressure have the same sign. For dilute ideal solutions, Ψs ≈ −Π. Distinguish the reduction in potential from the opposing applied pressure.
  5. Equal molarity implies equal osmotic potential. Dissociation and nonideality alter particle concentration and activity. Check particle count, temperature and assumptions.
  6. The temperature in van’t Hoff’s equation is Celsius. Absolute temperature is required. Convert to kelvin before multiplying.
  7. Positive pressure is absent in plant cells. Turgid living cells commonly have positive gauge pressure. Distinguish living cells from transpiring xylem.
  8. Xylem tension means negatively charged water. Tension describes pressure relative to the reference. Do not confuse hydraulic and electrical variables.
  9. At equilibrium water molecules stop moving. Individual molecules still exchange in both directions. Distinguish net flux from microscopic movement.
  10. Osmosis explains all xylem movement. Bulk hydraulic flow is the main longitudinal mechanism. Identify whether a selective membrane separates the compartments.
  11. Aquaporins actively pump water. They facilitate passive movement. Changing permeability does not reverse an unchanged potential difference.
  12. Flaccidity and extensive plasmolysis are identical. Loss of turgor can precede visible separation. Distinguish the zero-pressure state from morphological detachment.
  13. A pressure chamber reading is the positive potential of the leaf. The applied pressure approximately balances negative xylem pressure under assumptions. Check the sign convention and the physical endpoint.
  14. Wet soil always supplies water readily. Salinity and matric effects can lower its potential; conductance matters. Examine both potential and pathway resistance.
  15. Gravity is negligible at every scale. It is negligible across one cell but approximately 0.01 MPa per metre. Include elevation in tall-plant comparisons.
  16. No tissue mass change proves no water exchange. Water and solute exchange can offset one another. State experimental assumptions and controls.

Original examination practice

1. Part B

Which component is usually positive in a normally turgid living cell?

  1. Osmotic potential
  2. Pressure potential
  3. Matric potential
  4. None of these
Answer and explanation

B. Turgor contributes a positive hydrostatic pressure relative to the surroundings. Solutes typically reduce water potential; matric contributions usually lower it.

2. Part B

Which is a higher water potential?

  1. −1.0 MPa
  2. −0.3 MPa
  3. −0.8 MPa
  4. All are equal
Answer and explanation

B. Water potential is ordered algebraically: −0.3 is closer to zero and higher than the other negative values.

3. Part B

What is the SI dimension of water potential?

  1. Energy per mole
  2. Mass per volume
  3. Energy per volume
  4. Force times distance
Answer and explanation

C. Dividing chemical potential by partial molar volume gives joules per cubic metre, equivalent to pascals.

4. Part B

In the dilute ideal limit, which expression estimates solute potential?

  1. RTCosm
  2. −RTCosm
  3. ρgz
  4. −ρgz
Answer and explanation

B. Dissolved solute lowers water activity, and the corresponding osmotic potential is negative. The positive expression is ideal osmotic pressure.

5. Part B

What does an aquaporin directly change?

  1. Direction of an unchanged potential gradient
  2. Water permeability
  3. The sign of gravity
  4. ATP consumption per water molecule
Answer and explanation

B. Aquaporins facilitate passive water transfer. They do not directly pump water against an unchanged driving force.

6. Part B

The main mechanism of longitudinal xylem movement is

  1. Osmosis across each vessel element
  2. Hydraulic bulk flow
  3. ATP-driven pumping of individual water molecules
  4. Active sucrose transport
Answer and explanation

B. Conducting xylem supports collective water movement through a connected hydraulic path. Osmosis is important at membranes but is not the principal longitudinal mechanism.

7. Part B

Incipient plasmolysis in the simplest cell model occurs when

  1. Turgor is approximately zero
  2. The cell wall dissolves
  3. Osmotic potential becomes positive
  4. The cell always dies
Answer and explanation

A. At incipient separation, positive turgor has been lost. The wall remains and viable cells may recover after mild treatment.

8. Part C

Cell Ψs = −0.9 MPa and Ψp = +0.4 MPa. A bath is at −0.3 MPa. Predict initial flow.

  1. Outward
  2. Inward
  3. No net flow
  4. Insufficient because all negative potentials are equal
Answer and explanation

B. Cell total is −0.5 MPa. The bath is at higher potential, −0.3 MPa, so water initially enters. Later changes in volume and turgor alter the gradient.

Two solutions at 298 K contain 0.10 M NaCl and 0.10 M sucrose. Assuming ideal dissociation and impermeant solutes, which has lower Ψs?

  1. Sucrose
  2. NaCl
  3. Equal
  4. Cannot compare even ideally
Answer and explanation

B. Ideal NaCl gives about 0.20 osmol L⁻¹ and sucrose 0.10 osmol L⁻¹. Sodium chloride has the more negative estimate, although actual behavior may be nonideal.

10. Part C

A root at height zero has total Ψ = −0.4 MPa. A stem 20 m above has pressure plus solute contribution −0.5 MPa. Neglect matric effects. Which way is favored?

  1. Upward
  2. Downward
  3. Exactly zero
  4. Gravity does not matter
Answer and explanation

B. Upper total is −0.5 + 0.196 = −0.304 MPa. This exceeds the lower potential, so downward passive flow is favored.

A membrane’s water permeability falls by half while the initial potential difference stays the same in a linear model. What changes?

  1. Initial flux halves
  2. Initial direction reverses
  3. Equilibrium potential must double
  4. Solute activity instantly becomes one
Answer and explanation

A. Flux equals conductance times the driving force. Lower conductance reduces the rate without changing the gradient sign.

  4. 0.4 M

C. The zero is halfway between the two concentrations under the linear assumption. At 298 K this corresponds to approximately −0.74 MPa.
