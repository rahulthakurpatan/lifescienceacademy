1. The problem of moving water through a plant

A bean seedling can wilt on a warm afternoon even though the soil in its pot is still moist. A slice of cucumber loses firmness after salt is sprinkled over it. A tall tree supplies leaves many metres above the ground without a mechanical pump at the base of each vessel. These observations appear unrelated until we examine the energy state of water and the paths along which it can move.

Water performs several roles simultaneously. It dissolves metabolites, participates in reactions, carries mineral nutrients and provides the mechanical support of living tissues. Yet a plant cannot simply absorb water irrespective of the surroundings. Its metabolism can maintain solute gradients and regulate membrane permeability; water responds to the physical conditions those processes create. Distinguishing direct movement from the indirect creation of a driving force is the first step toward understanding plant transport.

A common beginner's explanation is that water moves from dilute solution toward concentrated solution. This predicts many osmotic experiments, but fails if pressure differs across the membrane. Consider two solutions with the same concentration, one under extra pressure. Water can flow from the pressurized compartment to the unpressurized compartment. Conversely, a concentrated cell sap may receive no additional water if the cell wall has already generated sufficient turgor pressure. We need a single quantity that accounts for both effects.

That quantity is water potential, Ψw. It expresses the relative thermodynamic tendency of water to move. A learner who can calculate total water potential and distinguish it from osmotic potential can predict initial water movement even when pressure and concentration oppose one another. The same reasoning will later explain how soil supplies water, why xylem operates under tension, why guard-cell turgor changes and how water enters the phloem.

The chapter assumes only that cells have selectively permeable membranes and that dissolved substances affect the properties of a solution. The detailed molecular transporters, root barriers and vascular mechanisms belong to the following chapters. Here the objective is to establish their common physical language.

2. Osmotic experiments and the historical origin of the theory

Nineteenth-century osmometer experiments, notably those associated with Wilhelm Pfeffer, showed that a solution separated from pure water by a semipermeable membrane can generate substantial pressure. If the membrane allows water to pass but retains dissolved particles, water enters the solution compartment. In an apparatus with a vertical tube, this raises the liquid column. The column's weight creates a hydrostatic pressure difference opposing further entry.

Eventually the column becomes stationary. This does not mean that the membrane has closed or water molecules have stopped crossing. Water continues exchanging in both directions, but the average rates balance. If an external piston imposes enough additional pressure on the solution, the direction of net water transfer can reverse. The osmotic phenomenon is therefore a physical equilibrium involving both solution composition and pressure.

Jacobus van ’t Hoff related the osmotic pressure of dilute solutions to particle concentration and absolute temperature. The equation resembles the ideal gas law because of the dilute-solution approximation, not because solutes become gaseous. The insight allows a comparison between sucrose, salts and mixtures in quantitative terms. It also shows why the number of particles matters more than their combined mass.

Plant physiology required an extension of this framework to living cells with elastic walls, soil pores that retain water and conduits containing water under tension. The concept of water potential makes these phenomena comparable because chemical potential differences are expressed in the same units as hydrostatic pressure. Experiments can then test predictions using changes in tissue mass, microscopy, pressure chambers or vapor equilibration.

Think of the osmometer as a controlled model of the living cell, not an exact anatomical copy. The tube provides a mechanical opposition to water entry; in a cell the wall provides the opposition. The membrane is responsible for selective permeability, whereas the wall is responsible for much of the mechanical resistance to swelling. These roles must not be confused when interpreting plasmolysis.

3. Chemical potential, water potential and reference units

Chemical potential μw is the partial molar Gibbs free energy of water: the change in Gibbs energy associated with adding an infinitesimal amount of water under specified conditions. It is measured in joules per mole. A difference in chemical potential is the thermodynamic driving force for transfer when the relevant pathway is available.

For a fixed temperature and reference height, water potential is defined relative to pure reference water: Ψw = (μw − μw*)/V̄w, where μw* is the molar chemical potential of reference pure water and V̄w is the partial molar volume of water. Its approximate value near room temperature is 18 × 10−6 m³ mol−1. Dividing joules per mole by cubic metres per mole gives joules per cubic metre, equivalent to pascal. The expression uses an approximately constant partial molar volume, adequate for ordinary plant-physiology calculations.

Pure free water at the reference pressure and height is assigned Ψw = 0. This is a chosen zero, not the absence of molecular energy. Dissolving solute or binding water to a matrix typically reduces the value; increasing hydrostatic pressure or raising the position relative to the reference adds positive contributions.

Plant physiology commonly uses megapascals: 1 MPa = 106 Pa = 1000 kPa = 10 bar. A value of −500 kPa equals −0.5 MPa. A negative potential describes a lower energy state than the reference; it does not describe negative mass, negative water concentration or electrical charge.

Under isothermal conditions, water tends to move from higher total potential toward lower total potential if a pathway permits it. In the signed sequence 0, −0.2, −0.5, −1.0 MPa, the most negative value is the lowest. Water will initially move from −0.2 toward −0.5 MPa rather than the reverse. If the potential difference is zero, there is no net flux driven by that difference. If a barrier prevents passage, the gradient can persist without actual movement.

The direction criterion does not specify flow rate. A steep gradient across an almost impermeable membrane may cause less flow than a modest gradient across a highly permeable membrane. This separation of thermodynamic driving force from conductance becomes important when comparing drought-stressed and hydrated roots.

4. Four components and their physical meaning

The convenient accounting equation is Ψw ≈ Ψs + Ψp + Ψg + Ψm. The four terms are solute, pressure, gravitational and matric potential. The decomposition is approximate and convention-dependent. In soil science a capillary pressure may be included in a pressure term or described separately as matric potential. The correct procedure is to use the definitions supplied and avoid double counting.

Solute potential Ψs, also denoted Ψπ, represents the decrease in water chemical potential due to dissolved particles. For pure water Ψs = 0. Adding ordinary dissolved solutes makes it negative. Osmotic pressure π is the positive external pressure required to prevent net osmosis under the defined conditions. In an idealized solution, Ψs ≈ −π. The positive pressure and negative potential refer to different quantities.

Pressure potential Ψp is the hydrostatic pressure relative to the reference, usually atmospheric pressure. A living turgid cell is pressurized as the cell wall restrains expansion. A transpiring xylem vessel can contain continuous water under negative gauge pressure, or tension. Both states can be expressed in MPa. Positive turgor raises Ψw; negative xylem pressure lowers it.

Gravitational potential Ψg changes with vertical position. If the root base is the reference, a leaf above the root has a positive contribution. Water must overcome this height-related energy increase when it rises. Across a single cell, the term is generally much smaller than osmotic and pressure effects; it matters considerably for tall plants.

Matric potential Ψm reflects water interactions with surfaces and porous structures. Adsorption to particles and curvature of liquid interfaces reduce the energy state of water relative to free water. The term is especially useful in unsaturated soils and porous tissues. It is not the same as solute potential because the dominant interaction is with the matrix rather than dissolved particles.

For an isolated small plant cell in a simple external solution, Ψg and a separately assigned Ψm are often neglected. Then Ψw = Ψs + Ψp. The familiar two-term equation is therefore a conditional simplification, not a universal claim that gravitational and matric effects do not exist.

5. Derivation and application of the van ’t Hoff relation

At fixed temperature and pressure, the chemical potential of water in a solution relative to pure water is RT ln aw, where aw is water activity, R the gas constant and T absolute temperature. Activity is dimensionless. It expresses the effective thermodynamic availability of water. In an ideal solution it approximates the mole fraction of water.

Let ns represent the number of moles of independently dissolved particles and nw the moles of water. The ideal water mole fraction is nw/(nw + ns). When ns is small relative to nw, ln aw ≈ −ns/nw using the dilute-limit logarithmic approximation. Dividing RT ln aw by V̄w gives Ψs ≈ −RT ns/(nw V̄w). The product nw V̄w approximates the volume of solvent, which in a dilute solution is close to solution volume. Thus Ψs ≈ −RT Cosm, where Cosm is osmotic particle concentration.

For a single solute, write Ψs ≈ −iCRT, with C the molarity of formula units and i the effective van ’t Hoff factor. Sucrose and glucose are non-electrolytes, so i is approximately one. Ideal fully dissociated NaCl produces two particles per formula unit and CaCl₂ produces three. Real ionic solutions deviate from these ideal factors because ions interact; in concentrated solutions measured activities or osmotic coefficients give better estimates.

With C in mol L−1, use R = 0.008314 MPa L mol−1 K−1. If R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1, concentration must be in mol m−3 and the resulting pressure converted from Pa. At 25°C (298 K), RT ≈ 2.48 MPa L mol−1. A 0.10 M sucrose solution has Ψs ≈ −0.248 MPa; a 0.10 M ideally dissociated NaCl solution gives about −0.496 MPa. The latter is an approximation, not an experimental guarantee of precisely twice the osmotic effect.

For a dilute mixture, sum effective particle concentrations. A solution of 0.10 M sucrose plus 0.05 M ideal NaCl has Cosm ≈ 0.10 + 2(0.05) = 0.20 mol particles L−1, so Ψs ≈ −0.496 MPa at 298 K. In highly concentrated solutions, independent addition can become inaccurate.

The use of absolute temperature is essential. Substituting 25 instead of 298 changes the calculated result by nearly a factor of twelve. At fixed ideal particle concentration the magnitude of Ψs increases with absolute temperature; however, a real leaf may also change water content and solute concentration as temperature rises. A single-variable equation does not describe all these simultaneous physiological processes.

If solute is supplied by mass, first divide by molar mass. A litre containing 34.2 g sucrose (approximately 342 g mol−1) has 0.10 M sucrose. A litre containing 5.84 g NaCl (58.4 g mol−1) also has 0.10 M formula units but an ideal particle concentration of 0.20 M. Equal mass is neither equal molarity nor necessarily equal osmotic potential.

At equilibrium in an osmometer with pure water on the reference side, the balancing pressure equals the magnitude of the solute potential, so π ≈ iCRT. This is why an ideal solution with Ψs = −0.50 MPa can be balanced by +0.50 MPa of applied pressure under the stated conditions.

Worked example

0.15 M CaCl₂ at 298 K, assuming complete ideal dissociation: Cosm = 3 × 0.15 = 0.45 mol L⁻¹. Ψs = −0.008314 × 298 × 0.45 ≈ −1.115 MPa. Real solutions can deviate from this ideal estimate.

6. Selectively permeable membranes and water flux

Osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable barrier, driven by a difference in water chemical potential. A membrane may conduct water through its lipid bilayer and through aquaporins. Restriction of relevant solutes allows an osmotic gradient to persist. If the membrane were equally permeable to the relevant solute, the concentration difference would tend to dissipate and could not produce the same sustained osmotic effect.

The molecular picture includes water moving in both directions at all times. Net osmosis arises because the statistical balance of those transfers changes with the chemical potential difference. Equal total water potential is therefore a dynamic equilibrium, not a stationary arrangement of molecules. The equilibrium criterion is independent of how quickly it is reached.

For a simplified membrane retaining the solutes, define inward ΔΨ = Ψoutside − Ψinside. A useful linear expression is Jv = LpΔΨ, where Jv is volume flux per unit area per unit time and Lp is hydraulic permeability. A positive value denotes inward flow under this convention. Doubling Lp at fixed initial ΔΨ doubles initial flux; closing aquaporins can decrease flux without reversing direction. Over time, water redistribution may change the gradient itself.

For a partly permeable solute, a reflection coefficient σ in the range from zero to one can describe its effectiveness in driving osmotic flow across a particular membrane in simple transport models. A value near one means that solute is effectively retained; a value near zero means the solute produces little sustained osmotic driving effect. This is one reason osmolarity and tonicity are not identical. The latter depends on the solutes' effectiveness across the relevant membrane and the observation time.

Active ion transport can establish solute gradients using metabolic energy. Water then responds to the changed Ψw. Aquaporins themselves are passive water pathways, not ATP-hydrolyzing water pumps. The plant-specific regulation of these proteins and of proton-coupled solute transport is the subject of 6.F.02.

Suppose a cell has Ψs = −0.60 MPa and Ψp = +0.30 MPa. Its total potential is −0.30 MPa. In a bath at −0.20 MPa water enters initially, because −0.20 is higher. In a bath at −0.50 MPa water leaves initially. In a bath at −0.30 MPa there is no initial net flux, even if the concentration in the bath differs from that in the cell. The pressure and solute contributions can balance.

7. Turgor, flaccidity, plasmolysis and deplasmolysis

A plant cell contains a selectively permeable plasma membrane and usually a large central vacuole bounded by the tonoplast. The wall is an extracellular load-bearing structure. It is relatively permeable to water and many small solutes. The membrane maintains solute distributions, while the wall resists excessive expansion. Together they allow a cell to sustain positive internal hydrostatic pressure.

When a flaccid cell is placed in a bath of higher Ψw, water enters, cell volume increases and the protoplast presses against the wall. As the wall stretches, its elastic resistance produces increasing positive turgor. Simultaneously, water entry dilutes the sap, tending to make Ψs less negative. Both changes raise internal Ψw, reducing the driving difference. Eventually internal and external water potentials may match.

A cell equilibrated with pure water has Ψw = 0 under the ordinary small-cell assumptions. If its final Ψs is −0.70 MPa, its Ψp must be +0.70 MPa. In a bath of −0.20 MPa, a final cell Ψs of −0.70 MPa would instead imply +0.50 MPa of turgor. Notice that the calculation requires the final osmotic potential. If the stated concentration is initial, cell volume changes can alter it before equilibrium.

Turgor supports the stiffness of herbaceous tissues. It also supplies a mechanical force for cell enlargement, but wall yielding is necessary for irreversible growth. A mature cell with a rigid wall can retain substantial positive turgor without appreciable growth. A developing cell may expand if the wall is loosened and water is available to maintain pressure. This distinction later connects water relations with auxin-dependent wall expansion.

When the cell loses water, positive turgor declines. At approximately zero turgor a cell is flaccid, and tissues may droop. The protoplast may still touch the wall. Further water loss in a sufficiently hypertonic medium causes the protoplast to contract and pull away from parts of the wall; this is plasmolysis. The wall generally retains its overall shape, whereas protoplast volume decreases. Therefore flaccidity and plasmolysis are not synonyms.

If membrane integrity remains adequate, transfer of a plasmolyzed cell to a higher-potential medium can cause water reentry and reexpansion, termed deplasmolysis. This demonstrates that early plasmolysis may be reversible, not that every osmotically stressed cell survives. Prolonged or extreme exposure can cause irreversible damage and ion toxicity, especially in saline treatments.

Hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic describe the effects of effective nonpenetrating solutes on cell volume relative to a specified cell. For a flaccid cell and retained solute, a hypotonic bath favors swelling and a hypertonic bath favors shrinkage. For a turgid plant cell, even a bath of equal osmolarity can receive water because the positive internal pressure raises Ψw. Thus in quantitative plant-cell problems, compare totals rather than relying solely on these labels.

8. Incipient plasmolysis as an experimental tool

At incipient plasmolysis, the protoplast just begins to separate from the wall in the idealized classical model. Turgor is approximately zero, so Ψw inside ≈ Ψs inside for a small cell. If the bath is at atmospheric pressure and the system has equilibrated, Ψs bath ≈ Ψs cell at that particular state. This provides an approximate method to estimate sap osmotic potential without directly extracting sap.

To perform a conceptual experiment, place comparable onion epidermal pieces in a series of sucrose concentrations, for instance 0.10–0.50 M. Standardize temperature, treatment duration, tissue source and microscopic scoring criteria. Determine the proportion of visibly plasmolyzed cells at each concentration. An increasing proportion indicates increasing osmotic water loss under those treatment conditions.

If half the observed cells show plasmolysis around 0.30 M sucrose at 298 K, the estimated median threshold is Ψs ≈ −0.008314 × 298 × 0.30 = −0.743 MPa. This estimate describes the median cell threshold at the treated state. It does not establish that all cells initially had that sap potential. Cells differ in solute content, wall–membrane attachment and volume, and some may have concentrated their sap through water loss before reaching zero turgor.

Repeat with a dilute solution after exposure to assess reversibility. If many cells regain their protoplast–wall contact, the observation supports the interpretation of reversible osmotic deformation. If cells remain contracted, possible explanations include insufficient equilibration time, injury or altered membrane permeability. The observation alone does not establish which mechanism caused non-recovery.

Compare an onion epidermis assay with a potato mass-change experiment. Incipient plasmolysis estimates sap osmotic potential at zero turgor. The zero-mass-change method estimates tissue water potential under the experimental conditions; tissues contain multiple cell types, walls and extracellular water. These measurements are related, but not interchangeable. A useful CSIR-NET analytical question may ask why the two methods give different results even without experimental error.

9. Gravitational potential and upward transport

Gravitational potential can be derived by expressing potential energy per unit volume. For mass m at height h, gravitational energy is mgh; dividing by volume gives (m/V)gh = ρwgh. Hence Ψg = ρwgh relative to the chosen reference. With water density approximately 1000 kg m−3 and g = 9.81 m s−2, every metre adds about 9810 Pa, or 0.00981 MPa. A 30 m rise adds about 0.294 MPa.

Across a single 100 μm cell, the gravitational difference is about 0.98 Pa, negligible compared with typical plant-cell potentials measured in tenths of a megapascal. For tall trees the same physical term is large. This is why it is appropriate to neglect gravity in one experiment but not in another. An equation is not wrong merely because it has fewer terms; the scale of the problem determines which simplifications are justified.

In a static continuous column of water with no net flow, pressure decreases with height so that Ψp + Ψg remains constant if other terms are uniform. At 10 m above the base, the required pressure difference is about −0.098 MPa. To drive upward flow, an additional total potential difference is needed to overcome hydraulic resistance. Balancing gravity alone gives equilibrium rather than upward flux.

Consider xylem at the base with Ψp = −0.10 MPa and at a leaf 20 m above it with Ψp = −0.50 MPa. Assume similar solute potentials and ignore a separate matric term. With the base as reference, the base total pressure-plus-gravity contribution is −0.10 MPa. At the leaf it is −0.50 + 0.196 = −0.304 MPa. The upward driving difference is +0.204 MPa, not +0.40 MPa. Ignoring gravity overestimates the gradient.

Changing the chosen reference height shifts both absolute Ψg values by the same constant. It does not change their difference. When a problem supplies total water potentials at two heights, do not add a gravity contribution again unless the quantities were explicitly defined as excluding it. The same warning applies to matrix effects in soil.

10. Matric potential, soil texture and salinity

Water in soil is held in pores and contacts mineral and organic surfaces. Adhesion to these surfaces and curvature of air–water interfaces can lower its chemical potential. As drying proceeds, larger pores usually drain before smaller pores. Water remains as films and in small pores, but is held at increasingly negative potentials. The presence of water alone is therefore not proof that it is readily available to a plant.

In an ideal cylindrical wetting capillary, the magnitude of capillary suction is approximated by 2γ cosθ/r, where γ is surface tension, θ the contact angle and r the radius. A smaller pore can sustain a larger suction magnitude. Real soils contain irregular interconnected pores and varying surface chemistry; the equation describes the trend, not a universal soil measurement formula.

Water content measures the quantity of water in soil, whereas water potential measures its energy state. Two soils with equal water content can have different potentials because their texture and pore geometry differ. A soil water retention curve relates the two for a particular soil; drying and wetting may follow different curves. This distinction also helps explain why visually moist soil does not guarantee effective root water supply.

Salinity lowers the solute contribution in soil water. Consider a root at −0.40 MPa. A well-watered nonsaline soil at −0.15 MPa favors inward movement, whereas a wet saline soil at −0.70 MPa may initially draw water from the root if the pathway permits. Salinity can produce physiological water stress without the soil appearing dry. Later stress-physiology chapters examine the additional problem of ion toxicity and cellular acclimation.

In some conventions capillary suction is included as negative pressure; in others it is separately classified as matric potential. If soil total potential has already been given, no additional subtraction should be made. Also, drying tends to break connected water pathways, increasing resistance. Water uptake can fall even when a substantial gradient is still present.

For example soil A has Ψm = −0.45 and Ψs = −0.05 MPa; soil B has Ψm = −0.05 and Ψs = −0.45 MPa. Ignoring other terms, each totals −0.50 MPa. Both have the same initial thermodynamic gradient relative to a root with identical Ψw, yet their hydraulic conductivities, ion concentrations and biological effects may differ. The equality of a driving force does not imply equality of an entire physiological situation.

11. The soil–plant–atmosphere continuum

Water usually moves from soil toward root surfaces, through root tissues and into the xylem. It is carried along the xylem toward leaves, reaches evaporating surfaces within leaves and finally enters the atmosphere. This overall route is called the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum. It is not one uninterrupted pipe with a single mode of transport: water crosses living membranes, travels as bulk liquid through conduits and diffuses as vapor.

A hypothetical daytime profile is soil Ψw = −0.10 MPa, root tissue = −0.30 MPa, lower xylem = −0.45 MPa and leaf = −0.90 MPa. If these values represent comparable total potentials, each successive compartment is lower, favoring movement toward the leaf. A local reversal can occur when a soil dries, when tissues release stored water or when transpiration changes rapidly. The general principle is local higher-to-lower transfer, not a claim that water must always move upward.

At constant temperature, the water potential of air in vapor equilibrium can be approximated by Ψair = (RT/V̄w) ln RH, with relative humidity RH expressed as a fraction. The formula follows from the chemical-potential dependence on vapor activity. At 298 K, RT/V̄w is approximately 138 MPa. For RH = 0.5, ln(0.5) ≈ −0.693, so Ψair ≈ −95.6 MPa. This expresses the energy state of vapor relative to saturated air at the same temperature; it is not a liquid pressure inside xylem.

The large leaf–air difference does not yield unlimited flow because stomatal aperture, boundary-layer diffusion, hydraulic conductance and water supply regulate the flux. The liquid pressure gradient drives bulk water flow within xylem. Osmosis is particularly relevant where selective living membranes intervene. Vapor transport occurs by diffusion and atmospheric transport. The water-potential framework unifies these processes without claiming they use identical mechanisms.

In a simple steady-state hydraulic model, Q = K(Ψupstream − Ψdownstream), where Q is volume flow rate and K is hydraulic conductance in compatible units. In a series pathway the largest resistance contributes the largest potential drop for a common steady Q. Real plants are not always at steady state: water-storing tissues may release stored water when evaporation exceeds current root uptake. This explains why a potometer's uptake measurement is not necessarily equal to instantaneous leaf transpiration.

Suppose a pathway has K = 4 arbitrary flow units per MPa and ΔΨ = 0.2 MPa. Q is 0.8 units. After drying, the driving difference increases to 0.4 MPa, but conductance falls to 1 unit per MPa. Q becomes 0.4 units. Even with a larger gradient, flow is lower. In subsequent lessons this reasoning will be applied to root barriers, xylem cavitation and stomatal regulation.

12. Experimental measurement and interpretation

The tissue mass-change method uses pieces of comparable tissue incubated in solutions of known osmotic potential. Record their initial mass, expose them for a standardized time, gently blot surface liquid using the same procedure and record final mass. Percentage mass change = 100 × (final − initial)/initial. Plot percentage change against external concentration. A near-zero mass change estimates the tissue's water potential under the experimental conditions, assuming exchange is predominantly water and the system has sufficiently equilibrated.

Consider hypothetical potato data: 0.10 M sucrose causes +8% mass change, 0.20 M +3%, 0.30 M −2%, and 0.40 M −7%. The zero crossing lies between 0.20 and 0.30 M. Assuming local linearity, the required fraction is 3/(3+2) = 0.60. Thus the estimated concentration is 0.20 + 0.60 × 0.10 = 0.26 M. At 298 K, Ψw ≈ −0.008314 × 298 × 0.26 = −0.644 MPa. This is a tissue estimate, not a direct measurement of the pressure of an individual cell.

Replicate each concentration, standardize tissue geometry, use comparable pieces and temperature, and examine whether the apparent equilibrium is stable over time. Poor blotting may leave external solution on tissue surfaces. Penetrating solutes, tissue damage or changes in metabolism may alter the response. A zero crossing does not by itself prove that every cell in the tissue has identical Ψw.

A pressure chamber estimates xylem or equilibrated tissue water status. An excised shoot or leaf is placed inside a chamber with its cut stem or petiole projecting outside. Gas pressure is increased until sap returns to the cut surface. Under appropriate equilibration and negligible xylem sap osmotic contribution, the negative of this balancing pressure approximates the relevant water potential. A reading of +0.80 MPa balancing pressure therefore corresponds approximately to Ψw = −0.80 MPa under these conditions. It is not +0.80 MPa of mesophyll-cell turgor.

Psychrometers or dew-point instruments estimate water potential using vapor equilibration. A sample with lower Ψw equilibrates with lower vapor activity at a common temperature. Because the saturation vapor pressure depends strongly on temperature, small gradients between the sample and sensor can bias results. Thermal equilibrium and instrument calibration matter. Pressure chambers and psychrometers measure water status via different physical principles; their estimates may disagree when equilibration or compartment assumptions fail.

A cell pressure probe can estimate hydrostatic pressure of individual cells through a fine capillary. Osmometry can estimate the solute potential of an extracted solution using freezing-point or vapor-pressure depression. Combining Ψw and Ψs can estimate Ψp by subtraction when the measurements represent the same compartment and other contributions are negligible. Mixing measurements from different compartments creates an invalid subtraction.

Relative water content (RWC) is a separate hydration index: RWC = 100 × (fresh mass − dry mass)/(turgid mass − dry mass). Two tissues can have the same RWC but different Ψw because their solute content and wall mechanics differ. For example, two genotypes might show similar leaf water content while one maintains more negative sap osmotic potential and higher turgor. The interpretation requires measurements, not an automatic assumption that equal RWC implies equal physiological water status.

13. Worked numerical and conceptual examples

Two cells with opposing osmotic and pressure effects. A has Ψs = −0.9 and Ψp = +0.5 MPa. B has Ψs = −0.6 and Ψp = +0.1 MPa. A totals −0.4, B totals −0.5. Water initially moves A to B despite A having a more negative osmotic potential. The individual components will change as volume and pressure adjust.

Equilibrium turgor. A cell is equilibrated in a bath of −0.35 MPa. Its final Ψs = −0.80 MPa. Since total water potential equals the bath, Ψp = −0.35 − (−0.80) = +0.45 MPa. If only the initial concentration had been supplied, volume information would be needed to calculate the final concentration and pressure.

Equal osmolarity, unequal total potential. A turgid cell with Ψs = −0.70 and Ψp = +0.40 MPa is immersed in a bath with Ψs = −0.70 MPa and zero pressure. The cell total is −0.30 MPa. Water initially leaves for the lower-potential bath. Matching osmolarities is not the same as matching total potential.

Flux and permeability. A membrane has Lp = 2 × 10−7 m s−1 MPa−1. The outside is −0.2 and inside −0.6 MPa. Jv = 2 × 10−7 × 0.4 = 8 × 10−8 m s−1 inward. Halving Lp at unchanged initial potentials halves flux to 4 × 10−8 m s−1, without reversing direction.

Soil salinity and root uptake. Soil Ψs = −0.25, Ψm = −0.30; root Ψs = −0.90, Ψp = +0.20 MPa. Soil total −0.55 exceeds root −0.70, favoring uptake. If soil Ψs falls to −0.50, soil total becomes −0.80 and the initial gradient reverses. This is an initial thermodynamic prediction, not a guarantee of eventual cellular damage.

Concentration changes during swelling. A cell initially contains 0.40 M ideal osmotic particles at 298 K. It increases its osmotic volume by 25% without solute exchange. The final concentration is 0.40/1.25 = 0.32 M and Ψs ≈ −0.793 MPa. If the final bath potential at equilibrium is −0.20 MPa, Ψp ≈ +0.593 MPa. Using the initial concentration would overestimate the required turgor. Real cells may require correction for osmotically inactive volume.

Gravitational correction. Root and leaf xylem pressures are −0.10 and −0.30 MPa, and the leaf is 30 m above the root. Neglect other differences. The leaf pressure-plus-gravity contribution is −0.30 + 0.294 = −0.006 MPa. This is higher than the root potential, so the stated gradient does not drive upward flow. Negative pressure at the leaf does not automatically overcome height.

Temperature and ionic dissociation. A 0.20 M ideal NaCl solution at 308 K has Ψs ≈ −2 × 0.20 × 0.008314 × 308 = −1.024 MPa. At 298 K the corresponding estimate is −0.991 MPa. The slight temperature effect is separate from the twofold ideal particle effect. Neither calculation accounts for real electrolyte nonideality.

Estimating relative water content. Fresh mass is 8 g, turgid mass 10 g and dry mass 2 g. RWC = 100 × (8−2)/(10−2) = 75%. It is not 80%, because dry solids must be excluded. Without further measurements this value does not specify the tissue water potential.

Combining methods. A properly equilibrated tissue has measured Ψw = −0.65 MPa and representative sap Ψs = −1.05 MPa. Assuming negligible height and separately counted matric terms, Ψp = +0.40 MPa. If the sap was extracted from a different organ, the subtraction would not be defensible. A numerical answer is only as valid as its compartment assumptions.

Problem-solving workflow

Identify the compartments and reference; convert units; calculate all relevant signed contributions; compare total potentials; determine whether a path exists; distinguish initial flux from eventual equilibrium.

14. Original Part B, Part C and assertion–reason practice

Original exam-oriented questions, not claimed as previous-year questions. Attempt each before opening the explanation.

1. Part B — A cell has Ψs −0.7 and Ψp +0.4 MPa. Which bath drives initial water entry?

  1. −0.8
  2. −0.4
  3. −0.2
  4. −0.6
Answer and explanation

C. Cell total is −0.3. Only −0.2 is higher; the other baths favor outward movement.

2. Part B — Which term denotes the positive pressure needed to oppose osmosis?

  1. Osmotic pressure
  2. Solute potential
  3. Matric potential
  4. Gravitational potential
Answer and explanation

A. π is positive by convention; Ψs is approximately −π. The other terms describe different contributions.

3. Part B — Turgor in a living plant cell arises primarily from:

  1. Negative ion charge
  2. Active water pumping
  3. Identical internal and external solute concentration
  4. Wall restraint on expansion
Answer and explanation

D. Wall restraint generates positive hydrostatic pressure. Ion charge and direct pumping are not the mechanism; equal concentration is unnecessary.

4. Part B — At equal total water potential:

  1. All water molecules stop
  2. No net water flow is driven by the difference
  3. Solute concentrations must match
  4. Pressure must be identical
Answer and explanation

B. Dynamic molecular exchange continues; pressure and solute terms can balance.

5. Part B — Plasmolysis is:

  1. Protoplast separation from portions of wall after water loss
  2. Wall dissolution
  3. Cell burst in dilute medium
  4. Complete loss of molecular water movement
Answer and explanation

A. Water loss shrinks the protoplast. The wall remains largely intact and molecular motion continues.

6. Part B — Closing aquaporins at unchanged initial ΔΨ mainly:

  1. Reverses flow
  2. Makes Ψs positive
  3. Decreases hydraulic permeability
  4. Pumps water uphill
Answer and explanation

C. Aquaporins are passive pathways; reducing permeability reduces flux magnitude, not necessarily direction.

7. Part B — The negative matric contribution in soil results mainly from:

  1. Photosynthesis
  2. Adsorption and capillary retention
  3. Positive turgor
  4. Ion charge alone
Answer and explanation

B. Surface interactions and curved interfaces lower the energy state of water.

8. Part B — 1 MPa is equivalent to:

  1. 100 Pa
  2. 10 kPa
  3. 1000 bar
  4. 1000 kPa
Answer and explanation

D. 1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa = 1000 kPa = 10 bar.

9. Part B — Negative xylem gauge pressure signifies:

  1. Negative water mass
  2. Only negative ions are present
  3. An empty conduit
  4. Water under mechanical tension
Answer and explanation

D. Negative pressure is mechanical tension relative to reference, not charge or an absence of liquid.

10. Part B — Ideal 0.1 M glucose plus 0.1 M sucrose gives particle concentration of:

  1. 0.05
  2. 0.2
  3. 0.1
  4. 0.4
Answer and explanation

B. Both are non-electrolytes; their particle concentrations add directly.

11. Part B — Pure free water at reference pressure and height has Ψw:

  1. 0 by convention
  2. Always −1 MPa
  3. Equal to gravitational acceleration
  4. Undefined
Answer and explanation

A. The zero is a defined reference, not absence of energy.

12. Part B — Which is not necessarily equal between two soils with the same Ψw?

  1. Total potential
  2. Initial driving difference against an identical root
  3. Hydraulic conductivity
  4. Potential value in MPa
Answer and explanation

C. Conductance depends on structure and connectivity; the stated potential and initial difference can be equal.

13. Part C — Ideal 0.20 M NaCl at 298 K has approximate Ψs:

  1. −0.25 MPa
  2. −0.99 MPa
  3. −0.50 MPa
  4. +0.99 MPa
Answer and explanation

B. iC = 0.40; −RTiC ≈ −0.992. Other answers neglect dissociation, use wrong concentration or sign.

14. Part C — A cell in equilibrium at −0.4 MPa has final Ψs −1.1 MPa. Ψp equals:

  1. −0.7
  2. +1.5
  3. −1.5
  4. +0.7
Answer and explanation

D. −0.4 − (−1.1) = +0.7 MPa. Subtract signed quantities.

15. Part C — The gravitational contribution at 40 m is about:

  1. 0.004 MPa
  2. 0.04 MPa
  3. 0.39 MPa
  4. 3.9 MPa
Answer and explanation

C. ρgh = 392400 Pa = 0.3924 MPa. Other values misconvert units.

16. Part C — A leaf 30 m above a root has Ψp −0.3; root Ψp −0.1 MPa. Other terms equal. Leaf total pressure plus gravity is:

  1. −0.006
  2. −0.6
  3. −0.3
  4. +0.3
Answer and explanation

A. −0.3 + 0.294 ≈ −0.006 MPa. This does not favor upward flow from the root.

17. Part C — Tissue gains 4% at 0.20 M and loses 6% at 0.30 M. Linear zero-crossing estimate:

  1. 0.20
  2. 0.26
  3. 0.30
  4. 0.24
Answer and explanation

D. Fraction 4/(4+6) = 0.4, so 0.24 M. The endpoints are not zero and 0.26 reverses weighting.

18. Part C — X: Ψs −0.8, Ψp +0.6; Y: Ψs −0.5, Ψp +0.1 MPa. Initial flow?

  1. Y to X
  2. No net flow
  3. X to Y
  4. Cannot compare
Answer and explanation

C. X totals −0.2; Y −0.4. X is higher.

19. Part C — Hydraulic permeability doubles while initial ΔΨ remains fixed. Which doubles?

  1. Initial flux magnitude
  2. Equilibrium potential
  3. Solute potential
  4. Gravitational contribution
Answer and explanation

A. In the linear model Jv = LpΔΨ. The other terms are not changed merely by increasing permeability.

20. Part C — A dry and a saline soil both have Ψw −0.5 MPa. Which inference is justified?

  1. Same ion toxicity
  2. Same initial driving difference against an identical root
  3. Same conductivity
  4. Unlimited supply
Answer and explanation

B. Equal potential gives equal initial driving difference; the mechanisms and conductivities can differ.

21. Part C — Cell osmotic volume doubles at conserved solute amount; initial 0.4 M becomes:

  1. 0.8
  2. 0.4
  3. 0.2
  4. 0.1
Answer and explanation

C. C = n/V, so doubling V halves C.

22. Part C — Equilibrated pressure chamber balancing pressure 0.8 MPa, negligible xylem sap osmotic effect. Approximate Ψw:

  1. −0.8
  2. +0.8
  3. 0
  4. −8
Answer and explanation

A. Negative of balancing pressure estimates the potential under these assumptions, not positive turgor.

23. Part C — Fresh 8 g, turgid 10 g, dry 2 g. RWC:

  1. 60%
  2. 75%
  3. 80%
  4. 125%
Answer and explanation

B. 100(8−2)/(10−2)=75%. The fresh/turgid ratio ignores dry matter.

24. Part C — At 298 K and 50% RH, approximate air Ψw:

  1. −0.5 MPa
  2. −9.6 MPa
  3. +96 MPa
  4. −96 MPa
Answer and explanation

D. 138 ln(0.5) ≈ −95.6 MPa. This is a vapor chemical-potential equivalent.

Assertion false; reason true.

Assertion false; reason true.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true.

Short-answer and experimental reasoning

31. Why does an osmometer reach a stationary level?

Model answer

Hydrostatic pressure balances the osmotic contribution; molecular exchange continues.

32. A cell at equilibrium in bath −0.30 MPa has final Ψs −0.95 MPa. Calculate pressure.

Model answer

+0.65 MPa under the two-term cell approximation.

33. Why does incipient plasmolysis not necessarily reveal original sap concentration?

Model answer

Water loss before zero turgor can concentrate sap. Cell heterogeneity and wall attachment affect onset.

34. Design two controls for potato tissue assay.

Model answer

Standardize temperature and blotting, use replicated pieces of equal geometry, and establish a time course to check equilibration.

35. Explain why 0.10 M real NaCl may not exert exactly twice the effect of 0.10 M sucrose.

Model answer

Nonideal ionic interactions and membrane-specific permeability alter effective osmotic behavior.

36. Why can water uptake decrease despite a growing gradient?

Model answer

Hydraulic conductance may decrease due to pore disconnection, aquaporin regulation or pathway failure.

37. A bath and a cell have equal solute potentials but the cell has positive turgor. Predict flow.

Model answer

Water initially leaves the cell, provided other terms are equal and the membrane permits it.

38. Why is a pressure chamber reading not direct mesophyll turgor?

Model answer

It reports balancing pressure for an excised hydraulic system under specified equilibration assumptions, rather than cell-specific positive pressure.

39. What differentiates osmotic flow from long-distance xylem flow?

Model answer

Osmosis involves a selective membrane; xylem mainly conducts liquid by bulk pressure-gradient flow with gravity.

40. Why can two leaves with identical RWC differ in Ψw?

Model answer

Solute content and wall elasticity can differ at the same relative hydration.

41. A root potential is −0.6 and soil total −0.8 MPa. What is the initial prediction?

Model answer

Water tends toward soil from root if a pathway exists; acclimation may change the potentials.

42. Why is the atmosphere's vapor water potential not xylem pressure?

Model answer

It represents chemical potential derived from relative vapor activity, not actual mechanical liquid pressure.
