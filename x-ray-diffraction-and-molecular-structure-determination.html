1. From molecular function to structural evidence

An enzyme binds one substrate much more strongly than another, although both may possess the same chemical groups. A mutation can abolish catalytic activity without substantially changing the mass of the protein. The explanation often lies in the relative positions and orientations of atoms: a catalytic group must approach a susceptible bond, a pocket must accommodate a ligand, and an interface must bring complementary surfaces together. Biochemistry identifies the relevant molecules and measures their behaviour, but a three-dimensional structural measurement can reveal how the arrangement of those molecules permits the behaviour. X-ray crystallography is a major method for constructing such a structural description.

The word structure has several meanings in biology. Primary structure specifies the sequence of covalently linked building blocks. Secondary structure describes local regular conformations, tertiary structure describes the arrangement of a folded chain, and quaternary structure describes the arrangement of multiple chains. A crystallographic atomic model can provide evidence at all these spatial levels, although the sequence is usually known beforehand from independent measurements. It supplies coordinate estimates for atoms that are sufficiently ordered in the specimen. The chemical bonds drawn between coordinates are interpreted according to chemical knowledge rather than independently photographed by the detector. At a lower level of detail, the arrangement of helices and sheets can be evident even where many side chains are ambiguous.

The scale presents a physical challenge. One ångström (Å) is one tenth of a nanometre, or 10⁻¹⁰ metre. A covalent carbon–carbon bond is typically about one and a half ångströms long, whereas a visible photon has a wavelength of several thousand ångströms. Optical microscopy cannot normally distinguish neighbouring atoms. X-rays have wavelengths comparable to interatomic separations and can therefore encode variations in the electron distribution on the required length scale. A short wavelength alone, however, does not make a structural measurement successful: sufficient scattered intensity, a suitable sample and an appropriate reconstruction method are essential.

A common misconception is to imagine an X-ray crystallographic detector as a very powerful camera recording a magnified image of the molecule. Instead the detector records radiation travelling in different directions after interaction with the electrons in the crystal. The pattern is related mathematically to the periodic electron distribution, and a subsequent reconstruction is necessary. Thus we must separate the specimen, the measured diffraction intensities, the reconstructed density and the fitted atomic model. These are different forms of information and have different uncertainties.

Crystallography developed from the demonstration of X-ray diffraction by crystals in the early twentieth century. The interpretation of diffraction using families of planes provided a simple geometrical connection between wavelength, scattering angle and lattice spacing. Applying these physical principles to proteins was much harder than applying them to small inorganic crystals: proteins contain thousands of atoms, have weak high-angle reflections and require a way to infer the phases of scattered waves. The early myoglobin and haemoglobin studies by John Kendrew and Max Perutz established that protein molecules could be described in three dimensions using diffraction evidence. Their work received the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The achievement was not merely the measurement of a regular protein lattice; it was reconstruction of the nontrivial molecular arrangement within that lattice.

Consider myoglobin as a recurring example. The protein contains a haem group within a folded polypeptide. Spectroscopic evidence can establish that oxygen interacts with haem, but the positions of surrounding side chains help explain the environment in which binding occurs. Crystallography can locate the haem relative to the protein and show which groups are near it; spectroscopy and binding experiments are then needed to determine how the local environment influences affinity and oxygen chemistry. A structural hypothesis and a functional hypothesis are therefore complementary. The static coordinate model should not be confused with a measurement of a reaction rate or with proof that a particular conformation is the only one occupied in solution.

The word determination can sound more absolute than the experimental process warrants. It is helpful to think of crystallography as an inverse problem. In a forward problem the investigator starts with an atomic arrangement and predicts the measurable pattern using a physical law. In an inverse problem the pattern is available and the arrangement must be inferred. There may be several candidate arrangements consistent with part of the data. The ability to calculate patterns from candidate models and reject inadequate explanations is what makes the inverse problem scientifically productive. The physical relation between the molecular arrangement and the intensity pattern is essential, but an accurate instrument alone does not solve the inference problem.

For example, a known polypeptide sequence strongly restricts the set of possible chemical models. Glycine lacks a carbon-containing side chain, whereas tryptophan has a large aromatic side chain. At sufficiently detailed density, an attempted sequence assignment that places a bulky tryptophan into a region having room only for a small group is suspect. Yet a glycine assignment is not proved merely because one region lacks a conspicuous side-chain feature. A large side chain may be disordered, and a weak map may fail to show it. The correct sequence and the connectivity to neighbouring residues must also be checked. The evidence for identification is the combination of continuity, shape, chemistry and independent sequence information.

This illustrates a broader principle in the methods of biology. A chromatogram separates chemical components, a fluorescence experiment can report proximity or environmental changes, and a crystallographic measurement constrains coordinates. None directly reports every molecular property. When two measurements answer different questions, their agreement can be more informative than repeating the same measurement. If the crystallographic model suggests that a ligand fits a narrow pocket, a biochemical competition experiment can investigate whether related molecules also occupy the pocket. A failure of competition would motivate reconsideration of the inferred affinity or access pathway, not necessarily rejection of the entire structural model.

Protein crystals are often much larger than the molecules that produce their pattern. The experimental advantage comes from repetition: many copies yield coherent scattering maxima whose locations are easier to measure than the scattering from an isolated molecule. A useful analogy is a regular row of identical antennas whose emitted waves reinforce at selected directions. This analogy captures interference but omits the detailed three-dimensional shape of each scattering unit and the fact that electron scattering is driven by incident radiation, not by individually powered antennas. It is the relationship between the direction-dependent amplitudes from the entire motif and the regular positions of its copies that encodes the structure.

A student reading a research paper should ask what was actually observed at each stage. A statement that a crystal diffracted to a particular spacing describes measured information or a processing decision. A statement that a chain contains a helix describes an interpretation of the reconstructed map. A statement that the helix transmits an allosteric signal is a functional proposal requiring additional evidence. A structural drawing may present all three in a visually persuasive way, but their evidential status is not identical. This distinction will recur in the interpretation of resolution, maps and validation.

2. Scattering, interference and the diffraction pattern

The electromagnetic wave consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. In X-ray scattering by ordinary biological matter, the electric field interacts predominantly with electrons. A small portion of incident radiation is redirected; for elastic scattering the outgoing wave has the same frequency and wavelength as the incoming wave. This elastic component is the basis of conventional crystallographic diffraction. Other interactions, including inelastic scattering and absorption, contribute background or radiation damage and are not equivalent sources of Bragg intensity.

To understand diffraction, picture two small scattering centres illuminated by the same plane wave. Each contributes a wave toward a distant detector. At that detector their electric fields add. If the peaks of one wave arrive with the peaks of the other, amplitudes reinforce one another; if a peak meets a trough of equal magnitude, they cancel. Intermediate relationships give intermediate resultant amplitudes. The relevant distinction is between amplitude, which can carry a sign or phase, and intensity, which is proportional to the square of the magnitude of the combined amplitude. Adding the intensities of coherent contributions would discard their interference.

A phase difference is conveniently measured as an angle: a path difference of one full wavelength corresponds to 2π radians, and a half-wavelength to π radians. Two equal-amplitude waves of magnitude A produce a resultant magnitude 2A if in phase, giving four times the intensity of either alone. If exactly out of phase, they produce zero resultant in the ideal two-wave case. This observation is essential to understanding why some reflections are absent despite many electrons being present. A dark spot is not necessarily a direction where no atoms scatter; it can be a direction where their contributions cancel.

A crystal contains many regularly arranged copies of a structural motif. When a large number of equivalent repeat units scatter coherently in a permitted direction, their amplitudes reinforce strongly. In other directions the phase changes from cell to cell and much of the scattered amplitude cancels. A finite ordered crystal therefore yields narrow maxima rather than the broad distribution of an isolated molecule. Smaller crystals and lattice disorder can broaden or weaken maxima. The detailed intensities are determined by the motif within the repeat unit, not merely by the total number of unit cells.

Atomic scattering factors express the scattering amplitude of individual atoms relative to the contribution of an electron under specified conditions. At very small scattering angle an ordinary neutral atom has a factor approaching its electron count. At greater angle the contributions of electrons at different positions within its own electron cloud become less coherent, so the factor generally decreases. This helps explain why high-angle scattering is challenging and why hydrogen, with only one electron, is often poorly distinguished in conventional macromolecular X-ray maps. Atomic identification cannot be made simply from the height of a single density maximum: occupancy, motion, neighbouring atoms and resolution all modify what is observed.

Diffraction from crystals is distinct from spectroscopy, which may monitor absorption or emission as frequency varies to infer energy levels or local environments. In a crystallographic experiment the wavelength is generally selected and the spatial variation of scattered intensity is measured. Wavelength changes can deliberately supply additional information in anomalous diffraction, but the principal structural information still comes from interference. It is also distinct from radiographic imaging, which measures attenuation along paths through a specimen. The physical word X-ray applies to all these methods without making their measured quantities interchangeable.

A convenient mathematical starting point is a sinusoidal electric field at a location on a detector, E(t) = A cos(ωt + φ). The amplitude A measures field magnitude, ω is angular frequency and φ is the phase offset. For two waves of the same frequency, E_total = A cos(ωt) + B cos(ωt + φ). Using trigonometric addition, the time-averaged squared field, up to a common proportionality constant, is A² + B² + 2AB cos φ when each separate intensity is expressed as A² or B² in the same convention. The last term is the interference term. It vanishes when averaged over rapidly varying unrelated phases, but remains important when the relative phase is stable.

As an example, let A = 3 and B = 4 in arbitrary amplitude units. If φ = 0, the normalized resultant intensity is (3 + 4)² = 49. If φ = π, the value is (3 − 4)² = 1. At φ = π/2, it is 9 + 16 = 25. These three values come from the same individual scatterers. Only their relative phase has changed. Unlike the equal-amplitude example, a pair of unequal amplitudes cannot cancel completely when opposed. This distinction will be useful when considering how a partial occupancy or substitution changes a reflection that is absent for two identical contributions.

Coherence does not require every pair of waves to have phase difference zero. It means that their phase relationship remains sufficiently well defined to support stable interference. The phase relationship between scattered waves from different positions changes with the direction of observation. In a crystal, a translation by one lattice repeat introduces the same direction-dependent phase change for every pair of equivalent motifs. The consequence is regular reinforcement at particular scattering vectors. A large number of repeats concentrates intensity in a small angular region, rather than simply increasing intensity equally in all directions.

A single motif has a direction-dependent scattering amplitude even when it is not repeated periodically. Imagine a rod-like distribution of electrons: contributions from its far ends can arrive almost in phase in a direction perpendicular to its long axis, while the path difference can become substantial in other directions. The scattering is therefore sensitive to the shape and orientation of the distribution. Repeating that motif on a regular lattice samples its scattering amplitude at the reciprocal lattice positions. The positions of Bragg spots reflect the translational periodicity; their intensities reflect both the motif and the quality of order.

The scattering factor of an atom should be distinguished from its atomic number. Atomic number is the count of protons in the nucleus. A neutral atom contains the same number of electrons, but an ion can differ. The small-angle electron scattering contribution of an atom is closely connected with its electron count. At higher angle the spatial spread of its electrons matters. An X-ray map does not usually distinguish neutral and ionic charge states from a simple peak-height rule. The chemically plausible state must be inferred by integrating environment and other measurements. This is particularly important for ions near proteins and nucleic acids.

Noncrystalline material also scatters X-rays. A solution of proteins has molecules at many positions and orientations; its scattered signal is usually much more diffuse than a single-crystal pattern. Solution small-angle X-ray scattering can constrain overall sizes and shapes, but the orientationally averaged pattern generally supplies less detailed atomic information than a suitable single-crystal dataset. A powder of many randomly oriented small crystals can instead show rings associated with plane spacings. This differs from a single crystal, which produces spatially separated indexed spots in favourable geometries. The sample form therefore changes the character and interpretability of the measurements.

3. Crystal lattices, unit cells and sample suitability

Crystalline order is described by a lattice, an abstract array of equivalent points related by translations. A motif associated with each lattice point gives the crystal its structure. In a macromolecular crystal the motif includes proteins or nucleic acids together with ordered solvent, ions and other bound components. The unit cell is a parallelepiped that reproduces the ideal lattice by translations. Its dimensions are a, b and c, with interaxial angles α between b and c, β between a and c, and γ between a and b. These parameters specify the geometry of the repeat, not the complete molecular coordinates.

The asymmetric unit is the smallest independently specified part of the crystal structure from which the rest can be generated by the space-group operations. It need not contain only one molecule, and its contents need not coincide with the functional assembly. For example, a biological dimer can be generated from one independently modelled subunit and a crystallographic symmetry operation. Alternatively, two chains within the asymmetric unit can be unrelated by exact crystallographic symmetry. A lattice contact between chains is not sufficient to prove that those chains interact in living cells.

The regularity of the crystal does not imply that all atoms are rigid. Protein crystals commonly contain aqueous channels between molecules. A chain may be well ordered in the folded core but adopt several positions in a solvent-exposed loop. The repetition of the average arrangement is sufficient for Bragg diffraction, while deviations from the average may contribute diffuse scattering or reduce the sharp reflection intensities. Different proteins, and different crystallization conditions for one protein, can yield crystals with different unit cells or space groups. This variation is often called crystal polymorphism and can influence the molecular environment in the lattice.

Crystal growth requires molecules to assemble into an ordered array rather than remain dissolved or aggregate without order. The thermodynamic idea of supersaturation is useful: the solution contains more solute than is stable at equilibrium with the crystal under those conditions. Excessive supersaturation can favour precipitation or excessive nucleation, while moderate conditions can permit ordered growth. Crystallographers examine the state of the sample, including molecular homogeneity, folding and aggregation, and explore conditions that favour regular assembly. A crystal that appears large and transparent may still be internally disordered, whereas a visually small crystal can diffract usefully. The decisive observation is the diffraction pattern.

Macromolecular crystals may be protected during collection, for example by cooling under suitable conditions, which can reduce some effects of radiation damage and motion. Cooling is not a guarantee of native-state structure; the crystallization environment and cooling process can shift populations of conformations. Ionising X-ray exposure can induce chemical changes, including changes at sensitive groups. Comparing early and late measurements can reveal dose-dependent alterations. Structural interpretations should distinguish features associated with biological conditions from those potentially caused by preparation or radiation.

Crystal packing also raises an important biological control. Suppose a flexible loop closes over a substrate pocket in a crystal containing no substrate. It is possible that a neighbouring symmetry-related molecule physically stabilizes the closed loop. Examining neighbouring molecules, comparing different crystal forms and examining the protein in solution help distinguish an intrinsic conformational preference from a packing effect. Absence of a large difference between structures is not evidence that no dynamic transitions occur: Bragg measurements report a periodic spatial average, not a time series of individual transitions.

A two-dimensional lattice is a useful way to learn the difference between translational symmetry and the appearance of a motif. Suppose every lattice point has a small asymmetric motif consisting of three differently arranged groups. Translating the whole motif by either primitive vector reproduces its neighbours. Rotating it by ninety degrees does not necessarily reproduce a neighbour. Translation symmetry is always present in the ideal periodic crystal, but rotation and other symmetry operations depend on the actual space group. One cannot determine the symmetry solely from the shape of one isolated molecule.

The term crystal system refers to classes of lattice symmetry and associated conventional cell geometry. The familiar cubic case has equal edges and right angles, while an orthorhombic cell has three mutually perpendicular edges that need not be equal. A tetragonal cell has two equal edges perpendicular to a third. Other crystal systems require different angular relations. These idealized geometries are useful for simple problems on plane spacings. They should not be confused with molecular symmetry: a molecule with no internal rotational symmetry may occupy a general position in a highly symmetric lattice because the symmetry relates different molecules, not necessarily parts of one molecule.

Fractional coordinates provide a compact description independent of the numerical length of the cell edges. In an orthogonal cell of dimensions 40, 50 and 60 Å, fractional coordinates (0.25, 0.20, 0.50) correspond to Cartesian offsets 10, 10 and 30 Å from the origin if the axes coincide with Cartesian directions. Adding one to any fractional coordinate represents translation to an equivalent position in the neighbouring repeat. A coordinate of 1.02 is periodically equivalent to 0.02 in the same direction, although a drawing of the full connected molecule may retain coordinates outside the conventional cell boundaries. For nonorthogonal cells the conversion requires the basis vectors, not multiplication alone.

A crystal may have more than one molecule in the asymmetric unit, and this sometimes supplies useful internal comparisons. If independent copies of a loop adopt distinct shapes, both may represent accessible conformations or one may be stabilized by particular packing contacts. A difference is not direct proof of a transition pathway. If independent copies are very similar, the result increases confidence in the reproducibility of that structural feature under those crystallization conditions, but it does not show that the protein lacks alternative states in solution.

The cell volume depends on the dimensions and angles. For an orthorhombic cell it is simply abc. A 40 × 50 × 60 Å cell has volume 120,000 Å³. In principle, given the molecular mass, cell contents and typical molecular partial specific volume, one can assess whether a proposed number of molecules per cell is compatible with realistic solvent content. For conceptual reasoning, a candidate arrangement that packs more molecular volume into the cell than the cell provides is impossible; a candidate with a plausible solvent fraction is only feasible, not proven. Crystal symmetry, diffraction intensities and the molecular model are still required.

The preparation of a specimen should be viewed as part of the measurement rather than as an incidental preliminary activity. A heterogeneous mixture can produce poorly ordered crystals or crystals of a component other than the intended molecule. Molecular purity and integrity measurements from the preceding molecular methods nodes can therefore support interpretation. A single band in an electrophoresis experiment is not complete proof of homogeneity in conformation or oligomeric state, so complementary measurements are helpful. The sample used to produce a structural model should be identified carefully enough to relate the structure back to the biological question.

The relationship between diffraction and crystal size illustrates the role of order. The idealized amplitude for N equally spaced scatterers along one dimension is the sum of N complex contributions with a constant phase increment. At a Bragg condition the amplitude scales with N and the ideal peak maximum scales with N². Between Bragg conditions the terms largely cancel. This maximum is not a statement that the total scattered energy increases quadratically without limit; as the peak becomes narrower, integrated intensity behaves differently. Finite size, absorption, imperfect coherence and sample geometry affect real measurements. The conceptual result is that long-range order concentrates the signal.

4. Bragg’s law: geometry and calculations

The scattering pattern from a crystal can be understood by considering sets of equally spaced parallel lattice planes. These are mathematical planes, not literal solid mirrors between molecules. The spacing d is the perpendicular separation between adjacent planes. An incident ray makes an angle θ with the plane; the ray directed toward the corresponding diffracted beam also makes θ with the plane in the simple geometrical construction. The angle between incident and outgoing beam directions is therefore 2θ.

Imagine two parallel incoming rays, one scattered from a point on the upper plane and another scattered from a corresponding point on the plane immediately below. The second ray travels an additional distance on the inward part and on the outward part of its journey. Projection of the interplanar distance onto each ray direction gives d sin θ. The total extra path is 2d sin θ. A constructive maximum occurs when this difference is an integer multiple of the wavelength. Hence Bragg’s law is nλ = 2d sin θ, where n is a positive integer, λ is the wavelength and d has the same length units as λ. In indexed diffraction patterns one usually absorbs the order into the Miller indices and uses the first-order form for each indexed reflection.

The restriction that sin θ cannot exceed one limits the possible combination of wavelength, spacing and order. At first order with λ = 1.50 Å and d = 3.00 Å, sin θ is 1.50 divided by 6.00, or 0.250. The inverse sine gives θ ≈ 14.48°, and a detector reporting scattering angle would record 2θ ≈ 28.96°. Reporting the half angle as the detector angle is a frequent factor-of-two error, not a small rounding difference. Conversely, if a problem gives 2θ = 60° at λ = 1.20 Å, θ is 30° and d equals 1.20 divided by twice 0.5, or 1.20 Å.

At fixed wavelength, larger θ corresponds to smaller d. High-angle reflections therefore carry information about short length-scale variations in the electron distribution. This does not mean that each particular high-angle reflection measures one covalent bond. Rather each reflection is a Fourier component determined by the arrangement of all scatterers in the unit cell. Many components are combined to obtain a density map. The finest available spacing establishes a nominal high-resolution cutoff, subject to data quality.

Changing wavelength alters the angles at which a specified plane spacing meets the diffraction condition. If the spacing remains unchanged while λ decreases, the necessary sine decreases. For a fixed detector angular limit, decreasing wavelength can in principle extend accessible reciprocal-space radius, but it does not automatically provide useful intensities at those radii. Crystal disorder, scattering strength, detector geometry and source characteristics still matter.

Bragg’s law predicts the geometry of reinforcement by equivalent planes. It does not predict the intensity without additional information on the contents of the unit cell. Consider two crystals with equal lattice parameters but different positions of atoms inside their cells. They may yield reflections in the same geometrical positions, yet some reflections can be much stronger in one than the other. This separation between geometry and intensity underlies the transition from Bragg’s equation to the structure-factor description.

The relation between interplanar spacing and the repeat distance along a crystal axis deserves careful attention. In a cubic lattice with unit-cell length a, the family (100) has spacing a, whereas the family (200) has spacing a/2. Calling the latter the second-order diffraction condition of (100) is compatible with the historical form of Bragg’s law, but the indexed reflection is normally described as (200) at order one. Using both a halved plane spacing and n = 2 for the same reflection would count the order twice. A problem must make clear which convention it uses.

Suppose a cubic cell has edge 40 Å and an X-ray wavelength of 1.00 Å. The (400) family has d = 10 Å. Its first-order sine is 1.00/(2 × 10.00) = 0.05, corresponding to θ ≈ 2.87°. The (800) family has d = 5 Å and θ ≈ 5.74°. Both angles are small, despite the second reflection having twice the spatial frequency. In a protein crystal whose unit-cell dimensions may be tens of ångströms, it takes relatively large Miller indices to reach spacings comparable with covalent bonds. An index value by itself is not a universal resolution measure because the cell parameters also enter the calculation.

An angular error can propagate into a spacing estimate. Starting from d = λ/(2 sin θ), a small change in θ at fixed wavelength changes d approximately according to Δd/d ≈ −cot θ Δθ when Δθ is expressed in radians. This follows by differentiating the logarithm of the expression. At small θ, cot θ is large, so a given small absolute angular error corresponds to a relatively large fractional spacing error. At θ = 10°, cot θ is about 5.67. An angular uncertainty of 0.1° is 0.001745 radians, giving a relative spacing uncertainty near 0.0099, or about one percent. This simplified calculation isolates angular error and assumes wavelength and all other calibrations are exact.

Bragg’s formula also supplies a useful feasibility test. With λ = 1.54 Å and d = 0.60 Å, first-order scattering would require sin θ = 1.54/1.20, which is greater than unity. No real Bragg angle satisfies those parameters. A shorter wavelength could make that spacing geometrically accessible, but the data would still need adequate signal and an appropriate detector. An impossible sine is therefore a diagnostic of an inconsistent problem or an inaccessible reflection, not an instruction to calculate an inverse sine outside its real domain.

In a common detector geometry, a flat panel placed perpendicular to the incident beam at distance L records a diffracted ray at radial distance R from the beam centre. In the idealized geometry, tan(2θ) = R/L. If L = 100 mm and R = 57.7 mm, the scattering angle is approximately 30°, so θ is about 15°. At wavelength 1.00 Å, d ≈ 1/[2 sin 15°] = 1.93 Å. Real data processing accounts for detector tilt, beam centre and additional geometric factors. This simple relationship explains why spots farther from the beam centre on a perpendicular detector generally correspond to smaller d for a fixed wavelength.

5. Miller indices and reciprocal space

A set of crystal planes is indexed by three integers h, k and l. A plane that intersects the direct-space axes at a/h, b/k and c/l is associated with indices (hkl), with a zero index indicating parallelism to the corresponding axis. Negative indices describe intercepts in the opposite direction. For example, (100) planes are parallel to the b and c directions, and (200) corresponds to half the spacing of (100). These indices identify a direction and a spacing; they are not the coordinates of one atom.

In the common crystallographic convention without a factor of 2π, the reciprocal basis vectors a*, b* and c* satisfy a*·a = 1, a*·b = a*·c = 0, and corresponding relations for b* and c*. Their units are inverse length. The vector g = h a* + k b* + l c* is perpendicular to the indexed planes and has magnitude 1/d(hkl). A lattice constructed from these vectors is the reciprocal lattice. This is a mathematical representation of spatial frequencies of periodic structures, not a second physical lattice inside the crystal.

For an orthorhombic cell, where all three angles are right angles, the reciprocal vectors have magnitudes 1/a, 1/b and 1/c and are mutually perpendicular. The magnitude squared of g is therefore h²/a² + k²/b² + l²/c², and d equals the reciprocal of the square root of this expression. In a cubic cell with edge a, this reduces to d = a/√(h² + k² + l²). For a cubic cell of edge 60 Å, the (300) spacing is 20 Å and the (330) spacing is 60/√18 ≈ 14.14 Å. Both are properties of a hypothetical lattice rather than distances between particular residues. For nonorthogonal cells the reciprocal metric includes angular terms, so the orthorhombic formula cannot be applied blindly.

The Ewald construction provides a geometric way to determine which reciprocal points meet the diffraction condition for a given crystal orientation and wavelength. Use vectors k_in and k_out each of magnitude 1/λ. The scattering vector k_out − k_in has magnitude 2 sin θ/λ and equals a reciprocal lattice vector for a Bragg reflection. This directly gives 1/d = 2 sin θ/λ and connects the reciprocal representation with Bragg’s law. A sphere of radius 1/λ can be drawn so that accessible reciprocal points lie on its surface. Rotating the specimen moves reciprocal points through diffraction conditions, allowing three-dimensional information to be sampled by successive detector images.

This also explains why recording only a single still image is generally inadequate for conventional single-crystal structure determination. A two-dimensional detector captures a subset of the three-dimensional reciprocal lattice for a given orientation. Rotation supplies additional observations, and symmetry can relate some observations to each other. Completeness is the proportion of expected unique reflections measured within a chosen resolution range. A high-quality measurement needs adequate completeness as well as a useful range of spacings. Missing data concentrated along one direction can cause directional uncertainty in the density map even if the nominal resolution number looks favourable.

The reciprocal vector can be understood without memorizing vector identities. Suppose we move within a particular direct-space plane. The quantity hx + ky + lz, where x, y and z are fractional coordinates, remains constant for all points of that plane. Adjacent members of the indexed family differ by one in this quantity. The vector perpendicular to those planes must therefore measure how rapidly that quantity changes with distance. For the reciprocal basis defined without 2π, the dot product of g with a displacement gives precisely this change in fractional phase cycles. A displacement along g of length 1/|g| changes the quantity by one. That is the geometric reason why the reciprocal vector magnitude is the inverse plane spacing.

Reciprocal-space representations occur in many wave problems. Spatial frequency in cycles per unit distance is the reciprocal of a period. The reciprocal lattice is a special discrete set of such frequencies imposed by crystal periodicity. A fine periodic pattern has a larger spatial frequency than a coarse one. The conventional unit Å⁻¹ is appropriate because the direct-space distances are measured in ångströms. Some physics texts define wavevectors with magnitude 2π/λ and reciprocal vectors with a factor of 2π. Both are valid conventions, but formulas must remain internally consistent. Combining a reciprocal lattice with 2π included and an Ewald radius of 1/λ would be incorrect.

Consider an orthorhombic crystal with a = 40 Å, b = 80 Å and c = 100 Å. For reflection (240), h/a = 2/40 = 0.05 Å⁻¹ and k/b = 4/80 = 0.05 Å⁻¹. Their vector sum has magnitude √(0.05² + 0.05²) ≈ 0.07071 Å⁻¹, giving d ≈ 14.14 Å. The reflection (400) has magnitude 0.10 Å⁻¹ and d = 10 Å. It has smaller d despite the sum of its indices being smaller than that for (240). The vector calculation, not an informal comparison of index numbers, determines the correct order of spatial frequencies.

When the crystal rotates, its direct lattice and reciprocal lattice rotate consistently. The incident beam and detector geometry establish a selection of possible outgoing directions at any instant. In the Ewald construction, the tip of the incident vector lies at the reciprocal origin if the sphere is drawn with its centre at the tail of that vector. A reciprocal point on the sphere then defines an outgoing vector of equal magnitude. The difference between that outgoing vector and the incident vector is the reciprocal vector from the origin to the point. This construction is a geometric restatement of elastic scattering and conservation of wavevector difference, not an independent empirical law.

The limiting reciprocal magnitude for elastic scattering at scattering angle 2θ is 2 sin θ/λ, and the mathematical maximum at θ = 90° is 2/λ. A practical instrument covers only a subset of all scattering directions. Crystal rotation changes the sampled region, while symmetry may supply equivalent observations. If a crystal orientation cannot be measured because of an obstruction or limited rotation, a wedge of missing reciprocal data may remain. The resulting density can be more uncertain along particular directions than others. A scalar nominal resolution number does not encode this directional distribution.

Indexing is the process of assigning integer labels to measured spots using a candidate lattice model and orientation. If two differently oriented crystals are simultaneously illuminated, their patterns may overlap; a single lattice model may fail to index all the spots. Twinning is a related but more specific crystallographic situation in which distinct domains have particular orientation relationships. Understanding the distinction is sufficient for examination reasoning: unexpected intensity relations can indicate problems with sample order or symmetry assumptions, and they should not be interpreted immediately as a novel molecular arrangement.

6. Structure factors and what the detector measures

To derive a useful expression for intensity, express the position of atom j in fractional coordinates (x_j, y_j, z_j) relative to the unit cell. The phase associated with its location in reflection (hkl) is 2π(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j) under one consistent Fourier sign convention. If f_j is the atomic scattering factor, the structure factor in an ideal static model is F(hkl) = Σ_j f_j exp[2πi(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j)]. The complex exponential compactly represents a vector of magnitude f_j pointing at the specified phase angle in the complex plane. Its real and imaginary components are cosine and sine contributions. Summing the vectors yields the resultant amplitude and phase.

After appropriate corrections and scaling, the observed intensity of a reflection is proportional to |F(hkl)|². The squared magnitude is the sum of the squares of the real and imaginary parts, not the sum of the individual atomic intensities. If two identical atoms have fractional x coordinates 0 and 1/2, and only reflections with k = l = 0 are considered, F(h00) = f[1 + exp(πih)]. For even h the terms add to 2f; for odd h they cancel exactly. The odd reflection has zero intensity in this ideal model. If one atom differs in scattering factor or occupancy, the cancellation need not be complete. In a real crystal, systematic absences arising from symmetry are valuable for distinguishing candidate space groups.

The atoms are not necessarily present at full occupancy in precisely one location. An occupancy factor weights the fraction of the crystal ensemble represented by a given atomic position. An isotropic displacement factor B is commonly incorporated as a multiplier exp[−B(sin θ/λ)²] on the atomic amplitude. Increasing B attenuates high-resolution contributions more than low-resolution contributions. B is conventionally expressed in Å²; for an isotropic Gaussian displacement model it relates to the mean-square displacement along a Cartesian direction U by B = 8π²U. Disorder, modelling errors and experimental factors can affect a refined B, so it should not automatically be interpreted as a direct measurement of molecular flexibility.

Practical data collection begins with a selected beam illuminating a mounted specimen. A detector records distributed intensity, and processing identifies spots, indexes their lattice coordinates, integrates intensity over each spot and subtracts estimated background. Scaling accounts for variation in exposure and other factors before equivalent observations are merged where appropriate. Because measured intensities have noise, an individual background-subtracted estimate can be negative; that does not imply a negative physical intensity or require that every such observation be discarded. Statistical treatments take the uncertainty into account when estimating amplitudes.

Repeated measurements provide redundancy and allow assessment of consistency. Weak reflections should be judged with their uncertainty, including how agreement varies across resolution shells. Completeness describes how much reciprocal-space information is present, whereas signal-to-noise measures describe measurement strength relative to uncertainty. These metrics answer different questions. A dataset with abundant repeated observations of a narrow region is not necessarily complete. Conversely, a complete but very weak high-angle shell does not guarantee that detailed features in a map will be reliable.

The structure-factor sum can be evaluated using elementary complex arithmetic. Let one atom contribute amplitude 4 at phase zero and another contribute amplitude 3 at phase π/2. The first contribution is 4 + 0i and the second is 0 + 3i. The resultant is 4 + 3i, with magnitude 5 and phase arctan(3/4), about 36.87°. Its intensity on a common proportional scale is 25. An intensity measurement of 25 would also be consistent with a resultant 5 + 0i, or −5 + 0i, or any other complex number on the circle of radius 5. This arithmetic prepares us to understand why phase inference is essential.

Atomic coordinates enter the phase expression through fractional positions. In a one-dimensional example, a single atom at x = 1/4 has a phase of 2πh/4. For h = 1 the phase is π/2; for h = 2 it is π; for h = 4 it is 2π, equivalent to zero. Moving the atom changes the phase by an amount that grows with h, making short-period Fourier terms especially sensitive to small displacements. This is one reason that high-resolution information helps distinguish close spatial alternatives, provided the intensities and phase estimates are adequate.

Occupancy and displacement parameters both modify amplitude, but their effects differ. A position occupied in half the crystal ensemble contributes roughly half the amplitude it would have at full occupancy under otherwise identical conditions. A B factor introduces an angle-dependent attenuation. Suppose two models assign a site the same occupancy and atomic scattering factor but B values of 10 and 30 Å². At d = 2 Å, sin θ/λ = 1/(2d) = 0.25 Å⁻¹. The ratio of displacement multipliers is exp[−(30−10) × 0.25²] = exp(−1.25) ≈ 0.287. At d = 4 Å the ratio is exp(−20 × 0.125²) ≈ 0.732. Thus the high-resolution contribution is relatively more attenuated. The expression describes individual atomic amplitudes; whole-reflection intensities depend on the sum with all other atoms.

Occupancy and B can be correlated during refinement because both can reduce predicted scattering from an atom. At limited resolution, it may be difficult to distinguish a partially occupied relatively ordered site from a more occupied but more disordered site. Introducing extra parameters does not necessarily create new information. The refined values should be interpreted in light of map shape, chemical constraints and the information available in the data. This parameter correlation is a concrete example of why coordinate models cannot be treated as collections of perfectly independent measured numbers.

Intensity integration is not merely reading the brightness of the brightest pixel. A reflection occupies a region of the detector and may be spread over successive images as the crystal rotates through its diffraction condition. Estimating its total intensity requires summing or modelling the spot signal and subtracting background. The processing software estimates uncertainties using available information about the measurement. A weak reflection may have an integrated estimate smaller than its uncertainty. Such observations are individually imprecise but can still carry aggregate information, so the statistical treatment of weak reflections matters.

Redundancy and internal consistency are useful but distinct concepts. If a reflection has repeated corrected intensity estimates of 100, 103 and 97 arbitrary units, the mean is 100 and the three observations are relatively consistent. A different reflection measured three times as 20, 50 and 80 has the same redundancy but much poorer consistency relative to the mean. Differences can arise from random counting errors, radiation damage, imperfect scaling or incorrect indexing. Internal merging statistics assess the consistency of repeated or symmetry-related observations; they do not test whether an atomic model fits the merged data. Rwork and Rfree address the latter question.

The correlation between independent half-datasets, often called CC1/2, provides a useful measure of reproducible variation in reflection intensities within a resolution shell. A positive correlation indicates that the two subsets tend to agree about which reflections are stronger and weaker. A low correlation indicates that much of the variation is not reproducible. The measure should be interpreted together with completeness, signal-to-noise and the purpose of the analysis rather than treated as a universal binary cutoff. At examination level, the distinction between measurement reproducibility and model agreement is more important than memorizing software-specific settings.

7. The phase problem and reconstruction of electron density

Each structure factor can be written as |F| exp(iφ), where φ is its phase. An ordinary diffraction detector yields information on the intensity and thus on amplitude, but not a direct measurement of φ for each reflection. The loss of the phases is the central phase problem of crystallography. It matters because the spatial location of the density depends on the relative phases of its Fourier components. Even an ideal set of exact amplitudes does not by itself provide a unique straightforward inverse transform.

For a consistent sign convention, the electron density at fractional position (x,y,z) can be expressed as ρ(x,y,z) = (1/V) Σ_hkl F(hkl) exp[−2πi(hx + ky + lz)], where V is the unit cell volume. With F expressed in electron units and V in Å³, the density has units of electrons per Å³. The observed structure factors are available only to a finite resolution and may lack some reflections. The calculated map is therefore an approximation to the periodic average density rather than a perfect, continuous description of every electron.

An elementary illustration uses one cosine density component, ρ(x) = ρ₀ + A cos(2πx + φ). Increasing φ by π moves the maximum to a location previously occupied by the minimum, but the component amplitude is unchanged. This is why an amplitude alone is insufficient to identify where a feature lies. Translating the entire contents of the unit cell produces phase changes but leaves diffraction intensities unchanged; an arbitrary choice of origin can thus produce equivalent descriptions. Other ambiguities, such as inversion under ordinary nonanomalous scattering, require appropriate chemical or experimental information.

Molecular replacement estimates initial phases by placing a previously determined related molecular structure within the new crystal cell. Rotation and translation are searched so that the model predicts diffraction observations consistent with the new dataset. The method is effective when the search model resembles the actual structure sufficiently. A poor search model can fail, while a plausible solution can still carry bias toward the template. A flexible domain may not occupy the position predicted by the search model, so the initial result must be rebuilt and tested against new data.

Isomorphous replacement compares data from a native crystal with a crystal in which a small number of strongly scattering atoms contribute additional scattering without materially altering the original lattice and molecular arrangement. The differences help locate the added scatterers. Their calculable complex contribution constrains the unknown native structure factors geometrically. A single derivative can leave phase ambiguity; multiple independent sources of information can resolve it. Failure of the isomorphism assumption complicates the interpretation because differences then reflect structural changes as well as the added scattering contribution.

Anomalous diffraction takes advantage of wavelength-dependent scattering near an absorption edge of an element. Under ordinary real scattering factors, Friedel-related reflections hkl and −h−k−l have equal intensities. An anomalous component introduces a complex contribution, and the two intensities may differ when the crystal and scatterers permit it. Such differences can help infer phases. Single-wavelength anomalous diffraction and multiwavelength anomalous diffraction are standard conceptual approaches; the distinction is whether the information is collected at one or more selected wavelengths. These methods require sufficient signal and careful data processing. They are not simply higher-resolution versions of conventional measurement.

The historical significance of phase estimation is particularly clear in the protein structure work of Perutz and Kendrew. Regular arrays of proteins gave diffraction patterns long before the full molecular arrangements could be read from them. Exploiting additional scattering contributions and calculating Fourier syntheses turned those patterns into interpretable structural evidence. The key examination distinction is that Bragg geometry tells where reflections can occur, intensity estimates the magnitude of a structure factor, and phasing supplies otherwise missing information needed for the density reconstruction.

A Fourier synthesis reconstructs a periodic function by adding components of different spatial frequencies, amplitudes and phases. In one dimension, a constant term represents the average density over the period, while sinusoidal terms describe how density varies about that average. At any chosen position the sinusoidal components may add constructively or destructively. Narrow density peaks require a combination of several frequencies. If the terms have correct amplitudes but phases that do not align appropriately, their maxima need not accumulate where the atoms are located. The reconstructed function can then be misleading even though the measured amplitude values are retained.

The inverse Fourier relation used in crystallography follows from the orthogonality of complex exponential functions over a periodic cell. Integrating the product of two distinct integer-frequency exponentials over one period gives zero; integrating equal-frequency contributions after complex conjugation gives a nonzero normalization. This permits a periodic density to be decomposed into coefficients and reconstructed from those coefficients. It also explains why the unit-cell volume appears in the inverse transform: the forward coefficient represents an integral of density over the cell, whereas the inverse returns density per unit volume. A full proof is not needed for the exam, but the conceptual separation between coefficient and spatial function is indispensable.

The reflection at zero spatial frequency corresponds mathematically to the total scattering electron content in the conventional description, often denoted F(000). It contributes a constant average density to the ideal synthesis. In conventional diffraction experiments it is not measured as an ordinary separate Bragg reflection because it lies in the direct beam direction. Model calculations and map conventions handle this term in practice. The example illustrates that even very complete data do not mean that every mathematical coefficient is measured without difficulty.

Phasing by molecular replacement has a particularly useful conceptual test. Imagine two related proteins share the same folded core but one contains an extra mobile domain. A model of the common core may provide enough initial signal to locate the molecule, but the domain may not be represented by the initial phases or search model. Once a solution is obtained, unexplained density should be investigated and the new model adjusted to the new data. Simply copying the extra region from a template or assuming its conformation is unchanged defeats the purpose of a structural experiment. Model agreement and validation should improve for the new crystal, not merely resemble the known structure.

In the isomorphous replacement approach, consider an idealized complex relation F_derivative = F_native + F_added. The magnitudes of the first two terms can be obtained from the two diffraction datasets, and the additional contribution can be estimated after its sites are identified. In the complex plane, the native structure factor lies at an intersection determined by circles with radii equal to the known magnitudes, displaced by the added contribution. Two intersections can occur. This explains why one derivative may leave alternative phase possibilities and why additional independent constraints can help. The construction depends on the approximation that the native contribution remains essentially unchanged in the derivative.

Anomalous scattering is useful because the imaginary contribution to the atomic scattering factor changes the simple conjugate relationship between Friedel-related reflections. If all factors are real, F(−h,−k,−l) is the complex conjugate of F(h,k,l) under the chosen convention, so their magnitudes are equal. With significant anomalous contributions, that equality need not hold. The differences between the two intensities provide additional constraints on the underlying scattering distribution. This is not a violation of the basic diffraction condition: reflection geometry and the complex scattering amplitudes play different roles.

Density modification can improve phase estimates using general features expected of macromolecular crystals, such as the distinction between relatively uniform disordered solvent regions and more structured molecular density. Information from repeated related copies of a molecule can also help when appropriate. Such approaches do not amount to making up missing detail arbitrarily. They apply defensible structural constraints to refine the inference, and their success depends on the accuracy of the assumptions. If a region incorrectly classified as solvent actually contains disordered molecular material, forcing it toward a uniform density may obscure information. The proper interpretation remains conditional on the data and the model assumptions.

8. Reading maps, resolution and local uncertainty

An electron-density map is often displayed by drawing contours at a selected density level. In three dimensions these contours become surfaces enclosing regions above the chosen threshold. A map contour is not the molecular surface in the biochemical sense. Its apparent shape changes with the threshold and with the map calculation. An atomic model displayed as sticks is overlaid to assess whether the sequence and chemically possible coordinates explain the density. At good resolution a well-ordered side chain can show distinct connected features; at poorer resolution the same side chain may be represented only by a broad envelope or may not be interpretable at all.

Nominal resolution in conventional crystallography is often stated as the smallest interplanar spacing d included in the measured or refined dataset. A structure at 1.5 Å is conventionally called higher resolution than one at 3.0 Å. This scale runs in the opposite direction from many ordinary quality scores. Smaller d corresponds to higher reciprocal spatial frequency. Truncating high-frequency information smooths a density map, just as omitting rapidly varying terms from a Fourier series smooths the represented function. But the analogy has limits: actual maps are affected by imperfect phases, missing reflections, atomic motion and noise in addition to the resolution cutoff.

Resolution is not the same as coordinate error. A well-constrained coordinate may be estimated with uncertainty considerably smaller than the nominal spacing, whereas a disordered loop may be poorly determined even in a globally high-resolution structure. The nominal limit applies to the dataset, and local interpretability varies throughout the model. For an experimental question about a single catalytic oxygen, the density surrounding that oxygen is therefore more relevant than the resolution value alone.

Common displayed maps include weighted combinations of observed amplitudes and calculated structure factors with estimated model phases. A 2mFo−DFc map is often used to inspect overall agreement between coordinates and density. An mFo−DFc difference map can reveal features that remain unexplained by the model. Positive difference density can indicate a missing atom or ligand, or an atom whose position is wrong. Negative difference density can indicate that the model places too much scattering matter at a location. A positive peak near a binding site is a reason to investigate, not by itself proof of a particular chemical identity.

Suppose a model assigns a water molecule to a peak adjacent to a negatively charged side chain, but the difference density remains strongly positive and the surrounding geometry suggests several oxygen ligands. A metal ion is one alternative explanation; occupancy, displacement factors, experimental conditions and anomalous information may help distinguish the hypotheses. A model assigning a metal without chemical or experimental support may fit the peak while giving an implausible coordination environment. Inferences about ions require integration of map fit, chemistry and independent evidence.

Consider an illustrative two-conformation loop with roughly equal populations. A map may show two weaker traces, or a broad ambiguous feature if the conformations are close. If the model contains only the first conformation at full occupancy, residual density may appear near the second. Adding alternate conformations is justified when both positions are supported and occupancies are physically meaningful. It is not justified simply to reduce a residual by adding enough arbitrary atoms. A missing model segment can be a scientifically appropriate expression of insufficient evidence rather than a failure to know the protein sequence.

It is often useful to distinguish three kinds of absence in a structural record. First, an atom can be chemically absent from the specimen, for example because the sequence differs. Second, it can be present in the specimen but contribute diffuse or weak density because it occupies many positions or has low occupancy. Third, it can be present and ordered but not represented adequately in a particular map due to phase errors, missing observations or processing limitations. These situations can yield similar visual impressions. Sequence data, sample characterization and a careful comparison of maps are needed to distinguish them.

A density contour at a stated number of standard deviations above the map mean is a display convention, not an automatic confidence interval for an individual atom. Lowering the contour level may reveal continuous weak features, but it also reveals more noise and diffuse density. Raising it can remove legitimate weak features. For comparisons, use consistent map type, scaling and contour conventions wherever practical. A figure showing a feature at an unusually low contour while the surrounding protein is shown at a higher contour can be informative if justified, but the viewer must be told what the comparison means.

Difference density is also a property of a calculated map, not a detector image of extra molecules. The difference coefficients combine observed amplitudes and model calculations using estimated phases and weights. A local positive feature can appear when the model underrepresents electron density; a local negative feature can occur when it overrepresents density. A misplaced atom often gives a paired positive and negative pattern: the new position is missing from the model and the old one has been overmodelled. A plausible coordinate adjustment should improve both features. Isolated peaks in noisy regions require caution because truncation and errors can create residuals unrelated to a missing atom.

The chemical identity of a small ligand can be especially difficult at moderate resolution. Two orientations of an aromatic compound can have similar shapes and similar electron counts. At high detail, substituent density and the contacts expected for different functional groups may distinguish them. At poorer resolution both may fit an envelope. It is preferable to state that orientation is uncertain than to draw a precise orientation and infer a specific hydrogen bond without support. The identity of a ligand can be established independently by knowing what was supplied or by chemical analysis, but its occupancy and pose still require structural evidence.

Hydrogen atoms contribute relatively weakly to X-ray scattering and are often not independently resolved in typical macromolecular structures. Hydrogen-bond geometry is therefore commonly inferred from the positions of heavier donor and acceptor atoms and chemical knowledge. Very high-resolution X-ray data can sometimes reveal hydrogen density, and neutron diffraction offers complementary sensitivity because neutrons interact with atomic nuclei rather than electron clouds. The useful examination rule is not that X-rays can never detect hydrogen, but that ordinary protein X-ray maps often do not establish its position directly. Claims about protonation and hydrogen transfer require stronger evidence.

A high-resolution dataset can still have poor local information for a mobile loop. Suppose the core of a protein has reproducible, sharply defined density and the last eight residues have weak, discontinuous density. Assigning all eight residues precise coordinates merely because the data extend to 1.5 Å would overstate the evidence. The global resolution describes the highest spatial frequencies included; it does not ensure that every feature contributes strongly at those frequencies. A transparent model can omit residues that cannot be placed defensibly and record their presence in the sequence information separately.

A nominal resolution cutoff is also not an exact physical boundary beyond which all information is absent. Reflection data near the outer shell may vary greatly in quality and directional coverage. Some contain signal even where others are weak. A practical cutoff is a decision based on the available observations and their contribution to reconstruction and refinement. If a higher-resolution shell is included, one should examine whether it improves the model and validation rather than assuming that the smaller stated number is invariably beneficial.

9. Model building, refinement and independent validation

After initial phase estimation, the density is inspected and a molecular model constructed. The known sequence provides a strong constraint on which residues can occur and in what order. Stereochemical knowledge provides expected ranges for bond lengths, angles and conformational preferences. The model is then used to calculate predicted structure factors. Differences between observed and predicted amplitudes and discrepancies in the map guide changes to atomic coordinates, displacement factors, occupancies and other model parameters. Iteration improves the model and can improve phase estimates, but introduces the possibility of bias because model and map are no longer independent.

Refinement is an optimization under incomplete information. An objective function contains a measure of agreement with diffraction observations together with restraints that favour plausible molecular geometry. Restraints are particularly important when the number of adjustable parameters is large relative to the number of informative observations. Without them, a model might distort bond lengths and angles to follow random errors in the dataset. With excessively strong restraints, genuine departures from ideal geometry might be suppressed. The goal is a model supported by data and chemistry, not merely a minimum value of one residual.

An amplitude residual is conventionally defined as R = Σ ||Fo| − |Fc|| / Σ |Fo| over a specified reflection set. The denominator normalizes the absolute amplitude discrepancies by the total observed amplitude. For illustrative observed amplitudes 100, 60 and 40, and calculated amplitudes 90, 70 and 35 on a common scale, the numerator is 10 + 10 + 5 = 25 and the denominator is 200. R = 0.125 or 12.5%. These invented three-reflection values are for calculation only and are not a realistic crystallographic quality assessment. The residual is not calculated from the squared intensities in this definition.

Rwork describes the agreement on reflections used in refinement. Rfree is calculated using a designated set of observations held out from fitting. The test set is chosen in advance and should not be repeatedly selected or used to tune the model, because its purpose is to provide an independent check of predictive agreement. Rfree often exceeds Rwork. A large gap may suggest overfitting, but data quality, the size and independence of the test set, and model problems also influence the interpretation. Neither a small gap nor a low work residual alone establishes biological correctness.

Validation has complementary levels. Global measures include resolution, completeness and overall residuals. Geometry measures include deviations of bond lengths and angles, clashes between atoms, side-chain rotamer outliers and the distribution of protein backbone dihedral angles on the Ramachandran plot. Local agreement can be evaluated with real-space correlation coefficients or real-space residuals comparing model-derived and observed map information near a residue. A model may have convincing global scores while a ligand, catalytic group or loop remains unsupported. Conversely an unusual backbone conformation may be genuine if independently supported by strong density and surrounding interactions.

A practical reasoning pattern is to compare alternative hypotheses with the same data and validation rules. An investigator testing a ligand pose should inspect local density and difference features, consider ligand stereochemistry, compare plausible alternative orientations and report uncertainty when they cannot be distinguished. An omit-map approach can reduce direct dependence on the omitted feature, although it does not make all model bias disappear. A ligand-binding assay or a carefully controlled mutation study can assess functional consequences, complementing the structural evidence.

Protein Data Bank entries commonly provide model coordinates together with crystallographic experimental information and a validation report. The reader can distinguish the deposited model from the underlying diffraction observations and ask how well the model fits. The report is not an automatic certificate that every biological claim derived from the structure is true. The distinction between an asymmetric unit and a biological assembly, the possibility of crystal-packing effects, and the local evidence at sites of interest remain important even when formal validation is satisfactory.

Model building initially involves identifying the chain trace and establishing which residues correspond to that trace. A continuous region of density with repeating backbone features can suggest a helix or strand. The sequence and the appearance of characteristic side chains help establish the register, which is the correspondence between successive residue identities and positions. A register shifted by one residue can produce apparently continuous backbone coordinates but place side chains in incorrect locations. The error can persist if refinement is allowed to adjust positions without reconsidering the sequence assignment. Detecting it requires visual map inspection and checking the sequence-dependent chemical environment.

Stereochemical restraints embody knowledge gained from many reliable molecular structures and chemistry. The typical C–N peptide bond is shorter than an ordinary single C–N bond because of partial double-bond character, and the peptide group tends toward planarity. Restraining these features prevents noisy diffraction information from producing unrealistic geometry. Backbone φ and ψ angles describe rotations around bonds adjoining the alpha carbon, and their combinations are restricted by steric interactions. Glycine and proline have distinct conformational preferences. A Ramachandran outlier is thus a warning requiring inspection, not an instruction to automatically change every unusual conformation to the most common one.

Refinement and validation can be compared to fitting a curve to experimental measurements. A flexible model can reduce residuals by following noise; a separate test dataset helps detect that problem. The analogy is valuable because both tasks involve parameter estimation and prediction. Its limits are that crystal structure factors are complex, observations may be related by symmetry and atomic geometry supplies independent constraints. In a crystallographic test set, related observations should not inadvertently leak information into the working set. A very small test set can yield a noisy Rfree estimate, so the comparison must be interpreted statistically.

Consider two hypothetical refinements of the same dataset. Model A has Rwork = 0.21 and Rfree = 0.25, while model B has Rwork = 0.16 and Rfree = 0.32. The work fit of B is numerically better, but the held-out prediction is worse and the gap has increased from 0.04 to 0.16. This is consistent with overfitting or another modelling problem. It is not a proof that B is wrong in every local feature. The investigator should inspect model complexity, occupancy and displacement parameters, stereochemistry and local density. Changing the validation set until B appears favourable would invalidate the comparison.

Now consider models C and D with Rwork/Rfree of 0.22/0.26 and 0.21/0.25, respectively, but D has a severe steric clash in the active site. The small improvement in global fit does not justify a chemically implausible clash automatically. The clash may signal an incorrect rotamer, alternate conformation, sequence assignment or ligand pose. If strong independent density and chemical evidence support an unusual arrangement, a genuine exception may be justified. If the local map is poor, a chemically plausible alternative or an explicit statement of uncertainty is preferable.

A local correlation measure tests whether model-calculated and map values rise and fall together around a selected feature. It is distinct from the global amplitude residual because it focuses on a region in real space rather than all indexed observations. For an enzyme mechanism, a local metric near the active site can matter more than the average metric over the whole molecule. A high local correlation still does not uniquely prove an atom's chemical identity, since similar electron distributions can yield similar correlations. Combining local fit with difference density, stereochemistry, occupancy and biochemical context produces stronger validation.

A coordinate file is a scientific model with metadata, not merely a three-dimensional picture. A structural database record may include the polymer sequence, chains, coordinates, atomic displacement parameters, occupancies, experimental method, crystal cell, space group and reported resolution. Structure factor data and a validation report provide additional evidence. When comparing entries, ensure that the reported sequence and conditions are appropriate, that the correct assembly is selected and that the region under investigation is actually modelled. Comparing two visually attractive rendered structures without examining their data quality can lead to unjustified conclusions.

10. Connecting diffraction evidence to biological mechanisms

Structural determination is valuable because a sequence of atoms can suggest testable functional explanations. In a binding protein, comparison of the empty and occupied sites can identify groups that are close enough for favourable interactions. A structure alone does not give the free energy of those interactions, because binding also depends on desolvation, entropy and changes throughout the system. The presence of a short separation in a model should therefore be treated as evidence of possible interaction rather than a numerical contribution to binding affinity.

A comparison of two structures requires consistent definitions. To ask whether a protein changes shape upon ligand binding, align comparable regions of the models and inspect differences relative to the uncertainty of the coordinates. Apparent shifts may arise from a different crystal form or solvent environment. Independent comparisons of ligand-bound and unbound specimens, preferably with similar resolution and conditions, improve the inference. If the difference is smaller than plausible local uncertainty, it is inappropriate to draw a detailed mechanistic conclusion from it.

Myoglobin and haemoglobin provide a classical example of how a common structural motif can occur in molecules with different biological roles. The presence of a haem pocket in both supports common aspects of oxygen binding chemistry. Haemoglobin has multiple subunits that can rearrange relative to one another, making its structure relevant to cooperative oxygen binding. The relationship between structural conformations and measured oxygen-binding curves supports allosteric models. The diffraction experiment alone does not measure the Hill coefficient or determine the full thermodynamic distribution of states. This requires quantitative functional experiments.

In an illustrative enzyme study, a mutant has the same overall fold as the wild-type model but a different side-chain orientation near the substrate site. Its catalytic rate is reduced while its substrate-binding affinity is almost unchanged. This combination supports the hypothesis that the mutated group influences the chemical step rather than gross folding or initial association. Other explanations remain possible, including changed conformational dynamics. A rigorous interpretation integrates the structure with kinetic measurements, folding controls and alternative substitutions rather than declaring the hypothesis proven solely by proximity to the substrate.

Different structural methods respond to different physical properties. X-ray crystallography is especially valuable when a sufficiently ordered crystal is obtainable and can provide detailed average electron density. Solution NMR uses magnetic-resonance measurements and can provide constraints on structures, motions and interactions under suitable solution conditions. Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy uses images of many particles in different orientations to reconstruct a three-dimensional map without a conventional crystal. The next node develops these comparisons more fully. Method choice depends on the molecular system, the biological question and the expected uncertainty, not on a universal ranking of technologies.

For examinations, work backward from the observable. A changed reflection intensity indicates a change in scattering contributions or measurement conditions; it does not automatically demonstrate a conformational transition. A missing density feature can reflect motion or low occupancy rather than chemical deletion. A good refined coordinate fit supports a model under specified assumptions, but does not uniquely establish every proposed cellular function. These distinctions allow structural data to be combined with biochemical evidence without confusing observation, reconstruction, model and mechanism.

Structural comparisons can be carried out at several levels. A rigid-body superposition minimizes positional differences for a chosen set of corresponding atoms by allowing translation and rotation. The root-mean-square deviation, or RMSD, is the square root of the average squared distance between matched coordinates after the chosen alignment. The meaning of the number depends on which atoms are matched. A small RMSD for a folded core can coexist with a substantial movement of a flexible domain excluded from the alignment. A larger RMSD across all atoms can be dominated by a few mobile loops even if most of the fold is essentially unchanged.

For an illustrative comparison, suppose four matched atoms have distances of 0.2, 0.2, 0.4 and 1.0 Å after alignment. The squared distances sum to 0.04 + 0.04 + 0.16 + 1.00 = 1.24 Å². Dividing by four gives 0.31 Å², and the square root is about 0.56 Å. The largest displacement has a disproportionately large effect because the distances are squared. If the last atom belongs to a poorly resolved loop, the result may be influenced by coordinate uncertainty. Reporting the matched atom set and inspecting the individual differences is therefore more informative than quoting RMSD alone.

A structural proposal for molecular recognition can be tested with independent changes in chemistry. Suppose a model shows a charged side chain near a ligand bearing the opposite charge. Replacing that side chain with an uncharged residue might weaken binding, but it might also destabilize the protein or alter the pocket geometry. A folding control and an appropriate comparison mutation can help discriminate these alternatives. If the mutant retains its fold but loses binding, the result strengthens the hypothesis that the local interaction matters. It does not isolate an exact electrostatic energy unless other thermodynamic contributions are considered.

Protein–nucleic acid structures illustrate another reason for combining techniques. A crystallographic model may show that a protein approaches particular base edges or the phosphate backbone. Sequence-specific binding can be investigated by changing those bases and measuring affinity. A short contact to a phosphate group alone does not establish sequence specificity because phosphates occur repeatedly along the backbone. Conversely a change in base sequence can influence binding indirectly by altering local shape or flexibility. A structure can guide hypotheses about direct and indirect readout, while binding measurements test their functional consequences.

Structural evidence is also relevant to the design of molecules that bind a known protein, because a pocket's shape and neighbouring functional groups suggest candidate interactions. However, a static structure cannot by itself guarantee high affinity, selectivity or biological effectiveness. The candidate ligand may incur a large desolvation penalty, prefer a different conformation, or fail to reach the target in a biological system. The scope here is the interpretation of structural evidence, not clinical or pharmacological prediction. In a typical NET question, the strongest conclusion is the one that follows directly from the combination of the stated structural and biochemical observations.

A final distinction concerns dynamic information. A model of two alternate conformations and their refined occupancies may suggest structural heterogeneity in the crystal. It does not specify the rate at which individual molecules interconvert, or even prove that interconversion occurred on the measurement timescale rather than different molecules retaining different states. Time-resolved experiments and solution techniques can probe dynamics in complementary ways. The next node discusses alternative structural methods; the present chapter establishes the logic needed to judge any reconstructed map and fitted model.

11. Experimental reasoning: from specimen to a validated model

An experimental study begins with a biological question rather than an assumption that a certain structural method will necessarily be successful. Consider a purified oxygen-binding protein suspected of binding a small molecule at a site remote from the haem. A useful question is whether the small molecule occupies a distinct pocket and whether occupancy correlates with a structural change in the oxygen-binding environment. A diffraction experiment can address the location and structural comparison if the protein forms suitable crystals, but an oxygen-binding assay is needed to establish a functional effect. The study therefore has at least two logically independent measurements.

The specimen identity and integrity are important controls. The molecular sequence, any truncation or tag, ligand exposure and composition of the crystal should be documented. A preparation that has partially degraded could form crystals of a stable fragment rather than the intended full-length chain. In that case a beautifully ordered diffraction pattern would still fail to answer the original question about the intact protein. Sample analysis can help detect degradation; the model sequence and molecular connectivity provide further checks. Successful diffraction is not the same as successful identification.

A standard conceptual workflow includes producing a suitable specimen, identifying crystals, collecting diffraction data at a series of orientations, processing the images, choosing a consistent lattice and symmetry model, obtaining phases, calculating initial maps, building and refining coordinates, and validating the result. Each stage changes the form of evidence. Detector frames contain intensities distributed across positions. Processing yields indexed intensities with uncertainty estimates. Phase estimation provides the missing complex information. The reconstructed density constrains possible atomic arrangements. Refinement yields a model, and validation examines how well it is supported. Keeping these levels distinct makes it easier to locate the cause of an apparent failure.

Suppose that a crystal produces numerous sharp spots but the indexing procedure finds two incompatible lattices. The immediate problem concerns the assignment of measured spots to one consistent reciprocal-space representation. It would be premature to discuss whether an amino acid adopts an alternative rotamer, since the relevant molecular map has not yet been established. Conversely, if indexing and processing succeed but an initial phase calculation produces an uninterpretable map, the failure may concern the search model, phase information or the adequacy of the data. Improving a model at this point is not simply a matter of increasing the number of coordinate parameters.

For a simple quantitative comparison, consider three hypothetical datasets of the same protein. Dataset A extends to 1.8 Å with 98% overall completeness, but its outer shell has poor reproducibility and substantial directional gaps. Dataset B extends to 2.1 Å with 99% completeness and stronger reproducible outer-shell information. Dataset C extends to 3.0 Å with high completeness but a different crystal form. These invented examples illustrate that no single number determines which dataset best addresses a question. A may contain useful fine detail, B may support a more uniform and reliable map, and C may provide an independent packing environment. A defensible assessment would inspect shell-specific information and map/model agreement before ranking their suitability.

If the purpose is to discriminate between two closely spaced ligand orientations, the local map in datasets A and B may be especially important. If the purpose is to test whether a loop shape is strongly influenced by crystal packing, the distinct crystal form in C may be valuable despite its lower nominal resolution. A study that includes only the dataset with the smallest stated spacing could miss a crucial alternative explanation. Experimental evidence should be chosen for its ability to distinguish the competing hypotheses.

Controls for interpreting a difference between ligand-free and ligand-bound structures include matching molecular identity and considering crystal packing, occupancy, solvent and data quality. An apo structure lacks the specific ligand under discussion, not necessarily every molecule other than the protein; solvent, ions and cofactors may still be present. A holo structure contains the relevant bound component. A ligand-dependent difference in the density is strengthened by evidence that the specimen actually contains the ligand and by a consistent local environment. A changed global unit cell may reflect altered packing rather than a local biochemical mechanism. The expected outcome should therefore be specified at both the molecular and crystal levels.

Negative findings require similar care. Failure to observe density for a ligand can mean that it does not bind detectably, that binding is weak or incomplete under the experimental conditions, that the bound ligand is disordered, or that the available map cannot resolve it. It would be incorrect to infer universally that the protein and ligand never interact. An independent affinity measurement under suitable conditions can distinguish some of these possibilities. If such a measurement reveals binding but the crystallographic density is weak, the disagreement may motivate a change in sample conditions or a complementary structural method rather than a false claim that one measurement must be invalid.

The ultimate output of a structural study is an explanation whose precision is matched to the evidence. A well-supported fold can be reported confidently while the side-chain orientation at one site remains uncertain. The researcher need not choose between declaring the entire structure correct and declaring it useless. Different conclusions have different evidential requirements. In a Part C question, identify the specific observation that supports each proposed conclusion and reject conclusions requiring information that the experiment did not provide.

12. Integrated calculations and reading structural data

Quantitative reasoning becomes easier when each calculation is placed at the correct stage of the workflow. A Bragg calculation links a measured angular position with an interplanar spacing. An amplitude calculation links diffraction intensity with the magnitude of the structure factor after scaling. A refinement residual compares observed and calculated magnitudes for a set of reflections. None of these calculations directly yields the biological binding affinity of a protein. Mixing equations across these stages produces answers that may have the right units but the wrong meaning.

Consider an illustrative orthorhombic cell with edges a = 30 Å, b = 40 Å and c = 50 Å. For reflection (345), the components of the reciprocal vector are 3/30 = 0.10 Å⁻¹, 4/40 = 0.10 Å⁻¹ and 5/50 = 0.10 Å⁻¹. Its magnitude is √0.03 = 0.1732 Å⁻¹. The spacing is 1/0.1732 = 5.77 Å. At wavelength 1.00 Å, sin θ = 1/(2 × 5.77) ≈ 0.0866, giving θ ≈ 4.97° and detector scattering angle 2θ ≈ 9.94°. This calculation passes directly from the cell geometry to a predicted spot angle. The intensity remains unknown because the contents of the cell have not been specified.

If a second specimen has the same orthorhombic cell but a molecular substitution inside it, the corresponding (345) spot can have a different intensity while its geometrical angle remains approximately unchanged. If instead all cell lengths increase by ten percent at the same wavelength, the reciprocal components each decrease by a factor of 1.1 and the spacing increases by a factor of 1.1. The Bragg angle decreases. These effects distinguish a change in the periodic geometry from a change in the electron distribution within the repeat, although in real biological crystals both can occur together.

The relation I ∝ |F|² requires attention to scale. If two corrected intensities measured on a common scale are 900 and 400 arbitrary units, the ratio of amplitudes is √(900/400) = 1.5, not 2.25. Without knowing the proportionality constant we can compare relative amplitudes but cannot assign absolute electron units. A reflection whose intensity doubles has an amplitude increase by a factor √2 under these assumptions. If the measurements have not been corrected and placed on a common scale, even that ratio may be misleading.

To connect phase and structure, suppose an illustrative one-dimensional cell contains two equal point scatterers at fractional positions zero and one quarter. For h = 1, the contributions have relative phase π/2 and F = f(1 + i), with magnitude √2f and intensity proportional to 2f². For h = 2, they have relative phase π, so F = 0. For h = 4, their phase difference is 2π, so F = 2f and intensity is proportional to 4f². If the second scatterer moves to one half, the h = 1 and h = 2 behaviour changes: the first now cancels and the second reinforces. This is a direct demonstration that reflection intensities encode positional information even for a fixed lattice.

Refinement residuals must be computed over the correct sets. Suppose the working set has total observed amplitude 1,000 and a sum of absolute amplitude discrepancies of 190. The working residual is 0.19. If the designated free set has total observed amplitude 100 and total discrepancy 25, the free residual is 0.25. The gap is 0.06, or six percentage points. It is not obtained by subtracting sums of discrepancies directly, because the sets have different denominators. If refinement changes the working discrepancy to 150 but the free discrepancy rises to 30, the two residuals become 0.15 and 0.30. The larger gap warns that the apparent improvement on the fitted observations is not reproduced in the test observations.

In a separate illustrative example, a protein model has a resolved core and a flexible terminal segment. Its overall R values are satisfactory, but the terminal residues have weak density and several steric clashes. The correct response is not to use the global R values as proof that the terminal model is correct. The discrepancies indicate that the terminal region requires independent inspection. A shorter model with an explicitly unmodelled terminal segment can be more scientifically accurate than a complete-looking coordinate list. This is a case where the appropriate answer is a qualified interpretation, not a numerical calculation.

13. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Incorrect: The detector photographs atoms. Correct: It records scattered intensities; density and coordinates are inferred. Rendered molecular graphics look like images. In an exam, identify the actual observable before deciding what is measured.

  2. Incorrect: Bragg planes are physical mirrors. Correct: They represent mathematical families of periodic positions. Reflection language invites optical imagery. Derive path difference from coherent scattering rather than literal mirror surfaces.

  3. Incorrect: θ is the full scattering angle. Correct: The full angle is 2θ. Instrument reports commonly show 2θ. Halve that angle before applying Bragg’s formula.

  4. Incorrect: Higher order and smaller indexed spacing are independent multipliers. Correct: Order can be incorporated in hkl; avoid double counting. Textbooks use both notations. State the convention and use either nλ with the original spacing or first order for the higher-index family.

  5. Incorrect: An intense reflection has a large phase angle. Correct: Intensity reports magnitude squared, not phase angle. Both occur in the complex coefficient. Use magnitude for intensity and reserve phase for interference and reconstruction.

  6. Incorrect: Zero intensity means no electrons are present. Correct: Destructive interference can cancel nonzero contributions. Students treat intensity as an atom count. Sum complex amplitudes before squaring.

  7. Incorrect: A reciprocal lattice is a second physical lattice. Correct: It represents discrete spatial frequencies. Diagrams can look like real crystal arrays. Check the inverse-length units and relation |g|=1/d.

  8. Incorrect: The asymmetric unit always equals the biological assembly. Correct: Assembly and crystallographic symmetry are distinct. A viewer may display one convenient chain grouping. Verify solution evidence and assembly information.

  9. Incorrect: Smaller d means poorer resolution. Correct: Smaller d corresponds to finer spatial frequencies. Ordinary quality scores often increase with quality. In crystallography the reported spacing decreases.

  10. Incorrect: A small resolution number guarantees every atom is reliable. Correct: Local disorder and map quality determine site-specific confidence. Global statistics summarize a dataset. Inspect the exact site queried in the question.

  11. Incorrect: Rwork alone determines model quality. Correct: Rfree, geometry and local agreement supply complementary tests. A fitted residual decreases as model flexibility increases. Examine withheld prediction and plausibility.

  12. Incorrect: Rfree should be repeatedly minimized using test reflections. Correct: The free set must remain independent of fitting. The name looks like a second optimization target. Treat it as evaluation, not training data.

  13. Incorrect: A positive difference peak proves a ligand’s identity. Correct: It suggests unexplained density; several hypotheses may fit. A map peak appears visually persuasive. Check chemistry, local shape, conditions and alternate models.

  14. Incorrect: No visible density means no molecule or residue exists. Correct: Disorder, low occupancy and limitations can obscure a present group. Students equate a model with full chemical composition. Compare sequence and sample evidence.

  15. Incorrect: Large B is a direct measurement of dynamic rate. Correct: B represents attenuation associated with displacement/disorder and model factors. Thermal-motion terminology invites a kinetic interpretation. Distinguish spatial spread from transition rate.

  16. Incorrect: All high-resolution structures directly reveal hydrogen atoms. Correct: Hydrogen scatters weakly and is often not independently resolved. Atomic models draw inferred bonds. Check actual map detail and complementary evidence.

  17. Incorrect: A crystal contact proves the same interaction occurs in cells. Correct: Packing can stabilize nonbiological contacts. The contact is geometrically real but may lack physiological relevance. Seek independent functional or solution measurements.

  18. Incorrect: A structural difference proves a catalytic or allosteric mechanism. Correct: Structural differences suggest hypotheses requiring functional tests. Visual comparisons can seem causal. Evaluate packing, local uncertainty and independent kinetics or binding data.

14. Part B — conceptual practice

Original practice questions; these are not previous-year examination questions.

Question 1. What does an ordinary X-ray diffraction detector directly record?

  1. Atomic coordinates
  2. Electron-density contour surfaces
  3. Scattered intensities at detector locations
  4. Phases of all structure factors
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — Scattered intensities at detector locations. The detector measures scattered radiation intensity at positions on its surface. Coordinates and density maps require reconstruction, and ordinary intensity measurement does not retain phase.

Question 2. Which wavelength scale is appropriate for resolving atomic-scale order?

  1. Several kilometres
  2. Several hundred micrometres
  3. Approximately one ångström
  4. Several metres
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — Approximately one ångström. An ångström is 10⁻¹⁰ m, comparable with interatomic distances. The other wavelengths are much longer and cannot provide the spatial frequencies needed for conventional atomic crystallography.

Question 3. What is the dominant source of ordinary elastic X-ray scattering by biological molecules?

  1. Electron clouds
  2. Nuclear spin transitions
  3. Proton radioactive decay
  4. Fluorescent labels
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Electron clouds. X-rays interact primarily with electrons in ordinary crystallography. Nuclear spin transitions concern NMR, radioactive decay is unrelated, and fluorescent tags are not needed for the basic diffraction process.

Question 4. Two equal coherent amplitudes A reach a detector in phase. Relative to the intensity of one, the resulting intensity is:

  1. One
  2. Two
  3. Four
  4. Zero
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — Four. The fields add to 2A and intensity is proportional to (2A)². A factor of two would incorrectly add the separate intensities rather than coherent amplitudes.

Question 5. A crystal lattice is best described as:

  1. The complete set of covalent bonds in one molecule
  2. A periodic array of equivalent points
  3. A surface contour of the density map
  4. A collection of randomly placed atoms
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — A periodic array of equivalent points. The lattice defines translations and repeat positions. The motif associated with each point specifies the contents; neither the lattice nor a contour alone defines a complete molecule.

Question 6. Which statement about the asymmetric unit is correct?

  1. It always contains exactly one chain
  2. It always equals the biological assembly
  3. Space-group operations generate the crystal from its independent contents
  4. It is the reciprocal lattice
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — Space-group operations generate the crystal from its independent contents. The asymmetric unit contains the independent structural contents needed to generate the crystal with space-group operations. Biological assembly and number of chains are separate matters.

Question 7. In Bragg’s law, θ denotes:

  1. The full angle between incident and outgoing beam directions
  2. The angle between the incident ray and the relevant lattice planes
  3. The unit-cell angle between a and b
  4. The angular velocity of the crystal
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — The angle between the incident ray and the relevant lattice planes. The full scattering angle is 2θ in the Bragg geometry. Cell angles and rotation speeds are distinct quantities.

Question 8. Which expression is Bragg’s law?

  1. nλ = d sin θ
  2. nλ = 2d sin θ
  3. λ = 2d cos θ/n²
  4. d = nλ sin θ
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — nλ = 2d sin θ. The extra path consists of two contributions of d sin θ, so constructive interference requires 2d sin θ = nλ. The other expressions do not follow from the path geometry.

Question 9. At constant λ, increasing θ in first-order diffraction corresponds to:

  1. Larger d
  2. Unchanged d
  3. Smaller d
  4. Zero intensity in every reflection
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — Smaller d. d = λ/(2 sin θ) decreases as θ increases in its physical range. Intensity depends on the scattering contents and cannot be inferred from angle alone.

Question 10. Under the convention without 2π, the magnitude of the reciprocal vector g(hkl) equals:

  1. d(hkl)
  2. 1/d(hkl)
  3. d(hkl)²
  4. λd(hkl)
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — 1/d(hkl). The reciprocal vector measures spatial frequency normal to the planes. Its magnitude has inverse-length units, ruling out the other expressions.

Question 11. A cubic cell has edge a. Which is the plane spacing d(200)?

  1. 2a
  2. a
  3. a/2
  4. a/4
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — a/2. For cubic geometry d = a/√(h²+k²+l²). For (200), the denominator is 2. The index is not a molecular distance multiplier.

Question 12. An ideal structure factor contains:

  1. Only a real intensity
  2. An amplitude and a phase
  3. Only the cell dimensions
  4. Only an atom’s mass
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — An amplitude and a phase. F is generally complex and is conveniently represented as |F| exp(iφ). Intensities constrain |F|², not the full complex coefficient.

Question 13. On a common corrected scale, intensity is proportional to:

  1. |F|
  2. |F|²
  3. 1/|F|
  4. The phase angle alone
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — |F|². The square magnitude of the resultant amplitude determines intensity. Phase affects the combination of atomic contributions but the intensity is not a direct readout of the resulting phase.

Question 14. Why is the conventional crystallographic phase problem important?

  1. The detector measures phases but no amplitudes
  2. The inverse Fourier reconstruction requires phase information unavailable directly from ordinary intensities
  3. Crystals have no periodic order
  4. The wavelength cannot be measured
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — The inverse Fourier reconstruction requires phase information unavailable directly from ordinary intensities. Both magnitudes and relative phases are needed for spatial reconstruction. The other options reverse the missing quantity or describe unrelated problems.

Question 15. Which method estimates phases by placing a known related structure in the new crystal cell?

  1. Molecular replacement
  2. SDS-PAGE
  3. Circular dichroism
  4. Density-gradient centrifugation
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Molecular replacement. Molecular replacement uses a structural search model to estimate orientation, translation and initial phases. The other techniques do not perform crystallographic phasing.

Question 16. What is the principal role of anomalous diffraction in this chapter?

  1. To guarantee a smaller crystal
  2. To extract extra phasing constraints from wavelength-dependent scattering differences
  3. To replace all atomic restraints
  4. To measure protein molecular weight directly
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — To extract extra phasing constraints from wavelength-dependent scattering differences. Anomalous contributions can break ordinary Friedel intensity equality, providing phasing information. They do not by themselves guarantee improved resolution or remove the need for model validation.

Question 17. Which dataset has the finer nominal resolution?

  1. 4.0 Å
  2. 3.2 Å
  3. 2.8 Å
  4. 1.4 Å
Answer and explanation

Correct: D — 1.4 Å. The minimum included spacing is smaller for finer nominal resolution. This does not prove uniform local accuracy, but 1.4 Å is the finest stated cutoff.

Question 18. A large isotropic B factor, other factors equal, preferentially attenuates:

  1. High-resolution amplitude contributions
  2. Only the direct beam
  3. All contributions by exactly the same factor
  4. The cell volume
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — High-resolution amplitude contributions. The multiplier exp[−B/(4d²)] decreases most strongly at small d. B does not itself change cell dimensions and its effect is not angle-independent.

Question 19. What is the usual distinction between Rwork and Rfree?

  1. One is an angle and the other a length
  2. Rwork assesses fitted reflections; Rfree assesses withheld reflections
  3. Rfree is calculated only from bond lengths
  4. Rwork is calculated from sequence identity
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — Rwork assesses fitted reflections; Rfree assesses withheld reflections. Both compare observed and calculated amplitudes on distinct sets. A withheld test set can detect overinterpretation that a fitted residual alone misses.

Question 20. Which measurement is especially relevant when judging a particular residue’s fit to its local density?

  1. Cell volume alone
  2. Number of authors
  3. Real-space correlation
  4. Protein extinction coefficient
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — Real-space correlation. Real-space correlation assesses local agreement between map and model. Cell volume and extinction coefficient may describe other properties, but do not test the residue’s local fit.

Question 21. Which statement about a missing loop in a coordinate model is most defensible?

  1. The protein sequence must lack it
  2. It is necessarily cleaved
  3. The available density may not support a unique position
  4. Every atom in a crystal is perfectly fixed
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — The available density may not support a unique position. Disorder and limited information can prevent coordinate assignment despite chemical presence. Independent sequence and sample evidence are required to infer deletion or cleavage.

Question 22. Which experiment most directly tests whether a structural mutation alters catalytic activity?

  1. A controlled kinetic measurement
  2. An inspection of the unit-cell angle alone
  3. A change in the map contour color
  4. A count of detector pixels
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — A controlled kinetic measurement. Kinetics measures catalytic behaviour. Structural evidence can suggest a mechanism, but the other listed observations do not measure catalytic rate.

15. Part C — analytical practice

Original practice questions; these are not previous-year examination questions.

Question 23. An X-ray wavelength is 1.50 Å and the detector reports 2θ = 60°. What is the first-order spacing?

  1. 0.866 Å
  2. 1.50 Å
  3. 3.00 Å
  4. 0.75 Å
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — 1.50 Å. θ = 30°, sin θ = 0.5. Therefore d = 1.50/(2×0.5) = 1.50 Å. Using 60° directly produces a different and incorrect value; doubling or halving again confuses the full angle.

Question 24. At λ = 1.20 Å, which first-order spacing is geometrically impossible?

  1. 3.0 Å
  2. 1.2 Å
  3. 0.60 Å
  4. 0.50 Å
Answer and explanation

Correct: D — 0.50 Å. For 0.50 Å, sin θ = 1.20/1.00 = 1.2, impossible. The 0.60 Å case is at the mathematical θ = 90° limit; practical detector accessibility is a separate matter.

Question 25. A cubic cell has a = 60 Å. Which reflection has d = 20 Å?

  1. (100)
  2. (200)
  3. (300)
  4. (600)
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — (300). d = a/√(h²+k²+l²); for (300), d = 60/3 = 20 Å. The others give 60, 30 and 10 Å.

Question 26. For an orthorhombic cell a = 40 Å, b = 80 Å, c = 100 Å, what is d(240)?

  1. 10.0 Å
  2. 14.14 Å
  3. 20.0 Å
  4. 40.0 Å
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — 14.14 Å. 1/d² = (2/40)²+(4/80)² = 0.005 Å⁻². Thus d = 1/√0.005 ≈ 14.14 Å. The component spacings cannot simply be averaged.

Question 27. At the same wavelength, crystal B has every cell length 10% larger than crystal A. For the same indexed reflection, what happens to the spacing and angle?

  1. d increases 10%, θ decreases
  2. d decreases 10%, θ increases
  3. Both increase 10%
  4. Both stay constant
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — d increases 10%, θ decreases. Uniform scaling increases direct spacing by 1.1 and decreases reciprocal magnitude by 1/1.1. Bragg’s law then gives smaller sin θ. θ itself does not scale linearly in general.

Question 28. Two corrected intensities are 1600 and 400. What is their amplitude ratio?

  1. 4
  2. 2
  3. 16
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — 2. The ratio is √(1600/400) = 2. Four is the intensity ratio, not the amplitude ratio; sixteen would square the ratio again.

Question 29. Two coherent amplitudes are 3 and 4 with relative phase π. What is the intensity on a scale where I = |F|²?

  1. 49
  2. 25
  3. 7
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

Correct: D — 1. F = 3−4 = −1, so I = 1. Forty-nine corresponds to equal phase, 25 to perpendicular phases, and 7 is not an intensity in the stated model.

Question 30. A two-atom motif has equal scattering factors f at x = 0 and x = 1/2. Which statement follows for h00?

  1. Odd h vanish; even h have I ∝ 4f²
  2. Even h vanish; odd h have I ∝ 4f²
  3. All h have equal intensity
  4. All h vanish
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Odd h vanish; even h have I ∝ 4f². F = f[1+exp(πih)]. Odd h give 1−1=0; even h give 1+1=2. The square of the latter gives 4f².

Question 31. If the second atom in the preceding ideal motif has scattering factor 0.7f rather than f, what is Iodd relative to f²?

  1. 0
  2. 0.09
  3. 0.3
  4. 2.89
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — 0.09. For odd h, the amplitude is f−0.7f=0.3f; the intensity is 0.09f². Perfect cancellation requires equal contributions; 2.89 is the even-h intensity.

Question 32. Two atoms of equal f occupy x = 0 and x = 1/4 in a one-dimensional model. Which indexed component vanishes?

  1. h = 1
  2. h = 2
  3. h = 3
  4. h = 4
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — h = 2. At h = 2 the relative phase is π, so contributions cancel. h=1 and 3 yield quadrature contributions; h=4 reinforces.

Question 33. Two atoms contribute 4 and 3i to F. What is its magnitude and phase?

  1. 7, 0°
  2. 5, about 36.9°
  3. 25, about 53.1°
  4. 1, 90°
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — 5, about 36.9°. The magnitude is √(4²+3²)=5 and the phase is atan(3/4)≈36.9°. Twenty-five is intensity and 7 would require contributions in phase.

Question 34. Two candidate models differ only by translation of the entire periodic motif. Ignoring other experimental information, ordinary intensities:

  1. Must change at every hkl
  2. Remain unchanged while phases may change
  3. Become negative
  4. Reveal the exact absolute origin
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — Remain unchanged while phases may change. A translation multiplies each structure factor by a phase factor of unit magnitude. The squared magnitude is unchanged; intensities cannot fix an arbitrary origin.

Question 35. At d = 2 Å and equal occupancy, increasing an atomic B factor from 10 to 30 Å² changes its amplitude by what multiplicative factor?

  1. e⁻¹·²⁵ ≈ 0.287
  2. e⁺¹·²⁵ ≈ 3.49
  3. 0.5 exactly
  4. 1 exactly
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — e⁻¹·²⁵ ≈ 0.287. The ratio is exp[−20/(4×2²)] = exp(−1.25). This is an individual atomic amplitude multiplier, not the general ratio of whole-reflection intensities.

Question 36. Two independent half-datasets have strong agreement for inner shells but very weak correlation in the outer shell. What is the best conclusion?

  1. The protein is chemically absent
  2. Outer-shell reproducibility is weak; inspect its contribution before choosing a cutoff
  3. The Rfree value must be zero
  4. The phases have been directly measured
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — Outer-shell reproducibility is weak; inspect its contribution before choosing a cutoff. Half-set correlation assesses reproducibility of intensity variation, not protein identity, model residual or phase acquisition. Shell-dependent data quality matters.

Question 37. A dataset is measured ten times in a limited orientation range and has low completeness. What should the investigator prioritize if feasible?

  1. Additional sampling of missing reciprocal regions
  2. Only more repeats in exactly the same region
  3. Deletion of every weak intensity
  4. Assigning all missing phases to zero
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Additional sampling of missing reciprocal regions. Repeated observations improve redundancy in the sampled region, not coverage of missing unique reflections. Additional orientations address completeness; arbitrary deletion or zero phasing can bias the reconstruction.

Question 38. Native and derivative crystals have substantial changes in unit-cell parameters. Which phasing interpretation requires particular caution?

  1. Isomorphous replacement
  2. The algebra of Bragg’s law
  3. The definition of an ångström
  4. The formula for a cubic d spacing
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Isomorphous replacement. Isomorphous replacement assumes that the underlying native arrangement is essentially preserved when added scatterers contribute. Cell changes raise concern about that assumption, although geometry and units remain valid.

Question 39. A molecular-replacement search model lacks a domain known from the new protein’s sequence. After placement of the conserved core, density appears elsewhere. What should be done?

  1. Copy the absent domain coordinates arbitrarily
  2. Inspect the new density, build the domain only where supported and validate
  3. Delete all extra sequence from the sample record
  4. Declare the initial model a direct detector image
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — Inspect the new density, build the domain only where supported and validate. The conserved core may provide initial phases, but the new model should explain the new experimental data. Arbitrary copying or deletion introduces unsupported claims and confuses a calculated model with measurements.

Question 40. An illustrative model has observed amplitudes 80, 70, 50 and calculated amplitudes 70, 80, 40. What is R?

  1. 0.05
  2. 0.10
  3. 0.15
  4. 0.30
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — 0.15. The absolute discrepancies are 10+10+10=30, and the observed total is 200. R=30/200=0.15. Signed errors would cancel incorrectly.

Question 41. Refinement changes Rwork/Rfree from 0.22/0.26 to 0.15/0.33 on identical sets. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. It guarantees a better model
  2. Work agreement improves but held-out prediction worsens; investigate overfitting or model error
  3. Rfree should be recalculated on the work set
  4. The nominal resolution has automatically doubled
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — Work agreement improves but held-out prediction worsens; investigate overfitting or model error. The gap grows from 0.04 to 0.18 and independent prediction worsens. A low working residual alone is not proof of improvement, and changing sets compromises the test.

Question 42. A 1.5 Å structure has excellent core density but weak loop density. A 2.2 Å structure shows a clearer trace for the corresponding loop. Which claim is justified?

  1. Every coordinate in the 1.5 Å model is necessarily better
  2. Local loop evidence may be stronger in the 2.2 Å model
  3. The 2.2 Å dataset has finer nominal resolution
  4. The loop must be absent in the first sample
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — Local loop evidence may be stronger in the 2.2 Å model. Global resolution and local evidence are distinct. A well-ordered loop can be more interpretable in a lower nominal-resolution structure; weak density alone does not prove absence.

Question 43. A difference map shows negative density around a modelled atom and positive density nearby. What is a plausible first interpretation?

  1. The atom may be displaced relative to the model
  2. The electron charge has become positive
  3. The crystal is necessarily amorphous
  4. All phases must be exact
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — The atom may be displaced relative to the model. A misplaced atom can generate an excess calculated contribution at its old location and unexplained positive density at the candidate location. Chemistry and local map support must be checked before moving it.

Question 44. An apparent protein dimer is formed by a lattice contact. Which independent observation best supports a biological dimer hypothesis?

  1. The two chains have different colors in a viewer
  2. Solution measurements support a dimer under relevant conditions
  3. The cell is orthorhombic
  4. The crystal has a small d cutoff
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — Solution measurements support a dimer under relevant conditions. Solution oligomeric-state evidence can support a biologically relevant assembly. Viewer colors, cell geometry and resolution do not determine whether a contact persists outside the crystal.

Question 45. A mutant shows unchanged overall fold and substrate affinity but a much lower catalytic rate. A structure indicates a local side-chain displacement near the active site. What inference is most appropriate?

  1. The mutation may impair catalytic chemistry or relevant dynamics
  2. The mutation necessarily abolishes all substrate binding
  3. The structure alone proves the exact reaction rate
  4. The protein must be completely unfolded
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — The mutation may impair catalytic chemistry or relevant dynamics. The combined data support a local functional perturbation rather than gross unfolding or initial binding failure. They do not uniquely specify the chemical mechanism, and independent kinetic controls remain important.

Question 46. A ligand has clear overall envelope density, but two orientations fit similarly and differ in a proposed hydrogen bond. What should be reported?

  1. One orientation is certain because the protein Rfree is low
  2. The ligand orientation is uncertain; use local map and independent chemical evidence
  3. Both orientations necessarily coexist at exactly 50% occupancy
  4. Hydrogen positions are always directly visible
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — The ligand orientation is uncertain; use local map and independent chemical evidence. Ambiguity should be preserved when data do not discriminate. A global residual cannot determine one local pose, and neither equal populations nor directly resolved hydrogen follows.

Question 47. A protein crystal yields 450 unique measured reflections out of 500 expected in a stated range. What is completeness?

  1. 10%
  2. 50%
  3. 90%
  4. 111%
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — 90%. Completeness=450/500×100=90%. Repeat measurements do not change the number of unique expected reflections; 10% is the missing fraction.

Question 48. A 2.0 Å map and a 3.0 Å map are calculated with equally accurate phases in a simplified example. Which difference is expected from the resolution limit alone?

  1. The 3.0 Å map includes more high-frequency information
  2. The 2.0 Å map can represent finer features
  3. The 3.0 Å map determines binding rates
  4. The maps must be identical
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — The 2.0 Å map can represent finer features. The 2.0 Å cutoff includes greater reciprocal spatial frequencies. Real maps also depend on order, noise and completeness; the stated assumptions isolate the cutoff effect.

16. Assertion–reason and SET-style practice

Original practice questions; these are not previous-year examination questions.

Question 49. Assertion–reason. Assertion: Equal crystal-cell dimensions do not guarantee equal reflection intensities. Reason: Intensity depends on the arrangement of scatterers within the cell.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Both true; reason explains assertion. Cell geometry predicts positions, whereas motif scattering determines amplitudes. Different internal arrangements can change their interference without changing indexed geometry.

Question 50. Assertion–reason. Assertion: A small nominal resolution value guarantees accurate placement of every residue. Reason: Local disorder can weaken interpretable density.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct: D — Assertion false; reason true. A small d cutoff indicates fine nominal information, not uniformly ordered residues. Local disorder is exactly why the assertion fails.

Question 51. Assertion–reason. Assertion: Rfree reflections should not be used to optimize the fitted model. Reason: Withholding them provides a check of predictive agreement.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Both true; reason explains assertion. The held-out set serves its purpose only when its information is not used in fitting or repeatedly exploited for tuning. It checks predictive performance rather than training agreement.

Question 52. SET-style numerical reasoning. A perpendicular detector is 100 mm from the crystal; a spot lies 100 mm from the beam centre. At λ = 1 Å, estimate first-order d.

  1. 0.71 Å
  2. 1.31 Å
  3. 2.00 Å
  4. 4.00 Å
Answer and explanation

Correct: B — 1.31 Å. tan(2θ)=1, so 2θ=45° and θ=22.5°. d=1/(2×0.38268)≈1.3066 Å. Using 45° as θ yields about 0.707 Å.

Question 53. Experimental interpretation. Early and late diffraction observations differ systematically and a sensitive side chain loses density over exposure. What alternative must be considered?

  1. Radiation-induced change
  2. A compulsory change in atomic number of every atom
  3. An increase in completeness by definition
  4. Perfectly stable sample chemistry
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — Radiation-induced change. Ionising radiation can alter sensitive groups and reduce order. Systematic dose-dependent differences motivate examination of damage; they do not prove a biological transition.

Question 54. Multiple statements. (i) Bragg’s law constrains reflection geometry. (ii) A Fourier map needs complex structure factors. (iii) Rfree is a direct measure of ligand affinity. Which are correct?

  1. i only
  2. ii only
  3. i and ii
  4. i, ii and iii
Answer and explanation

Correct: C — i and ii. Geometry and complex coefficients are correctly described. Rfree is a model-to-data residual and has no direct interpretation as ligand binding affinity.

Question 55. Experimental comparison. Two structures have different crystal forms and show a small loop displacement. Which additional observation would best strengthen the biological interpretation?

  1. The same loop change appears in another relevant structural or solution experiment
  2. A different choice of figure color
  3. A higher number of decimal places in coordinates
  4. Deleting the alternative structure
Answer and explanation

Correct: A — The same loop change appears in another relevant structural or solution experiment. Independent evidence under appropriate conditions helps distinguish packing and experimental uncertainty from a reproducible biological change. Formatting or coordinate precision cannot substitute for evidence.

17. Revision and mastery

Diffraction encodes the periodic electron distribution through coherent scattering. The geometry gives indexed positions and spacings; the intensities constrain amplitude magnitudes. Phase estimation permits an inverse Fourier calculation. An atomic model is built and refined against observations with stereochemical constraints. Independent validation, local maps and complementary experiments determine the defensibility of biological conclusions.
