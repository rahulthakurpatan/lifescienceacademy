1. From molecular function to structural evidence

An enzyme binds one substrate much more strongly than another, although both may possess the same chemical groups. A mutation can abolish catalytic activity without substantially changing the mass of the protein. The explanation often lies in the relative positions and orientations of atoms: a catalytic group must approach a susceptible bond, a pocket must accommodate a ligand, and an interface must bring complementary surfaces together. Biochemistry identifies the relevant molecules and measures their behaviour, but a three-dimensional structural measurement can reveal how the arrangement of those molecules permits the behaviour. X-ray crystallography is a major method for constructing such a structural description.

The word structure has several meanings in biology. Primary structure specifies the sequence of covalently linked building blocks. Secondary structure describes local regular conformations, tertiary structure describes the arrangement of a folded chain, and quaternary structure describes the arrangement of multiple chains. A crystallographic atomic model can provide evidence at all these spatial levels, although the sequence is usually known beforehand from independent measurements. It supplies coordinate estimates for atoms that are sufficiently ordered in the specimen. The chemical bonds drawn between coordinates are interpreted according to chemical knowledge rather than independently photographed by the detector. At a lower level of detail, the arrangement of helices and sheets can be evident even where many side chains are ambiguous.

The scale presents a physical challenge. One ångström (Å) is one tenth of a nanometre, or 10⁻¹⁰ metre. A covalent carbon–carbon bond is typically about one and a half ångströms long, whereas a visible photon has a wavelength of several thousand ångströms. Optical microscopy cannot normally distinguish neighbouring atoms. X-rays have wavelengths comparable to interatomic separations and can therefore encode variations in the electron distribution on the required length scale. A short wavelength alone, however, does not make a structural measurement successful: sufficient scattered intensity, a suitable sample and an appropriate reconstruction method are essential.

A common misconception is to imagine an X-ray crystallographic detector as a very powerful camera recording a magnified image of the molecule. Instead the detector records radiation travelling in different directions after interaction with the electrons in the crystal. The pattern is related mathematically to the periodic electron distribution, and a subsequent reconstruction is necessary. Thus we must separate the specimen, the measured diffraction intensities, the reconstructed density and the fitted atomic model. These are different forms of information and have different uncertainties.

Crystallography developed from the demonstration of X-ray diffraction by crystals in the early twentieth century. The interpretation of diffraction using families of planes provided a simple geometrical connection between wavelength, scattering angle and lattice spacing. Applying these physical principles to proteins was much harder than applying them to small inorganic crystals: proteins contain thousands of atoms, have weak high-angle reflections and require a way to infer the phases of scattered waves. The early myoglobin and haemoglobin studies by John Kendrew and Max Perutz established that protein molecules could be described in three dimensions using diffraction evidence. Their work received the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The achievement was not merely the measurement of a regular protein lattice; it was reconstruction of the nontrivial molecular arrangement within that lattice.

Consider myoglobin as a recurring example. The protein contains a haem group within a folded polypeptide. Spectroscopic evidence can establish that oxygen interacts with haem, but the positions of surrounding side chains help explain the environment in which binding occurs. Crystallography can locate the haem relative to the protein and show which groups are near it; spectroscopy and binding experiments are then needed to determine how the local environment influences affinity and oxygen chemistry. A structural hypothesis and a functional hypothesis are therefore complementary. The static coordinate model should not be confused with a measurement of a reaction rate or with proof that a particular conformation is the only one occupied in solution.

2. Scattering, interference and the diffraction pattern

The electromagnetic wave consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. In X-ray scattering by ordinary biological matter, the electric field interacts predominantly with electrons. A small portion of incident radiation is redirected; for elastic scattering the outgoing wave has the same frequency and wavelength as the incoming wave. This elastic component is the basis of conventional crystallographic diffraction. Other interactions, including inelastic scattering and absorption, contribute background or radiation damage and are not equivalent sources of Bragg intensity.

To understand diffraction, picture two small scattering centres illuminated by the same plane wave. Each contributes a wave toward a distant detector. At that detector their electric fields add. If the peaks of one wave arrive with the peaks of the other, amplitudes reinforce one another; if a peak meets a trough of equal magnitude, they cancel. Intermediate relationships give intermediate resultant amplitudes. The relevant distinction is between amplitude, which can carry a sign or phase, and intensity, which is proportional to the square of the magnitude of the combined amplitude. Adding the intensities of coherent contributions would discard their interference.

A phase difference is conveniently measured as an angle: a path difference of one full wavelength corresponds to 2π radians, and a half-wavelength to π radians. Two equal-amplitude waves of magnitude A produce a resultant magnitude 2A if in phase, giving four times the intensity of either alone. If exactly out of phase, they produce zero resultant in the ideal two-wave case. This observation is essential to understanding why some reflections are absent despite many electrons being present. A dark spot is not necessarily a direction where no atoms scatter; it can be a direction where their contributions cancel.

A crystal contains many regularly arranged copies of a structural motif. When a large number of equivalent repeat units scatter coherently in a permitted direction, their amplitudes reinforce strongly. In other directions the phase changes from cell to cell and much of the scattered amplitude cancels. A finite ordered crystal therefore yields narrow maxima rather than the broad distribution of an isolated molecule. Smaller crystals and lattice disorder can broaden or weaken maxima. The detailed intensities are determined by the motif within the repeat unit, not merely by the total number of unit cells.

Atomic scattering factors express the scattering amplitude of individual atoms relative to the contribution of an electron under specified conditions. At very small scattering angle an ordinary neutral atom has a factor approaching its electron count. At greater angle the contributions of electrons at different positions within its own electron cloud become less coherent, so the factor generally decreases. This helps explain why high-angle scattering is challenging and why hydrogen, with only one electron, is often poorly distinguished in conventional macromolecular X-ray maps. Atomic identification cannot be made simply from the height of a single density maximum: occupancy, motion, neighbouring atoms and resolution all modify what is observed.

Diffraction from crystals is distinct from spectroscopy, which may monitor absorption or emission as frequency varies to infer energy levels or local environments. In a crystallographic experiment the wavelength is generally selected and the spatial variation of scattered intensity is measured. Wavelength changes can deliberately supply additional information in anomalous diffraction, but the principal structural information still comes from interference. It is also distinct from radiographic imaging, which measures attenuation along paths through a specimen. The physical word X-ray applies to all these methods without making their measured quantities interchangeable.

3. Crystal lattices, unit cells and sample suitability

Crystalline order is described by a lattice, an abstract array of equivalent points related by translations. A motif associated with each lattice point gives the crystal its structure. In a macromolecular crystal the motif includes proteins or nucleic acids together with ordered solvent, ions and other bound components. The unit cell is a parallelepiped that reproduces the ideal lattice by translations. Its dimensions are a, b and c, with interaxial angles α between b and c, β between a and c, and γ between a and b. These parameters specify the geometry of the repeat, not the complete molecular coordinates.

The asymmetric unit is the smallest independently specified part of the crystal structure from which the rest can be generated by the space-group operations. It need not contain only one molecule, and its contents need not coincide with the functional assembly. For example, a biological dimer can be generated from one independently modelled subunit and a crystallographic symmetry operation. Alternatively, two chains within the asymmetric unit can be unrelated by exact crystallographic symmetry. A lattice contact between chains is not sufficient to prove that those chains interact in living cells.

The regularity of the crystal does not imply that all atoms are rigid. Protein crystals commonly contain aqueous channels between molecules. A chain may be well ordered in the folded core but adopt several positions in a solvent-exposed loop. The repetition of the average arrangement is sufficient for Bragg diffraction, while deviations from the average may contribute diffuse scattering or reduce the sharp reflection intensities. Different proteins, and different crystallization conditions for one protein, can yield crystals with different unit cells or space groups. This variation is often called crystal polymorphism and can influence the molecular environment in the lattice.

Crystal growth requires molecules to assemble into an ordered array rather than remain dissolved or aggregate without order. The thermodynamic idea of supersaturation is useful: the solution contains more solute than is stable at equilibrium with the crystal under those conditions. Excessive supersaturation can favour precipitation or excessive nucleation, while moderate conditions can permit ordered growth. Crystallographers examine the state of the sample, including molecular homogeneity, folding and aggregation, and explore conditions that favour regular assembly. A crystal that appears large and transparent may still be internally disordered, whereas a visually small crystal can diffract usefully. The decisive observation is the diffraction pattern.

Macromolecular crystals may be protected during collection, for example by cooling under suitable conditions, which can reduce some effects of radiation damage and motion. Cooling is not a guarantee of native-state structure; the crystallization environment and cooling process can shift populations of conformations. Ionising X-ray exposure can induce chemical changes, including changes at sensitive groups. Comparing early and late measurements can reveal dose-dependent alterations. Structural interpretations should distinguish features associated with biological conditions from those potentially caused by preparation or radiation.

Crystal packing also raises an important biological control. Suppose a flexible loop closes over a substrate pocket in a crystal containing no substrate. It is possible that a neighbouring symmetry-related molecule physically stabilizes the closed loop. Examining neighbouring molecules, comparing different crystal forms and examining the protein in solution help distinguish an intrinsic conformational preference from a packing effect. Absence of a large difference between structures is not evidence that no dynamic transitions occur: Bragg measurements report a periodic spatial average, not a time series of individual transitions.

4. Bragg’s law: geometry and calculations

The scattering pattern from a crystal can be understood by considering sets of equally spaced parallel lattice planes. These are mathematical planes, not literal solid mirrors between molecules. The spacing d is the perpendicular separation between adjacent planes. An incident ray makes an angle θ with the plane; the ray directed toward the corresponding diffracted beam also makes θ with the plane in the simple geometrical construction. The angle between incident and outgoing beam directions is therefore 2θ.

Imagine two parallel incoming rays, one scattered from a point on the upper plane and another scattered from a corresponding point on the plane immediately below. The second ray travels an additional distance on the inward part and on the outward part of its journey. Projection of the interplanar distance onto each ray direction gives d sin θ. The total extra path is 2d sin θ. A constructive maximum occurs when this difference is an integer multiple of the wavelength. Hence Bragg’s law is nλ = 2d sin θ, where n is a positive integer, λ is the wavelength and d has the same length units as λ. In indexed diffraction patterns one usually absorbs the order into the Miller indices and uses the first-order form for each indexed reflection.

The restriction that sin θ cannot exceed one limits the possible combination of wavelength, spacing and order. At first order with λ = 1.50 Å and d = 3.00 Å, sin θ is 1.50 divided by 6.00, or 0.250. The inverse sine gives θ ≈ 14.48°, and a detector reporting scattering angle would record 2θ ≈ 28.96°. Reporting the half angle as the detector angle is a frequent factor-of-two error, not a small rounding difference. Conversely, if a problem gives 2θ = 60° at λ = 1.20 Å, θ is 30° and d equals 1.20 divided by twice 0.5, or 1.20 Å.

At fixed wavelength, larger θ corresponds to smaller d. High-angle reflections therefore carry information about short length-scale variations in the electron distribution. This does not mean that each particular high-angle reflection measures one covalent bond. Rather each reflection is a Fourier component determined by the arrangement of all scatterers in the unit cell. Many components are combined to obtain a density map. The finest available spacing establishes a nominal high-resolution cutoff, subject to data quality.

Changing wavelength alters the angles at which a specified plane spacing meets the diffraction condition. If the spacing remains unchanged while λ decreases, the necessary sine decreases. For a fixed detector angular limit, decreasing wavelength can in principle extend accessible reciprocal-space radius, but it does not automatically provide useful intensities at those radii. Crystal disorder, scattering strength, detector geometry and source characteristics still matter.

Bragg’s law predicts the geometry of reinforcement by equivalent planes. It does not predict the intensity without additional information on the contents of the unit cell. Consider two crystals with equal lattice parameters but different positions of atoms inside their cells. They may yield reflections in the same geometrical positions, yet some reflections can be much stronger in one than the other. This separation between geometry and intensity underlies the transition from Bragg’s equation to the structure-factor description.

5. Miller indices and reciprocal space

A set of crystal planes is indexed by three integers h, k and l. A plane that intersects the direct-space axes at a/h, b/k and c/l is associated with indices (hkl), with a zero index indicating parallelism to the corresponding axis. Negative indices describe intercepts in the opposite direction. For example, (100) planes are parallel to the b and c directions, and (200) corresponds to half the spacing of (100). These indices identify a direction and a spacing; they are not the coordinates of one atom.

In the common crystallographic convention without a factor of 2π, the reciprocal basis vectors a*, b* and c* satisfy a*·a = 1, a*·b = a*·c = 0, and corresponding relations for b* and c*. Their units are inverse length. The vector g = h a* + k b* + l c* is perpendicular to the indexed planes and has magnitude 1/d(hkl). A lattice constructed from these vectors is the reciprocal lattice. This is a mathematical representation of spatial frequencies of periodic structures, not a second physical lattice inside the crystal.

For an orthorhombic cell, where all three angles are right angles, the reciprocal vectors have magnitudes 1/a, 1/b and 1/c and are mutually perpendicular. The magnitude squared of g is therefore h²/a² + k²/b² + l²/c², and d equals the reciprocal of the square root of this expression. In a cubic cell with edge a, this reduces to d = a/√(h² + k² + l²). For a cubic cell of edge 60 Å, the (300) spacing is 20 Å and the (330) spacing is 60/√18 ≈ 14.14 Å. Both are properties of a hypothetical lattice rather than distances between particular residues. For nonorthogonal cells the reciprocal metric includes angular terms, so the orthorhombic formula cannot be applied blindly.

The Ewald construction provides a geometric way to determine which reciprocal points meet the diffraction condition for a given crystal orientation and wavelength. Use vectors k_in and k_out each of magnitude 1/λ. The scattering vector k_out − k_in has magnitude 2 sin θ/λ and equals a reciprocal lattice vector for a Bragg reflection. This directly gives 1/d = 2 sin θ/λ and connects the reciprocal representation with Bragg’s law. A sphere of radius 1/λ can be drawn so that accessible reciprocal points lie on its surface. Rotating the specimen moves reciprocal points through diffraction conditions, allowing three-dimensional information to be sampled by successive detector images.

This also explains why recording only a single still image is generally inadequate for conventional single-crystal structure determination. A two-dimensional detector captures a subset of the three-dimensional reciprocal lattice for a given orientation. Rotation supplies additional observations, and symmetry can relate some observations to each other. Completeness is the proportion of expected unique reflections measured within a chosen resolution range. A high-quality measurement needs adequate completeness as well as a useful range of spacings. Missing data concentrated along one direction can cause directional uncertainty in the density map even if the nominal resolution number looks favourable.

6. Structure factors and what the detector measures

To derive a useful expression for intensity, express the position of atom j in fractional coordinates (x_j, y_j, z_j) relative to the unit cell. The phase associated with its location in reflection (hkl) is 2π(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j) under one consistent Fourier sign convention. If f_j is the atomic scattering factor, the structure factor in an ideal static model is F(hkl) = Σ_j f_j exp[2πi(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j)]. The complex exponential compactly represents a vector of magnitude f_j pointing at the specified phase angle in the complex plane. Its real and imaginary components are cosine and sine contributions. Summing the vectors yields the resultant amplitude and phase.

After appropriate corrections and scaling, the observed intensity of a reflection is proportional to |F(hkl)|². The squared magnitude is the sum of the squares of the real and imaginary parts, not the sum of the individual atomic intensities. If two identical atoms have fractional x coordinates 0 and 1/2, and only reflections with k = l = 0 are considered, F(h00) = f[1 + exp(πih)]. For even h the terms add to 2f; for odd h they cancel exactly. The odd reflection has zero intensity in this ideal model. If one atom differs in scattering factor or occupancy, the cancellation need not be complete. In a real crystal, systematic absences arising from symmetry are valuable for distinguishing candidate space groups.

The atoms are not necessarily present at full occupancy in precisely one location. An occupancy factor weights the fraction of the crystal ensemble represented by a given atomic position. An isotropic displacement factor B is commonly incorporated as a multiplier exp[−B(sin θ/λ)²] on the atomic amplitude. Increasing B attenuates high-resolution contributions more than low-resolution contributions. B is conventionally expressed in Å²; for an isotropic Gaussian displacement model it relates to the mean-square displacement along a Cartesian direction U by B = 8π²U. Disorder, modelling errors and experimental factors can affect a refined B, so it should not automatically be interpreted as a direct measurement of molecular flexibility.

Practical data collection begins with a selected beam illuminating a mounted specimen. A detector records distributed intensity, and processing identifies spots, indexes their lattice coordinates, integrates intensity over each spot and subtracts estimated background. Scaling accounts for variation in exposure and other factors before equivalent observations are merged where appropriate. Because measured intensities have noise, an individual background-subtracted estimate can be negative; that does not imply a negative physical intensity or require that every such observation be discarded. Statistical treatments take the uncertainty into account when estimating amplitudes.

Repeated measurements provide redundancy and allow assessment of consistency. Weak reflections should be judged with their uncertainty, including how agreement varies across resolution shells. Completeness describes how much reciprocal-space information is present, whereas signal-to-noise measures describe measurement strength relative to uncertainty. These metrics answer different questions. A dataset with abundant repeated observations of a narrow region is not necessarily complete. Conversely, a complete but very weak high-angle shell does not guarantee that detailed features in a map will be reliable.

7. The phase problem and reconstruction of electron density

Each structure factor can be written as |F| exp(iφ), where φ is its phase. An ordinary diffraction detector yields information on the intensity and thus on amplitude, but not a direct measurement of φ for each reflection. The loss of the phases is the central phase problem of crystallography. It matters because the spatial location of the density depends on the relative phases of its Fourier components. Even an ideal set of exact amplitudes does not by itself provide a unique straightforward inverse transform.

For a consistent sign convention, the electron density at fractional position (x,y,z) can be expressed as ρ(x,y,z) = (1/V) Σ_hkl F(hkl) exp[−2πi(hx + ky + lz)], where V is the unit cell volume. With F expressed in electron units and V in Å³, the density has units of electrons per Å³. The observed structure factors are available only to a finite resolution and may lack some reflections. The calculated map is therefore an approximation to the periodic average density rather than a perfect, continuous description of every electron.

An elementary illustration uses one cosine density component, ρ(x) = ρ₀ + A cos(2πx + φ). Increasing φ by π moves the maximum to a location previously occupied by the minimum, but the component amplitude is unchanged. This is why an amplitude alone is insufficient to identify where a feature lies. Translating the entire contents of the unit cell produces phase changes but leaves diffraction intensities unchanged; an arbitrary choice of origin can thus produce equivalent descriptions. Other ambiguities, such as inversion under ordinary nonanomalous scattering, require appropriate chemical or experimental information.

Molecular replacement estimates initial phases by placing a previously determined related molecular structure within the new crystal cell. Rotation and translation are searched so that the model predicts diffraction observations consistent with the new dataset. The method is effective when the search model resembles the actual structure sufficiently. A poor search model can fail, while a plausible solution can still carry bias toward the template. A flexible domain may not occupy the position predicted by the search model, so the initial result must be rebuilt and tested against new data.

Isomorphous replacement compares data from a native crystal with a crystal in which a small number of strongly scattering atoms contribute additional scattering without materially altering the original lattice and molecular arrangement. The differences help locate the added scatterers. Their calculable complex contribution constrains the unknown native structure factors geometrically. A single derivative can leave phase ambiguity; multiple independent sources of information can resolve it. Failure of the isomorphism assumption complicates the interpretation because differences then reflect structural changes as well as the added scattering contribution.

Anomalous diffraction takes advantage of wavelength-dependent scattering near an absorption edge of an element. Under ordinary real scattering factors, Friedel-related reflections hkl and −h−k−l have equal intensities. An anomalous component introduces a complex contribution, and the two intensities may differ when the crystal and scatterers permit it. Such differences can help infer phases. Single-wavelength anomalous diffraction and multiwavelength anomalous diffraction are standard conceptual approaches; the distinction is whether the information is collected at one or more selected wavelengths. These methods require sufficient signal and careful data processing. They are not simply higher-resolution versions of conventional measurement.

The historical significance of phase estimation is particularly clear in the protein structure work of Perutz and Kendrew. Regular arrays of proteins gave diffraction patterns long before the full molecular arrangements could be read from them. Exploiting additional scattering contributions and calculating Fourier syntheses turned those patterns into interpretable structural evidence. The key examination distinction is that Bragg geometry tells where reflections can occur, intensity estimates the magnitude of a structure factor, and phasing supplies otherwise missing information needed for the density reconstruction.

8. Reading maps, resolution and local uncertainty

An electron-density map is often displayed by drawing contours at a selected density level. In three dimensions these contours become surfaces enclosing regions above the chosen threshold. A map contour is not the molecular surface in the biochemical sense. Its apparent shape changes with the threshold and with the map calculation. An atomic model displayed as sticks is overlaid to assess whether the sequence and chemically possible coordinates explain the density. At good resolution a well-ordered side chain can show distinct connected features; at poorer resolution the same side chain may be represented only by a broad envelope or may not be interpretable at all.

Nominal resolution in conventional crystallography is often stated as the smallest interplanar spacing d included in the measured or refined dataset. A structure at 1.5 Å is conventionally called higher resolution than one at 3.0 Å. This scale runs in the opposite direction from many ordinary quality scores. Smaller d corresponds to higher reciprocal spatial frequency. Truncating high-frequency information smooths a density map, just as omitting rapidly varying terms from a Fourier series smooths the represented function. But the analogy has limits: actual maps are affected by imperfect phases, missing reflections, atomic motion and noise in addition to the resolution cutoff.

Resolution is not the same as coordinate error. A well-constrained coordinate may be estimated with uncertainty considerably smaller than the nominal spacing, whereas a disordered loop may be poorly determined even in a globally high-resolution structure. The nominal limit applies to the dataset, and local interpretability varies throughout the model. For an experimental question about a single catalytic oxygen, the density surrounding that oxygen is therefore more relevant than the resolution value alone.

Common displayed maps include weighted combinations of observed amplitudes and calculated structure factors with estimated model phases. A 2mFo−DFc map is often used to inspect overall agreement between coordinates and density. An mFo−DFc difference map can reveal features that remain unexplained by the model. Positive difference density can indicate a missing atom or ligand, or an atom whose position is wrong. Negative difference density can indicate that the model places too much scattering matter at a location. A positive peak near a binding site is a reason to investigate, not by itself proof of a particular chemical identity.

Suppose a model assigns a water molecule to a peak adjacent to a negatively charged side chain, but the difference density remains strongly positive and the surrounding geometry suggests several oxygen ligands. A metal ion is one alternative explanation; occupancy, displacement factors, experimental conditions and anomalous information may help distinguish the hypotheses. A model assigning a metal without chemical or experimental support may fit the peak while giving an implausible coordination environment. Inferences about ions require integration of map fit, chemistry and independent evidence.

Consider an illustrative two-conformation loop with roughly equal populations. A map may show two weaker traces, or a broad ambiguous feature if the conformations are close. If the model contains only the first conformation at full occupancy, residual density may appear near the second. Adding alternate conformations is justified when both positions are supported and occupancies are physically meaningful. It is not justified simply to reduce a residual by adding enough arbitrary atoms. A missing model segment can be a scientifically appropriate expression of insufficient evidence rather than a failure to know the protein sequence.

9. Model building, refinement and independent validation

After initial phase estimation, the density is inspected and a molecular model constructed. The known sequence provides a strong constraint on which residues can occur and in what order. Stereochemical knowledge provides expected ranges for bond lengths, angles and conformational preferences. The model is then used to calculate predicted structure factors. Differences between observed and predicted amplitudes and discrepancies in the map guide changes to atomic coordinates, displacement factors, occupancies and other model parameters. Iteration improves the model and can improve phase estimates, but introduces the possibility of bias because model and map are no longer independent.

Refinement is an optimization under incomplete information. An objective function contains a measure of agreement with diffraction observations together with restraints that favour plausible molecular geometry. Restraints are particularly important when the number of adjustable parameters is large relative to the number of informative observations. Without them, a model might distort bond lengths and angles to follow random errors in the dataset. With excessively strong restraints, genuine departures from ideal geometry might be suppressed. The goal is a model supported by data and chemistry, not merely a minimum value of one residual.

An amplitude residual is conventionally defined as R = Σ ||Fo| − |Fc|| / Σ |Fo| over a specified reflection set. The denominator normalizes the absolute amplitude discrepancies by the total observed amplitude. For illustrative observed amplitudes 100, 60 and 40, and calculated amplitudes 90, 70 and 35 on a common scale, the numerator is 10 + 10 + 5 = 25 and the denominator is 200. R = 0.125 or 12.5%. These invented three-reflection values are for calculation only and are not a realistic crystallographic quality assessment. The residual is not calculated from the squared intensities in this definition.

Rwork describes the agreement on reflections used in refinement. Rfree is calculated using a designated set of observations held out from fitting. The test set is chosen in advance and should not be repeatedly selected or used to tune the model, because its purpose is to provide an independent check of predictive agreement. Rfree often exceeds Rwork. A large gap may suggest overfitting, but data quality, the size and independence of the test set, and model problems also influence the interpretation. Neither a small gap nor a low work residual alone establishes biological correctness.

Validation has complementary levels. Global measures include resolution, completeness and overall residuals. Geometry measures include deviations of bond lengths and angles, clashes between atoms, side-chain rotamer outliers and the distribution of protein backbone dihedral angles on the Ramachandran plot. Local agreement can be evaluated with real-space correlation coefficients or real-space residuals comparing model-derived and observed map information near a residue. A model may have convincing global scores while a ligand, catalytic group or loop remains unsupported. Conversely an unusual backbone conformation may be genuine if independently supported by strong density and surrounding interactions.

A practical reasoning pattern is to compare alternative hypotheses with the same data and validation rules. An investigator testing a ligand pose should inspect local density and difference features, consider ligand stereochemistry, compare plausible alternative orientations and report uncertainty when they cannot be distinguished. An omit-map approach can reduce direct dependence on the omitted feature, although it does not make all model bias disappear. A ligand-binding assay or a carefully controlled mutation study can assess functional consequences, complementing the structural evidence.

Protein Data Bank entries commonly provide model coordinates together with crystallographic experimental information and a validation report. The reader can distinguish the deposited model from the underlying diffraction observations and ask how well the model fits. The report is not an automatic certificate that every biological claim derived from the structure is true. The distinction between an asymmetric unit and a biological assembly, the possibility of crystal-packing effects, and the local evidence at sites of interest remain important even when formal validation is satisfactory.

10. Connecting diffraction evidence to biological mechanisms

Structural determination is valuable because a sequence of atoms can suggest testable functional explanations. In a binding protein, comparison of the empty and occupied sites can identify groups that are close enough for favourable interactions. A structure alone does not give the free energy of those interactions, because binding also depends on desolvation, entropy and changes throughout the system. The presence of a short separation in a model should therefore be treated as evidence of possible interaction rather than a numerical contribution to binding affinity.

A comparison of two structures requires consistent definitions. To ask whether a protein changes shape upon ligand binding, align comparable regions of the models and inspect differences relative to the uncertainty of the coordinates. Apparent shifts may arise from a different crystal form or solvent environment. Independent comparisons of ligand-bound and unbound specimens, preferably with similar resolution and conditions, improve the inference. If the difference is smaller than plausible local uncertainty, it is inappropriate to draw a detailed mechanistic conclusion from it.

Myoglobin and haemoglobin provide a classical example of how a common structural motif can occur in molecules with different biological roles. The presence of a haem pocket in both supports common aspects of oxygen binding chemistry. Haemoglobin has multiple subunits that can rearrange relative to one another, making its structure relevant to cooperative oxygen binding. The relationship between structural conformations and measured oxygen-binding curves supports allosteric models. The diffraction experiment alone does not measure the Hill coefficient or determine the full thermodynamic distribution of states. This requires quantitative functional experiments.

In an illustrative enzyme study, a mutant has the same overall fold as the wild-type model but a different side-chain orientation near the substrate site. Its catalytic rate is reduced while its substrate-binding affinity is almost unchanged. This combination supports the hypothesis that the mutated group influences the chemical step rather than gross folding or initial association. Other explanations remain possible, including changed conformational dynamics. A rigorous interpretation integrates the structure with kinetic measurements, folding controls and alternative substitutions rather than declaring the hypothesis proven solely by proximity to the substrate.

Different structural methods respond to different physical properties. X-ray crystallography is especially valuable when a sufficiently ordered crystal is obtainable and can provide detailed average electron density. Solution NMR uses magnetic-resonance measurements and can provide constraints on structures, motions and interactions under suitable solution conditions. Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy uses images of many particles in different orientations to reconstruct a three-dimensional map without a conventional crystal. The next node develops these comparisons more fully. Method choice depends on the molecular system, the biological question and the expected uncertainty, not on a universal ranking of technologies.

For examinations, work backward from the observable. A changed reflection intensity indicates a change in scattering contributions or measurement conditions; it does not automatically demonstrate a conformational transition. A missing density feature can reflect motion or low occupancy rather than chemical deletion. A good refined coordinate fit supports a model under specified assumptions, but does not uniquely establish every proposed cellular function. These distinctions allow structural data to be combined with biochemical evidence without confusing observation, reconstruction, model and mechanism.
